What Happens When Your Commercial Loan Matures in 2026?

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team · February 1, 2026 · Last Updated: March 2026
The Short Version

The 2026 commercial loan maturity wall refers to the $875 billion in commercial and multifamily mortgage debt maturing this year. Most loans were originated at low rates and peak valuations, making standard refinancing difficult due to compressed values and higher rates. Borrowers have five main options: traditional refinance, bridge loan, mezzanine debt, preferred equity, or private credit. The best path depends on your asset's current DSCR, value, and timeline. What Is the Commercial Loan Maturity Wall?

The commercial loan maturity wall refers to the unprecedented volume of commercial real estate loans scheduled to mature in a compressed timeframe. In 2026, approximately $875 billion in commercial and multifamily mortgage debt is coming due, representing about 17 percent of the $5 trillion outstanding commercial mortgage universe. Most of these loans were originated when rates were near historic lows and values were at peak levels.

To understand the scale, consider the trajectory: approximately $929 billion matured in 2024, $957 billion matured in 2025, $875 billion is scheduled for 2026, and $652 billion is projected for 2027. While the wave is past its peak — 2026 represents a 9 percent decrease from 2025 — the dollar volume remains historically massive. In total, more than $4 trillion in commercial real estate loans are set to mature between 2025 and 2029, according to the Mortgage Bankers Association.

The exposure is not evenly distributed. Among lender types, depositories (banks) hold the largest share at $396 billion (21 percent of their CRE portfolio maturing), followed by CMBS, CLOs, and other asset-backed securities at $200 billion (25 percent), and credit companies and warehouse lenders at $163 billion (29 percent). Life insurance companies have $76 billion maturing (10 percent), while GSEs — Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, FHA, and Ginnie Mae — face only $39 billion (4 percent).

The pain also varies by property type. Hotels and motels face the highest proportional burden, with 30 percent of all hotel loans maturing in 2026 — the most of any property type. Industrial properties follow at 23 percent, office at 17 percent, healthcare at 15 percent, and multifamily at 13 percent. For hotel owners in particular, the combination of maturity pressure and ongoing PIP requirements creates an especially challenging environment.

Why 2026 Is Different From Past Maturity Cycles

In previous maturity cycles, borrowers could typically refinance with minimal disruption. Property values were rising, interest rates were stable or declining, and lenders were competing for business. The 2026 cycle is fundamentally different because the loans maturing today were originated in a completely different rate environment.

Between 2020 and early 2022, the federal funds rate sat near zero, and most commercial loans were originated at rates between 3 and 4.5 percent. The Federal Reserve then executed the fastest rate-hiking cycle in decades, raising the federal funds rate from 0.25 percent to 5.25–5.50 percent between March 2022 and July 2023. After three consecutive cuts in 2025, the current rate stands at 3.50–3.75 percent — still significantly above the origination environment for most maturing loans. As a result, borrowers who locked in 3–4 percent rates are now facing refinancing costs of 5.5–8.0 percent or higher, depending on loan type and risk profile.

The math is stark. When a loan originated at 3.5 percent refinances at 6.5 percent, debt service increases by roughly 50–60 percent on the same balance. For a property with net operating income (NOI) of $1 million that previously supported a DSCR of 1.50x at the original rate, the same property at the higher rate might fall to 0.95x–1.10x — below most lenders' 1.20x–1.25x minimums. Combine that rate compression with property values that have declined in many sectors (especially office), and borrowers face a double problem: a compressed appraisal produces smaller loan proceeds while higher rates create larger debt service payments. This negative leverage dynamic — where the cost of debt exceeds the property's cap rate — has made many transactions fundamentally unworkable under traditional lending structures.

What Happens If You Cannot Refinance at Maturity?

If your commercial loan matures and you cannot refinance the full balance, several consequences can unfold — none of them ideal, but some more manageable than others.

Maturity default. A maturity default occurs when the borrower cannot repay or refinance the loan balance at maturity. Unlike a payment default, the borrower may have been making every payment on time — but they simply cannot satisfy the full balance when it comes due. Maturity defaults can trigger the lender's right to accelerate the loan, charge default interest, and ultimately foreclose.

Loan extension negotiations. In many cases, lenders prefer to negotiate rather than foreclose. This has led to what the industry calls "extend and pretend" — temporary loan extensions, often 6–12 months, that give borrowers additional time to find refinancing or improve their property's financial profile. Extensions typically come with fees, higher rates, and requirements for additional reserves or equity.

Distressed property sale. Some borrowers conclude that selling the property is the best path — particularly if the asset requires significant capital expenditure or faces structural demand challenges (as with certain office properties). Distressed sales typically result in lower prices than market-rate transactions, as buyers price in the urgency and risk.

Equity injection. Lenders may require borrowers to bring additional equity to the table to reduce the loan-to-value ratio or improve the DSCR to acceptable levels. This can mean writing a check for hundreds of thousands or millions of dollars — capital that many borrowers did not budget for at origination.

Lender workouts. In the most challenging situations, borrowers and lenders may negotiate a formal workout that restructures the loan terms — potentially modifying the rate, extending the term, or accepting a discounted payoff. Workouts are complex and typically require experienced legal counsel.

Your 5 Options When a Commercial Loan Matures

Not every maturing loan is a crisis. Many borrowers have viable options — the key is understanding which one fits your specific situation and timeline. Here are the five primary paths available to borrowers facing a 2026 maturity.

1. Traditional Refinance

If your property has maintained its value, your DSCR remains above 1.20x–1.25x at current rates, and your financial profile is strong, a traditional refinance through a bank, CMBS lender, or life insurance company may be the most straightforward option. Current conventional bank rates range from approximately 4.93–8.75 percent, while CMBS conduit rates run 5.63–7.56 percent. This is the lowest-cost option but requires the strongest fundamentals.

2. Bridge Loan

If your property needs time — whether for lease-up, renovation, or value stabilization — a bridge loan provides short-term financing (typically 12–36 months) while you execute your business plan. Bridge rates currently range from 8 to 14.5 percent, with most transitional CRE projects pricing in the 10.5–12.5 percent range. Bridge loans are interest-only, which can help with cash flow during the transition period. High-quality borrowers with strong collateral may access rates in the high-7 to 8 percent range.

3. Mezzanine Debt

If your senior loan refinance leaves a gap between the proceeds you can get and the balance you need to pay off, mezzanine debt can fill that gap. Mezzanine sits behind the senior loan in the capital stack and is secured by the borrower's equity interest rather than the property itself. DSCR requirements on a blended basis are typically 1.00x–1.05x, with the senior lender still requiring 1.25x or above on their portion.

4. Preferred Equity

Similar to mezzanine debt in function but structured as an equity investment rather than a loan, preferred equity provides capital without adding to your debt stack. This can be particularly useful when lenders impose maximum leverage limits. Preferred equity investors receive a priority return before common equity holders but sit behind all debt in the capital structure.

5. Private Credit

Non-bank lending has experienced explosive growth in recent years. The global private credit market now exceeds $1.5 trillion in assets under management, and alternative lenders claimed 40 percent of non-agency CRE loan volume in Q4 2025 — up from 23 percent the previous year. Debt funds alone saw 112 percent year-over-year growth in lending volume. Private credit providers offer faster execution, more flexible underwriting, and creative structuring that traditional banks often cannot match. For borrowers with complex situations — low DSCR, transitional assets, or unconventional property types — private credit may be the most viable path.

How to Choose the Right Path for Your Situation

The right refinancing strategy depends on several factors that are specific to your property and financial position. Here is a framework for evaluating your options:

Current DSCR. If your property generates a DSCR of 1.25x or higher at today's rates, you likely qualify for a traditional refinance. Between 1.00x and 1.24x, bridge financing or a combination of senior debt with mezzanine may be appropriate. Below 1.00x, you are likely looking at a bridge loan with an execution plan, preferred equity to supplement your capital stack, or a property sale.

Timeline to stabilization. If your property is already stabilized, focus on permanent financing. If you need 12–24 months to complete renovations, fill vacancies, or execute a PIP, a bridge loan is typically the right tool. If stabilization is more than 24 months away, you may need a longer-term bridge or a capital partner who can be patient.

Remaining loan balance vs. current value. If your current loan balance exceeds 75–80 percent of today's appraised value, traditional refinancing will likely produce a shortfall. You will need to either bring additional equity, use subordinate financing (mezzanine or preferred equity), or negotiate with your current lender.

Property condition and capital needs. If your property requires significant capital expenditure — whether for deferred maintenance, a brand-required PIP, or market-competitive upgrades — factor those costs into your analysis. Some bridge lenders will include renovation budgets in their loan structures, while traditional lenders typically will not.

Your 90-Day Action Plan

If your commercial loan matures within the next 12 months, start taking action now. Here is a step-by-step plan:

  1. Assess your current DSCR and debt yield. Pull your trailing 12-month financials and calculate your net operating income. Then divide by your annual debt service to determine your current DSCR. If you do not know your debt yield, divide NOI by your outstanding loan balance. These two metrics will immediately tell you where you stand relative to lender requirements.
  2. Get a current market appraisal. Your 2021 or 2022 appraisal is likely outdated. Understanding your property's current value — and the gap between that value and your loan balance — is critical for determining which refinancing strategies are viable.
  3. Consult a commercial loan specialist. A specialist who understands the current market, the available capital sources, and the specific requirements of each lender type can save you months of wasted effort. This is particularly true in 2026, when lending conditions vary dramatically by property type, geography, and deal size.
  4. Identify whether bridge or permanent financing fits. Based on your DSCR, property condition, and business plan, determine whether you need short-term (bridge) or long-term (permanent) financing. Many borrowers in 2026 need a bridge to stabilize before they can access permanent debt.
  5. Begin lender outreach — earlier is always better. The closer you get to maturity, the fewer options you have and the less leverage you hold in negotiations. Starting early gives you time to shop multiple lenders, negotiate terms, and close without being forced into a distressed decision.
