How Defeasance Works

Defeasance is a mechanism that allows commercial real estate borrowers to release their property from a mortgage lien without paying off the loan early. It is most commonly associated with CMBS (commercial mortgage-backed securities) loans, where traditional prepayment is either prohibited or heavily restricted.

The process works by substituting the collateral securing the loan. Instead of the property backing the mortgage, the borrower purchases a portfolio of U.S. government securities (typically Treasury bonds and STRIPS) that produce cash flows matching the remaining scheduled loan payments. These securities are placed in a successor borrower entity, and the original property is released from the lien. The loan itself continues to exist and is serviced by the securities until maturity.

Because the government securities carry the full faith and credit of the United States, investors in the CMBS trust receive the same payment stream they would have received from the original loan. This is why defeasance is permitted under CMBS loan documents: the investor is made whole.

Understanding the Cost Components

Defeasance costs are made up of several components. The largest, by far, is the securities portfolio.

Securities Cost = Present Value of Remaining Payments (at Treasury Rate) minus Loan Balance

The key driver of defeasance cost is the spread between your loan interest rate and current Treasury yields for matching maturities. When Treasury rates are significantly below your loan rate, the securities portfolio costs more because lower-yielding Treasuries need a larger principal amount to generate the same cash flows as your higher-rate loan payments. Conversely, when Treasury rates are close to or above your loan rate, the securities cost drops dramatically.

  • Securities Portfolio: This is the cost to purchase U.S. Treasury securities whose cash flows match your remaining loan payments. This is typically the largest line item, ranging from 1% to 15% (or more) of the loan balance depending on the rate environment.
  • Legal Fees: A law firm must review the loan documents, prepare the successor borrower entity, and coordinate the closing. Legal fees typically range from $25,000 to $50,000.
  • Defeasance Consultant Fee: A specialized consultant structures the securities portfolio, coordinates with the servicer, and manages the process. Fees range from $10,000 to $25,000.
  • Servicer and Rating Agency Fees: The loan servicer charges a processing fee (typically $5,000 to $15,000), and in some cases, rating agency confirmation may be required.
  • Accountant Fees: An independent accountant may be required to verify the cash flow matching. This typically costs $2,000 to $5,000.

For this calculator, we group the non-securities costs into an estimated legal and processing fee range of $25,000 to $75,000. The exact amount depends on the complexity of your loan and the number of parties involved.

When Defeasance Makes Sense

Defeasance is a significant expense, so it only makes financial sense in certain situations. The most common scenarios include:

  • Selling the property at a large profit: If you have a buyer willing to pay a price that more than covers the defeasance cost, the transaction can still be highly profitable.
  • Refinancing to a much lower rate: If interest rates have dropped substantially since origination, the savings from a new lower-rate loan may justify the upfront defeasance expense over the remaining term.
  • Portfolio repositioning: Institutional investors may need to sell properties to rebalance their holdings, and defeasance is simply a cost of doing business.
  • Treasury rates near or above the loan rate: When this happens, defeasance becomes much cheaper because the securities portfolio costs less. This creates windows of opportunity for borrowers who have been waiting to sell or refinance.

Defeasance vs. Yield Maintenance: A Comparison

Both defeasance and yield maintenance serve the same purpose: they compensate the lender (or investors) for the loss of future interest income when a borrower wants to exit a loan early. However, they work very differently in practice.

Feature Defeasance Yield Maintenance
How It Works Purchase securities to replace collateral Pay a cash penalty to the lender
Loan Status After Loan continues (serviced by securities) Loan is fully retired
Typical Loan Types CMBS, some life company loans Bank loans, life company loans, agency loans
Typical Cost Higher (securities + legal + processing) Lower (cash payment only)
Timeline 30 to 90 days Typically faster (days to weeks)
Complexity High (multiple parties, legal entities) Lower (calculation and payment)

If your loan gives you the choice between defeasance and yield maintenance, yield maintenance is almost always the less expensive option. However, most CMBS loans require defeasance because the loan cannot simply be paid off early; it must continue performing for the benefit of the bondholders in the securitization trust.

Frequently Asked Questions

Defeasance is a process that allows a borrower to release a property from a commercial mortgage lien (most commonly a CMBS loan) without paying off the loan. Instead of repaying the lender directly, the borrower purchases a portfolio of U.S. Treasury securities that generates cash flows matching the remaining loan payments. These securities are placed in a trust, and the property is released from the mortgage. The loan continues to be serviced by the treasury portfolio until it matures.

Defeasance costs vary depending on your loan balance, interest rate, remaining term, and current Treasury rates. The largest cost component is the securities portfolio, which can range from 1% to 15% or more of the loan balance. When Treasury rates are well below your loan rate, the cost is higher because lower-yielding securities require a larger purchase to match your loan payments. Legal and processing fees typically add $25,000 to $75,000 to the total. A defeasance consultant can provide an exact quote based on current market conditions.

Both defeasance and yield maintenance are prepayment mechanisms for commercial loans, but they work differently. Yield maintenance is a prepayment penalty calculated as the present value of the difference between the loan rate and a reference Treasury rate, applied to the remaining payments. Defeasance involves purchasing actual Treasury securities to replace the loan collateral. Yield maintenance results in a cash payment to the lender and the loan is retired. Defeasance keeps the loan in place but releases the property. In many cases, defeasance costs more than yield maintenance, but it is the only option for most CMBS loans.

Defeasance makes sense when the financial benefit of selling or refinancing the property exceeds the total defeasance cost. Common scenarios include selling a property at a significant profit, refinancing into a much lower rate (when rates have dropped substantially since origination), or repositioning a portfolio. Defeasance is generally more cost-effective when Treasury rates are close to or above your loan interest rate, because the securities portfolio costs less. When Treasury rates are far below your loan rate, defeasance becomes very expensive.

Not all commercial loans allow defeasance. Defeasance is most commonly required by CMBS (conduit) loans and some life insurance company loans. Bank loans and credit union loans typically use prepayment penalties or yield maintenance instead. The defeasance provision must be written into the original loan documents. Most CMBS loans have a lockout period (usually 2 years) during which neither prepayment nor defeasance is permitted, followed by a period when defeasance is allowed, and sometimes a short open prepayment window near maturity.
