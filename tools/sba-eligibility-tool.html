    Understanding SBA Loan Programs

    The U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) offers government-backed loan programs designed to help small businesses access affordable financing. The two most popular programs are the 7(a) Loan Program and the 504 Loan Program. While both are backed by the SBA, they serve different purposes, have different structures, and work best in different situations.

    Choosing the right program can save you tens of thousands of dollars over the life of your loan. The wrong choice can mean higher rates, unnecessary restrictions, or a longer approval timeline. This tool helps you quickly narrow down which program fits your business based on six key factors that lenders evaluate.

    SBA 7(a) Loan Program Overview

    The 7(a) program is the SBA's most popular and most flexible loan option. It can be used for nearly any legitimate business purpose: purchasing real estate, buying equipment, funding working capital, refinancing existing debt, or acquiring another business. The maximum loan amount is $5 million, with terms up to 25 years for real estate and up to 10 years for working capital or equipment.

    Interest rates on 7(a) loans are variable and tied to the prime rate, though some lenders offer fixed-rate options. The SBA guarantees 75% to 85% of the loan (depending on the amount), which reduces the lender's risk and allows them to offer more favorable terms than conventional financing. Down payments typically range from 10% to 20%.

    SBA 504 Loan Program Overview

    The 504 program is specifically designed for major fixed-asset purchases, primarily commercial real estate and heavy equipment. It uses a unique three-party structure: a conventional lender provides 50% of the project cost, a Certified Development Company (CDC) provides up to 40%, and the borrower contributes at least 10% as a down payment.

    The CDC portion carries a below-market, fixed interest rate for the full term (20 or 25 years for real estate, 10 years for equipment). This makes the 504 program particularly attractive for borrowers who want long-term rate certainty. However, the 504 program cannot be used for working capital, inventory, or debt consolidation without a real estate or equipment component.

    Key Differences at a Glance

    Maximum loan amount $5 million $5.5 million (CDC portion)

    Best for Flexibility, working capital, speed Real estate, long-term fixed rates

    Who Qualifies for SBA Loans?

    Both programs share baseline eligibility requirements. Your business must be a for-profit entity operating in the United States, and it must fall within the SBA's size standards for your industry. You must have invested personal equity into the business and must demonstrate that you have sought (and been unable to obtain) financing on reasonable terms through other channels.

    Personal guarantees are required from anyone owning 20% or more of the business. Most lenders look for credit scores of 680 or higher, though some will work with lower scores if there are compensating factors such as strong cash flow, significant collateral, or industry experience. Startups can qualify for 7(a) loans but generally face higher scrutiny and larger down payment requirements. The 504 program is more difficult for startups because CDCs want to see established cash flow.
