What Is Cap Rate?

Capitalization rate (cap rate) is the most widely used metric for evaluating commercial real estate investments. It measures the relationship between a property's net operating income and its value, expressed as a percentage.

Cap Rate = Net Operating Income / Property Value x 100

For example, a property generating $250,000 in annual NOI with a purchase price of $3,500,000 has a cap rate of 7.14%. Cap rate tells you the unleveraged yield on the property: the return you would earn if you paid all cash. It does not account for financing costs, which is why investors also look at cash-on-cash return and leveraged IRR for a complete picture.

Cap rate is also used to estimate property value. If you know the NOI and the market cap rate for similar properties, you can divide NOI by the cap rate to find the implied value.

Cap Rate Compression and Expansion

Cap rates move inversely to property values. When cap rates compress (go down), property values increase. When cap rates expand (go up), property values decrease. Understanding what drives these movements is essential for timing acquisitions and dispositions.

Cap rate compression happens when: investor demand increases, interest rates fall, property fundamentals improve (lower vacancy, rising rents), or capital flows into a market.

Cap rate expansion happens when: interest rates rise, investor demand weakens, property fundamentals deteriorate, or economic uncertainty increases.

Between 2020 and 2022, cap rates compressed dramatically as low interest rates fueled strong demand. Since 2022, cap rates have expanded across most property types as interest rates rose sharply. In 2026, many markets are stabilizing, though office properties continue to see upward pressure on cap rates.

Understanding where you are in the cap rate cycle helps you make better acquisition and disposition decisions.

Using Cap Rate in Acquisitions

Cap rate is a starting point, not the final word, on whether a deal is worth pursuing. Here is how experienced investors use it.

First, compare the cap rate to the cost of debt. If you can borrow at 6.5% and the property trades at a 7.5% cap rate, there is positive leverage: the property earns more than the debt costs, meaning financing amplifies returns. If the cap rate is below the borrowing rate (negative leverage), the property earns less than the cost of debt, which compresses cash returns.

Second, compare to market cap rates for similar properties. A property trading well above market cap rates may signal hidden problems (deferred maintenance, tenant risk, environmental issues) or a genuine bargain. A property trading below market may be overpriced or reflect premium quality.

Third, look at the "going-in" cap rate versus the "exit" cap rate in your projections. If you assume cap rate compression at exit but rates stay elevated, your actual returns will fall short. Conservative underwriting assumes flat or slightly expanding cap rates at disposition.

Frequently Asked Questions

There is no single "good" cap rate because the right number depends on property type, location, tenant quality, and your investment goals. In general, cap rates between 5% and 8% are the most common range for stabilized commercial properties. Lower cap rates (4% to 6%) are typical of prime locations and institutional-quality assets where investors accept lower yields in exchange for stability and appreciation potential. Higher cap rates (8% to 10%+) are found in secondary markets, value-add properties, and higher-risk asset types. The key is to compare the cap rate to both the cost of debt and to market averages for similar properties.

Interest rates and cap rates generally move in the same direction, though not in lockstep. When interest rates rise, the cost of borrowing increases, which reduces what investors can pay for properties while maintaining positive leverage. This pushes prices down and cap rates up. When interest rates fall, cheaper debt allows investors to pay more, pushing prices up and cap rates down. The "spread" between cap rates and interest rates matters as well. Historically, commercial cap rates have traded 150 to 250 basis points above the 10-year Treasury yield. When spreads are thin, the market may be overheated. When spreads are wide, there may be buying opportunities.

Cap rate measures the unleveraged yield on a property (NOI divided by property value). Cash-on-cash return measures the leveraged yield on the equity you invest (annual pre-tax cash flow divided by total cash invested). If you buy a property all cash, the two numbers are identical. But most investors use financing, which changes the picture. With favorable leverage (when the cap rate exceeds the cost of debt), cash-on-cash return will be higher than the cap rate because the debt amplifies equity returns. With unfavorable leverage, cash-on-cash return can be lower than the cap rate or even negative.
