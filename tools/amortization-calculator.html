Understanding Commercial Loan Amortization

Amortization is the process of spreading a loan's repayment over a series of fixed periodic payments. Each payment includes two parts: a portion that goes toward principal (reducing your loan balance) and a portion that covers interest. In the early years, most of your payment covers interest. As the balance decreases, a larger share of each payment goes toward principal.

Commercial real estate loans typically use a longer amortization schedule than the actual loan term. This is one of the most important distinctions borrowers need to understand. The amortization period determines your monthly payment amount, while the loan term determines when the remaining balance comes due.

Amortization Period vs. Loan Term

In residential lending, a 30-year mortgage usually has a 30-year term: you make payments for 30 years and the loan is fully paid off. Commercial loans work differently. A commercial loan might amortize over 25 years but have a term of only 7 or 10 years.

Monthly Payment = P x [r(1+r)^n] / [(1+r)^n - 1]

Where P is the loan amount, r is the monthly interest rate, and n is the total number of amortization payments. The payment stays the same throughout the amortizing period, but the loan balance at the end of the term (the balloon payment) must be refinanced or paid off.

How Balloon Payments Work

When the loan term is shorter than the amortization period, a balloon payment is due at maturity. For example, a $1,000,000 loan with a 25-year amortization and a 10-year term will still have a substantial balance remaining after 10 years of payments. That remaining balance is the balloon payment.

Borrowers plan for balloon payments in several ways: refinancing into a new loan, selling the property, or using accumulated equity and reserves. The key is to plan well in advance, because market conditions and property performance at the time of maturity will determine your refinancing options.

Interest-Only Periods

Many commercial loans offer an interest-only period at the beginning of the loan, typically ranging from 12 to 36 months. During this period, you pay only interest with no principal reduction. This reduces your initial monthly payment and can help with cash flow during lease-up, renovation, or stabilization.

After the interest-only period ends, payments switch to full principal and interest based on the remaining amortization schedule. Keep in mind that the full original balance must now be amortized over fewer remaining periods, so your payment will be higher than if the loan had been amortizing from the start.

Frequently Asked Questions

The amortization period is the length of time used to calculate your monthly payment, typically 20 to 30 years. The loan term is when the remaining balance becomes due. In commercial lending, terms of 5, 7, or 10 years are common even with 25 or 30 year amortization schedules. When the term is shorter than the amortization period, a balloon payment is due at maturity.

A balloon payment is the remaining loan balance that comes due at the end of the loan term when the term is shorter than the amortization period. For example, a $1 million loan on a 25-year amortization with a 10-year term will still owe a large portion of the original balance after 10 years. Borrowers typically refinance or sell the property before the balloon payment is due.

During an interest-only period, you pay only the interest on the loan with no principal reduction. This results in lower monthly payments upfront, but the loan balance remains unchanged. Once the interest-only period ends, your payments increase because you must now amortize the full original balance over the remaining amortization period. Interest-only periods of 12 to 36 months are common on bridge loans and some permanent financing.

The most common amortization periods for commercial real estate loans are 25 and 30 years. SBA 504 loans can go up to 25 years for real estate. CMBS loans typically use 30-year amortization. Bank loans range from 20 to 25 years. Bridge loans are often interest-only with no amortization at all. A longer amortization period results in lower monthly payments but more total interest paid over the life of the loan.

Early payoff depends on the prepayment terms in your loan agreement. Some loans allow prepayment with a penalty (such as yield maintenance or a step-down schedule), while CMBS loans typically require defeasance. SBA and some bank loans may have prepayment penalties that expire after 3 to 5 years. Always review the prepayment provisions before signing, because the cost of early payoff can significantly affect your total cost of capital.
