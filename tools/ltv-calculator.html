What Is Loan-to-Value Ratio?

Loan-to-value ratio (LTV) is one of the most fundamental metrics in commercial real estate lending. It measures the size of the loan relative to the appraised value of the property. Lenders use LTV to assess how much equity cushion exists in a deal. If a borrower defaults, the lender needs to recover the outstanding loan balance by selling the property. A lower LTV means there is a larger buffer between the loan amount and the property's value, giving the lender greater protection against loss.

LTV = Loan Amount / Property Value x 100

For example, if you are borrowing $3,500,000 against a property appraised at $5,000,000, your LTV is 70%. This means the lender is financing 70% of the property's value and you are providing 30% in equity. The lower the LTV, the more equity the borrower has at stake, which lenders view as a sign of lower risk.

LTV is used at every stage of the lending process. It is part of the initial loan screening, the formal underwriting, and the final approval. Many lenders publish maximum LTV guidelines by property type, and most loan programs have hard caps that cannot be exceeded regardless of the borrower's financial strength or the property's cash flow.

Maximum LTV by Loan Type

Different loan programs have different maximum LTV thresholds. The maximum LTV a lender will offer depends on the loan program, the property type, the market, and the borrower's track record. The table below summarizes typical limits across the most common commercial real estate loan types. Keep in mind that these are general ranges; individual deals may fall above or below these figures depending on the specifics.

How LTV Affects Your Interest Rate

LTV has a direct impact on the interest rate you will pay. Lenders price risk into their rates, and higher LTV means more risk. A deal at 60% LTV might receive a rate 50 to 100 basis points lower than the same deal at 75% LTV. This is because the lender has more collateral protection at lower leverage. With a thicker equity cushion, the probability of loss on default drops significantly, and lenders reward that reduced risk with tighter pricing.

Some lenders use "LTV pricing tiers" where rates step up at specific thresholds (e.g., under 60%, 60% to 65%, 65% to 70%, 70% to 75%). If you are close to a tier boundary, even a small reduction in loan amount can meaningfully lower your rate. For borrowers with flexibility, putting slightly more equity into the deal can result in significant savings over the life of the loan.

Beyond the rate itself, LTV also influences other loan terms. Lower LTV deals may receive longer interest-only periods, more flexible prepayment provisions, and fewer reserve requirements. Lenders view low-leverage borrowers as less risky partners, which often translates into a smoother approval process and faster closing timelines. On the other hand, higher LTV requests may trigger additional conditions such as personal guarantees, escrow holdbacks, or supplemental collateral.

LTV vs. LTC: What Is the Difference?

LTV and LTC (loan-to-cost) are related but measure different things. LTV uses the property's current appraised value as the denominator. LTC uses the total project cost (purchase price plus renovation or construction costs) as the denominator.

For stabilized acquisitions with no renovation planned, LTV and LTC are often similar. But for value-add deals or ground-up construction, LTC is typically the more relevant metric. Lenders use LTC to ensure the borrower has enough equity relative to total project costs, not just relative to the as-is property value.

A property purchased for $3M with $2M in planned renovations has a total cost of $5M. If the loan is $3.5M, the LTC is 70% even though the LTV (based on as-is value of $3M) would be 117%. Understanding which metric your lender prioritizes helps you structure the right capital stack.

For stabilized property acquisitions, focus on LTV. For construction, heavy renovation, or repositioning projects, expect lenders to underwrite to both LTV (often based on the projected stabilized value) and LTC. In many value-add scenarios, lenders will size the loan using the more conservative of the two metrics, so it pays to run both calculations before approaching a lender.

Frequently Asked Questions

Most commercial lenders are comfortable with LTVs between 65% and 75%. An LTV under 65% is considered conservative and will typically earn you the best rates and terms. At 75% to 80%, you will still find lenders, but options narrow and rates increase. Above 80%, conventional commercial lending becomes difficult, though SBA programs can go up to 90% for qualifying owner-occupied properties. The right LTV depends on your financing goals: lower leverage means lower payments and better terms, while higher leverage preserves cash for other investments.

Maximum LTV varies significantly by loan type. Conventional bank loans typically cap at 70% to 75%. CMBS loans range from 65% to 75% depending on property type. SBA 504 loans can reach 90% for owner-occupied properties. Bridge lenders may go to 75% to 80% of as-is value or even higher based on after-renovation value. The maximum LTV a specific lender will offer also depends on property type, market location, tenant quality, and the borrower's financial strength. Multifamily properties generally receive the highest LTV limits, while special-purpose and hospitality properties receive the lowest.

You can reduce LTV by either increasing equity or increasing property value. Increasing equity means putting more cash into the deal. Increasing property value can be achieved through property improvements, lease-up to stabilized occupancy, or obtaining a higher appraisal. Some borrowers also use mezzanine debt or preferred equity to bridge the gap between the senior loan and their available cash, which keeps the senior LTV low while still allowing higher total leverage. Another approach is negotiating a lower purchase price, which simultaneously increases your equity percentage and may result in a lower appraised value baseline.

Both metrics are critical, and lenders will size the loan to whichever produces the lower amount. LTV protects against collateral value decline, while DSCR protects against cash flow shortfalls. In practice, the binding constraint depends on the deal. For properties with strong cash flow but modest values, DSCR may allow a larger loan than LTV. For properties with high values but thin margins, LTV may be more generous than DSCR. Most commercial loans must satisfy minimum thresholds for both metrics, and some lenders also apply a debt yield test. The loan is sized to the most conservative result.
