What Is an Interest Reserve?

An interest reserve is a portion of a construction loan set aside at closing to cover the borrower's interest payments during the building period. Construction loans work differently from permanent financing because funds are disbursed gradually as the project progresses rather than all at once. Since the borrower typically has no rental income during construction, the lender creates an interest reserve to ensure that interest payments can be made on time without requiring the borrower to come out of pocket each month.

Think of the interest reserve as a dedicated escrow account. Each month, as the outstanding loan balance grows with new draws, the lender debits the interest reserve for the interest due on the amount currently outstanding. The reserve shrinks over time while the loan balance grows, and both processes continue until construction is complete and the loan either converts to permanent financing or is paid off.

Why Construction Lenders Require Interest Reserves

Lenders require interest reserves for several practical reasons. First, construction projects produce no income. Unlike an acquisition loan on an operating property where rental income covers debt service, a building under construction generates zero cash flow. Without a reserve, the borrower would need to make increasing interest payments each month from personal funds, creating a significant financial strain and repayment risk for the lender.

Second, interest reserves protect the project timeline. If a borrower cannot make an interest payment mid-construction, the loan goes into default, potentially halting the project. A properly sized reserve eliminates this risk by ensuring that interest is always covered through the expected construction period.

Third, regulators and bank examiners expect to see interest reserves on construction loans. For banks and credit unions, carrying construction loans without funded reserves can trigger regulatory scrutiny. This is one reason why interest reserves are standard on virtually all construction lending.

How the Interest Reserve Affects Your Available Proceeds

One of the most important things borrowers need to understand is that the interest reserve comes out of your total loan amount. If a lender approves a $5 million construction loan and the interest reserve is $400,000, only $4.6 million is available for actual construction costs (land, hard costs, and soft costs).

Available Proceeds = Total Loan Amount - Interest Reserve

This has direct implications for project feasibility. When budgeting a construction project, you must account for the interest reserve as a cost that reduces your available proceeds. If your hard and soft costs total $4.8 million and the loan is $5 million, a $400,000 interest reserve means you are $200,000 short and will need additional equity or a larger loan.

To estimate the interest reserve before approaching a lender, use the following rule of thumb: take 50% to 65% of the total loan amount (representing the average outstanding balance), multiply by the annual interest rate, and multiply by the construction period in years. This gives you a rough starting figure.

Planning for Construction Delays

Construction projects frequently run behind schedule. Weather delays, permitting issues, supply chain disruptions, and labor shortages can all push the timeline beyond original estimates. Most lenders build a contingency buffer of 5% to 15% into the interest reserve to account for this possibility. If the reserve is exhausted before completion, borrowers will typically need to fund interest payments from their own capital, which can create serious cash flow problems.

When planning your construction budget, it is wise to model the interest reserve using a timeline that is at least 10% to 20% longer than your best-case construction estimate. This ensures you have enough reserve even if the project encounters typical delays. Discuss extension options with your lender at origination so you understand the costs and process if more time is needed.
