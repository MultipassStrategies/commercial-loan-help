What Is Debt Yield?

Debt yield is a risk metric used by commercial real estate lenders to evaluate how quickly they could recover their investment if a loan defaults. Unlike DSCR (which depends on interest rate and amortization) or LTV (which depends on appraised value), debt yield looks only at the relationship between property income and loan amount. This makes it a "rate-neutral" and "value-neutral" measure of risk.

Debt Yield = Net Operating Income / Loan Amount x 100

For example, if a property generates $500,000 in annual NOI and the loan request is $5,000,000, the debt yield is 10%. This means the lender would recover 10% of their principal each year from property income alone. Higher debt yields mean lower risk for the lender.

Why Lenders Use Debt Yield

Lenders began emphasizing debt yield after the 2008 financial crisis. During the downturn, many loans that had strong DSCRs (because rates were low) and strong LTVs (because values were inflated) still defaulted because the underlying property income could not support the debt. Debt yield strips away those variables. It does not depend on interest rates, amortization schedules, or subjective property valuations.

For CMBS lenders in particular, debt yield has become a primary sizing metric. If rates change or values shift, debt yield remains the same as long as NOI is stable. This makes it especially useful in volatile rate environments like 2025 and 2026.

Debt Yield vs. DSCR vs. LTV

These three metrics work together in lender underwriting, but each measures something different.

LTV (Loan-to-Value) measures the loan relative to property value. It protects the lender against loss of collateral value. A lower LTV means more equity cushion.

DSCR (Debt Service Coverage Ratio) measures income relative to debt payments. It protects against cash flow shortfalls during the loan term. A higher DSCR means more income cushion.

Debt Yield measures income relative to the loan amount itself. It protects against loss severity in default. A higher debt yield means faster principal recovery.

Most lenders require all three metrics to meet minimum thresholds before approving a loan. The loan amount is typically sized to the most conservative result among all three tests. For example, if LTV allows $6M, DSCR allows $5.5M, and debt yield allows $5M, the loan will be sized at $5M.

Frequently Asked Questions

Most CMBS lenders require a minimum debt yield between 8% and 10%, depending on the property type and market. A debt yield above 10% is considered strong and will generally satisfy most lender requirements. For properties in primary markets with strong credit tenants, some lenders may accept debt yields as low as 7.5%. For secondary or tertiary markets, lenders often require 10% or higher. The higher the debt yield, the lower the perceived risk.

Debt yield directly constrains how much you can borrow. If a lender requires a 10% minimum debt yield and your property generates $500,000 in NOI, the maximum loan amount is $500,000 divided by 0.10, which equals $5,000,000. If the lender requires 8%, the maximum loan increases to $6,250,000. This is why improving NOI (through higher rents, lower vacancy, or reduced expenses) directly increases your borrowing capacity.

CMBS (Commercial Mortgage-Backed Securities) loans are packaged and sold to bond investors. These investors want a metric that does not change based on interest rates or property appraisals, both of which can fluctuate. Debt yield provides that stability because it depends only on NOI and loan amount. After the 2008 crisis, CMBS investors demanded this metric as a safeguard against the same type of over-leveraging that caused widespread defaults. Today, it is one of the first metrics CMBS underwriters evaluate.
