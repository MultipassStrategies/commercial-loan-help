Partner Buyout Financing: How to Buy Out a Business or Real Estate Partner

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Financing programs: SBA 7(a), cash-out refi, bank term
  • SBA maximum: $5 million
  • Valuation: Required (business and/or property)
  • Down payment: 10% to 25%
  • Timeline: 30 to 90 days
  • Key document: Buy-sell agreement

What Is a Partner Buyout?

A partner buyout occurs when one partner (or group of partners) purchases the ownership interest of another partner in a business, a piece of commercial real estate, or both. This transaction transfers full control to the remaining owner while compensating the departing partner for their equity stake. Partner buyouts are among the most common ownership transitions in small and mid-sized businesses, and they arise for a wide range of reasons: retirement, strategic disagreements, health issues, divorce, or simply a desire to move on to new ventures.

The financial mechanics of a partner buyout are straightforward in concept but often complex in execution. The buying partner must determine the fair market value of the departing partner's share, secure enough capital to fund the purchase, and restructure the business's legal and financial arrangements to reflect the new ownership. Because most business owners do not have millions of dollars in liquid capital sitting idle, financing is almost always required. The type of financing that works best depends on the nature of the partnership (business only, real estate only, or both), the size of the buyout, the financial health of the business, and the terms both parties can agree upon.

Structuring a partner buyout correctly is critical. A poorly planned buyout can drain the business of working capital, create tax liabilities that erode the deal's value, or trigger legal disputes that delay or derail the transaction entirely. This guide walks through the financing options, valuation methods, legal considerations, and step-by-step process for completing a successful partner buyout.

Types of Partner Buyouts

Partner buyouts fall into three general categories, and the financing approach differs for each.

Business Partnership Buyout

In a business partnership buyout, the purchasing partner acquires the departing partner's equity interest in the operating company. This is common in professional practices (law firms, medical groups, accounting firms), retail businesses, restaurants, and service companies. The buyout price is based on a valuation of the entire business, including tangible assets (equipment, inventory, accounts receivable) and intangible assets (goodwill, brand value, customer relationships, intellectual property). The SBA 7(a) loan program is the most frequently used financing tool for this type of buyout because it treats partner buyouts as a change of ownership, which is an eligible use of funds.

Real Estate Partnership Buyout

When partners co-own commercial real estate (whether through an LLC, limited partnership, or tenancy-in-common arrangement), a buyout involves one partner purchasing the other's ownership share of the property. The valuation is based on a commercial appraisal of the real estate, and the buyout price is the departing partner's proportional share of the property's equity (fair market value minus outstanding debt). Cash-out refinancing is the most common financing tool here, because the remaining partner can refinance the existing mortgage into a new loan large enough to pay out the departing partner's equity. Conventional bank loans, CMBS loans, and bridge loans may also be appropriate depending on the property type and loan-to-value ratio.

Combined Business and Real Estate Buyout

Many partnerships involve both an operating business and the real estate it occupies. For example, two partners may jointly own a restaurant and the building it operates from. In these situations, the buyout must address both the business valuation and the property valuation. Financing often involves a combination of products: an SBA 7(a) loan to cover the business equity buyout and a separate cash-out refinance or new commercial mortgage for the real estate component. Coordinating these two financing streams requires careful planning to ensure the total debt service remains manageable and both transactions close on a compatible timeline.

SBA 7(a) Loans for Partner Buyouts

The SBA 7(a) loan program is the single most popular financing tool for buying out a business partner. The SBA explicitly allows 7(a) funds to be used for changes of ownership, which includes purchasing a partner's equity stake in an existing business. This makes the 7(a) program a natural fit for partner buyouts involving operating companies.

Under the 7(a) program, you can borrow up to $5 million to fund the buyout. The loan can cover the purchase price of the partner's equity interest as well as working capital to sustain operations during the transition period. Interest rates on 7(a) loans are typically tied to the Wall Street Journal prime rate plus a spread of 1.75% to 2.75%, depending on the loan size and term. Repayment terms for business acquisitions and partner buyouts are generally capped at 10 years, though loans with a real estate component may qualify for up to 25-year terms.

To qualify for an SBA 7(a) partner buyout loan, you must meet several requirements. The business must be a for-profit enterprise operating in the United States and must fall within the SBA's size standards for its industry. The buying partner must demonstrate relevant management experience and the ability to successfully operate the business after the departing partner leaves. The transaction must occur at fair market value, which means a professional business appraisal is required. All owners with 20% or more equity must provide personal guarantees, and the SBA will require a thorough review of business and personal tax returns, financial statements, and a detailed plan for how the business will operate post-buyout.

One important consideration with SBA 7(a) buyout financing is the treatment of seller notes. If the departing partner is carrying a note for part of the purchase price, the SBA has specific rules about how that note must be structured. In most cases, the seller note must be placed on full standby (meaning no payments of principal or interest are due) for at least the first two years of the SBA loan, or for the life of the SBA loan if the note represents the equity injection. Your SBA lender will provide guidance on structuring the seller note in a way that complies with all program rules.

Cash-Out Refinancing for Real Estate Buyouts

When the partnership primarily involves commercial real estate, cash-out refinancing is often the most efficient way to fund the buyout. In a cash-out refi, the remaining partner refinances the existing mortgage on the property into a new, larger loan. The difference between the new loan amount and the payoff of the old mortgage is disbursed as cash, which the buying partner uses to pay the departing partner for their share of the property's equity.

For example, suppose two partners co-own a commercial building worth $2 million with an existing mortgage balance of $800,000. Each partner owns 50%. The departing partner's equity share is $600,000 (half of the $1.2 million in equity). The remaining partner could refinance the property into a new $1.4 million loan (70% loan-to-value), pay off the existing $800,000 mortgage, and use the remaining $600,000 to buy out the departing partner.

The feasibility of this approach depends on two factors: the property's current appraised value and the lender's maximum loan-to-value ratio. Most conventional lenders cap cash-out refinances at 70% to 75% LTV for commercial properties. If the departing partner's equity share exceeds the available cash-out proceeds, the buying partner will need to cover the difference with personal funds, seller financing, or a secondary loan.

Cash-out refinance terms vary by lender and property type. Interest rates may be fixed or variable, and terms typically range from 5 to 25 years depending on the loan program. Properties with strong cash flow, stable tenancy, and solid physical condition will qualify for the most favorable terms. The buying partner should expect to provide two to three years of property operating statements, rent rolls, a current appraisal, and personal financial documentation as part of the application.

Valuation Methods and Requirements

Establishing the fair market value of the departing partner's interest is the foundation of every buyout transaction. Both parties (and any lender involved) need to agree on a defensible valuation. The method used depends on whether the buyout involves a business, real estate, or both.

Business Valuation Methods

  • Income approach: This method values the business based on its ability to generate future earnings. The most common technique is the discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis, which projects future cash flows and discounts them to present value using a rate that reflects the business's risk profile. Capitalization of earnings is a simpler variation that divides a single year's normalized earnings by a capitalization rate. The income approach is widely used for profitable operating businesses.
  • Market approach: This method compares the business to similar businesses that have recently sold. Appraisers use databases of private company transaction data and public company valuation multiples to derive a value based on revenue multiples, earnings multiples, or other relevant metrics. The market approach works best when there are sufficient comparable transactions in the same industry and size range.
  • Asset approach: This method calculates the business's value based on the net value of its tangible and intangible assets minus its liabilities. The asset approach is most appropriate for asset-heavy businesses (such as manufacturing companies or real estate holding entities) or businesses that are not generating significant earnings.

Real Estate Appraisal

For real estate components, a licensed commercial appraiser will prepare a formal appraisal using the sales comparison approach (recent comparable sales), the income approach (capitalized net operating income), and the cost approach (replacement cost minus depreciation). The appraised value serves as the basis for calculating the departing partner's share of property equity and for determining the loan-to-value ratio available from the lender.

Buy-Sell Agreements and Predetermined Formulas

Well-structured partnerships often include a buy-sell agreement that specifies how the buyout price will be determined. Common formulas include a fixed multiple of earnings (such as 3x EBITDA), book value with adjustments, or a requirement to obtain independent appraisals. If your partnership has a buy-sell agreement in place, it will typically govern the valuation process and can significantly simplify negotiations. If no such agreement exists, both parties should agree on a valuation methodology and an independent appraiser before starting the process.

Deal Structure Considerations

The structure of a partner buyout affects the purchase price, tax consequences, financing options, and legal complexity. Two primary structures exist, and the choice between them has significant implications.

Asset Purchase vs. Equity Purchase

In an equity purchase (also called a stock or membership interest purchase), the buying partner acquires the departing partner's ownership interest in the entity itself. The entity continues to exist with its assets, liabilities, contracts, and tax history intact. This is the simpler structure from an operational standpoint, because nothing changes except the ownership roster. However, the buying partner inherits any hidden liabilities or contingent obligations that may exist within the entity.

In an asset purchase, the buying partner (or a new entity controlled by the buying partner) purchases specific assets from the partnership rather than the ownership interest itself. This allows the buyer to select which assets and liabilities to assume and which to leave behind. Asset purchases offer better liability protection but are more complex to execute because each asset must be individually transferred, and contracts, licenses, and permits may need to be reassigned.

Installment Sales

Rather than paying the full buyout price at closing, many partner buyouts are structured as installment sales where the purchase price is paid over time. This reduces the amount of financing the buying partner needs to secure upfront and can provide tax benefits to the selling partner by spreading the capital gain over multiple years. Installment structures are especially common when the buying partner cannot qualify for enough debt to cover the entire buyout in one transaction.

Earnout Provisions

In some deals, a portion of the purchase price is contingent on the future performance of the business. These earnout provisions tie part of the seller's compensation to revenue, profit, or other metrics during a defined period after the buyout closes. Earnouts can bridge valuation gaps when the parties disagree on what the business is worth, but they also create ongoing obligations and potential disputes. Clear, measurable performance metrics and a well-drafted earnout agreement are essential if this structure is used.

Legal Considerations

Every partner buyout involves a web of legal issues that must be addressed to protect both parties and ensure a clean transfer of ownership.

  • Partnership or operating agreement review: The existing partnership agreement or LLC operating agreement is the starting point for any buyout. It may contain provisions governing buyout rights, valuation methods, non-compete obligations, and dispute resolution procedures. If the agreement is silent on buyouts, or if no formal agreement exists, both parties should engage attorneys to negotiate terms before proceeding.
  • Purchase agreement: The buyout should be documented in a formal purchase agreement that specifies the purchase price, payment terms, representations and warranties, indemnification provisions, closing conditions, and any post-closing obligations (such as transition assistance or non-compete covenants).
  • Non-compete and non-solicitation clauses: The buying partner will typically require the departing partner to agree not to compete with the business or solicit its customers and employees for a defined period and geographic area. These clauses must be reasonable in scope to be enforceable.
  • Liability allocation: The purchase agreement should clearly define which party is responsible for liabilities that existed before the buyout date (such as pending lawsuits, unpaid taxes, or warranty claims) and how post-closing liabilities will be handled.
  • Consent requirements: Depending on the business structure and its contracts, the buyout may require consent from lenders (if existing loan agreements contain change-of-ownership clauses), landlords (if the business operates from leased space), or key customers and vendors (if contracts contain assignment restrictions).

Tax Implications

The tax consequences of a partner buyout are significant for both the buyer and the seller, and they vary depending on how the deal is structured.

For the selling partner, the buyout typically generates a capital gain (or loss) equal to the difference between the purchase price received and the partner's tax basis in their ownership interest. Long-term capital gains rates apply if the partner held the interest for more than one year. However, certain portions of the gain may be recharacterized as ordinary income under Section 751 of the Internal Revenue Code if the partnership holds "hot assets" such as inventory or unrealized receivables. The selling partner should work with a tax advisor to model the tax impact before agreeing to a price.

For the buying partner, the tax treatment depends on whether the deal is structured as an equity purchase or an asset purchase. In an equity purchase, the buyer generally does not receive a step-up in the tax basis of the partnership's underlying assets unless a Section 754 election is made. A 754 election allows the buying partner to adjust the inside basis of partnership assets to reflect the purchase price paid, which can create valuable depreciation and amortization deductions. In an asset purchase, the buyer automatically receives a stepped-up basis in the acquired assets, allocated according to IRS rules.

Both parties should also consider the impact of state taxes, transfer taxes, and any sales tax obligations that may arise from the transfer of tangible business assets. Consulting with a CPA or tax attorney who specializes in partnership transactions is strongly recommended before finalizing the buyout terms.

Seller Financing as a Component

Seller financing (also known as a seller note or seller carryback) is a common and often essential component of partner buyout transactions. In this arrangement, the departing partner agrees to receive a portion of the purchase price over time rather than demanding full payment at closing. The deferred amount is documented as a promissory note from the buying partner to the selling partner, with agreed-upon interest, a payment schedule, and a maturity date.

Seller financing serves several important functions in a partner buyout. First, it bridges the gap when the buying partner cannot obtain enough bank or SBA financing to cover the full purchase price. Second, it demonstrates the selling partner's confidence in the business's ongoing viability, which reassures institutional lenders. Third, it provides tax advantages to the seller by allowing the capital gain to be recognized over multiple years through installment sale treatment under IRS rules. Fourth, it gives the buying partner more manageable cash flow obligations during the critical transition period.

Typical seller note terms in a partner buyout include interest rates of 4% to 8%, repayment periods of 3 to 10 years, and either monthly or quarterly payment schedules. The note may be secured by a lien on the business assets (subordinate to any senior lender) or it may be unsecured. If an SBA loan is part of the financing structure, the SBA's standby requirements will govern whether and when the seller note payments can begin. Both parties should have their attorneys review the note to ensure it complies with all lender requirements and adequately protects both sides.

Required Documents

A partner buyout involves substantial documentation. While the exact requirements vary by lender and deal structure, the following documents are commonly needed.

  • Buy-sell agreement: The existing agreement (if any) that governs how ownership transfers are handled.
  • Partnership or operating agreement: The document that defines each partner's rights, obligations, and ownership percentages.
  • Business valuation report: A professional appraisal of the business's fair market value, prepared by a certified business appraiser.
  • Commercial property appraisal: Required if real estate is part of the buyout, prepared by a licensed commercial appraiser.
  • Business tax returns: Three years of federal business tax returns.
  • Personal tax returns: Three years of personal tax returns for all principals with 20% or more ownership.
  • Financial statements: Year-to-date profit and loss statement, balance sheet, and interim financial statements for the business.
  • Personal financial statement: A detailed personal financial statement for the buying partner.
  • Purchase agreement: The formal agreement between buyer and seller outlining the terms of the buyout.
  • Business plan or transition plan: A document explaining how the business will operate after the departing partner exits, including management structure, key personnel, and financial projections.
  • Debt schedule: A list of all existing business debts, including balances, payment amounts, interest rates, and maturity dates.

Step-by-Step Partner Buyout Process

A successful partner buyout follows a structured process that moves from initial conversation to final closing. While every deal has unique elements, the general sequence of steps is consistent.

  1. Initial discussion and intent. The partners discuss the buyout and agree in principle that one will purchase the other's interest. This conversation should address the general timeline, the process for determining the price, and whether both parties are willing to negotiate in good faith. If a buy-sell agreement exists, both parties should review it to understand its provisions.
  2. Engage professional advisors. Both the buying and selling partner should retain their own attorney, and the buying partner should engage a CPA or tax advisor. If financing will be needed, the buying partner should begin conversations with lenders or a loan marketplace at this stage to understand what options are available and what documentation will be required.
  3. Obtain a professional valuation. An independent business valuation and/or commercial property appraisal is ordered. Both parties should agree in advance on the appraiser and the valuation methodology to minimize disputes. If the buy-sell agreement specifies a valuation formula, that formula is followed.
  4. Negotiate the buyout terms. Using the valuation as a foundation, the partners negotiate the purchase price, payment structure, seller financing terms (if any), non-compete provisions, transition support, and any other material terms. The goal is to reach agreement on all key points before engaging lenders or drafting final legal documents.
  5. Secure financing. The buying partner submits a formal loan application to the chosen lender (SBA, bank, or both). The application includes all required financial documents, the signed purchase agreement or letter of intent, and the valuation report. The lender underwrites the deal and issues a commitment letter if approved.
  6. Draft and execute legal documents. The attorneys prepare the purchase agreement, promissory note (if seller financing is involved), amended operating agreement or partnership agreement, and any other closing documents. Both parties review, negotiate, and sign all documents.
  7. Close the transaction. The lender disburses the loan proceeds, the buying partner pays the selling partner (either in full or the initial payment under an installment arrangement), and the ownership transfer is recorded. The amended operating agreement or articles of organization are filed with the appropriate state agency.
  8. Post-closing transition. The departing partner fulfills any transition obligations (such as training the remaining partner, introducing key clients, or providing consulting services for a defined period). The buying partner assumes full operational control and begins servicing the buyout debt.

The entire process typically takes 30 to 90 days from the point where both parties are committed to the buyout, though complex deals involving both a business and real estate may take longer. Working with experienced lenders and legal counsel is the single most important factor in keeping the process on track and avoiding costly delays.

Valuation disagreements are common in partner buyouts, and there are several established approaches to resolving them.
