Your Commercial Loan Is Maturing: A Complete Guide to Your Options

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Start preparation: 12 to 18 months before maturity
  • Your options: Refinance, extend, sell, or pay off
  • Primary risk: Maturity default
  • Key underwriting factors: DSCR and LTV at current values
  • 2026 maturity wall: $875B+ in CRE loans maturing
  • First step: Get a loan sizing estimate

Why Loan Maturity Matters More Than Ever in 2026

Commercial real estate loans do not work like residential mortgages. Most commercial loans carry terms of 5, 7, or 10 years, even though the amortization schedule may stretch to 25 or 30 years. When the term expires, the entire remaining principal balance comes due in a single lump sum, known as a balloon payment. If a borrower cannot refinance, extend, sell, or pay off that balance by the maturity date, the loan goes into default regardless of whether every monthly payment was made on time.

In 2026, this structural reality has become an urgent concern for an enormous segment of the commercial real estate market. Industry estimates from the Mortgage Bankers Association, Trepp, and other data providers indicate that $875 billion or more in commercial real estate loans are scheduled to mature in 2026. A significant portion of this debt was originated in 2021 and 2022, when property values were near historic highs and interest rates were at or near historic lows. Many of those borrowers now face a very different environment: higher interest rates, lower property valuations in some sectors, and tighter lending standards across the board.

This convergence of maturing debt and changed market conditions is what industry professionals call the "maturity wall." For borrowers with loans coming due in 2026 and 2027, understanding your options and starting your preparation early is not just good practice. It is essential to protecting your investment and avoiding the serious consequences of a maturity default.

Your Four Options When a Commercial Loan Matures

Every borrower facing a loan maturity has four fundamental choices. The right one depends on the property's current financial performance, the borrower's goals, the amount of equity in the deal, and broader market conditions. Here is what each option involves and when it makes the most sense.

Option 1: Refinance Into a New Loan

Refinancing means replacing the maturing loan with a new loan from either the same lender or a different one. This is the most common path for borrowers who intend to hold the property long term. A successful refinance pays off the old loan balance in full and establishes new terms (rate, amortization, and term) for the replacement debt.

Refinancing works best when the property is performing well, with occupancy and net operating income (NOI) strong enough to support a new loan at current interest rates. Lenders will underwrite the refinance based on today's property value and today's rates, not the original loan terms. This means a borrower who took out a loan at 3.5% in 2021 may now face a refinance rate of 6.5% to 8% or higher, depending on the property type and lending source. The higher debt service must be supportable by current cash flow, which is the central challenge of the 2026 maturity wall.

The best candidates for refinancing include stabilized properties with strong occupancy (90%+ for most property types), properties where NOI has grown or remained stable since the original loan was originated, and borrowers with strong credit and a clean payment history. If your DSCR at current rates falls below 1.20x to 1.25x, you may need to bring in additional equity, negotiate a lower loan amount, or explore alternative lending sources such as debt funds or bridge lenders.

Option 2: Negotiate a Loan Extension or Modification

If a refinance is not immediately viable, the next option is to negotiate an extension with your existing lender. An extension pushes the maturity date out by one to three years, giving the borrower additional time to stabilize the property, improve cash flow, or wait for more favorable market conditions.

Many commercial loan agreements include built-in extension options. These are typically available in one-year increments and require the borrower to meet specific conditions: being current on payments, maintaining a minimum DSCR, and sometimes paying an extension fee or posting additional cash reserves. If your loan does not include a built-in extension option, you can still request a negotiated modification. Lenders often prefer a performing extension over the cost and uncertainty of foreclosure, especially when the borrower is cooperating and has a credible plan for the property.

A loan modification may also involve changing the interest rate, adjusting the amortization schedule, converting a floating rate to a fixed rate, or restructuring other terms. These modifications are negotiated on a case-by-case basis and depend on the lender's assessment of the property, the borrower, and the broader market.

Option 3: Sell the Property

Selling the property before or at maturity is a clean exit strategy that eliminates the debt entirely. The sale proceeds pay off the loan balance, and any remaining equity goes to the borrower. This option makes sense when the property's value has declined to the point where refinancing would require significant additional equity, when the borrower has lost interest in holding the asset, or when a sale generates a better risk-adjusted return than continuing to hold the property through an uncertain market.

Selling under pressure, however, is rarely ideal. Buyers can sense urgency, and a forced sale often results in a lower price than a marketed disposition. If you are considering selling, starting the process 12 to 18 months before maturity gives you the best chance of achieving a fair price without the pressure of an imminent default.

Option 4: Pay Off the Loan

If the borrower has sufficient liquidity, paying off the maturing loan with cash or other available funds eliminates the debt and all associated obligations. This is the simplest option but rarely practical for most commercial real estate borrowers, given the size of typical loan balances. Payoff is most commonly used when the remaining balance is relatively small, when the borrower has sold other assets and has cash available, or when the cost of refinancing exceeds the cost of deploying capital directly.

Rate Shock: Understanding the Numbers

The term "rate shock" describes what happens when a borrower who locked in a low rate during 2020 to 2022 must refinance at today's significantly higher rates. Even a modest rate increase can have a dramatic impact on monthly debt service and overall feasibility. The following table illustrates the effect of rate changes on a $5 million loan balance.

Original Rate New Rate Monthly Payment (25-yr amort) Annual Increase Additional NOI Needed
3.5% 6.5% $24,945 to $33,760 $105,780 NOI must grow 35%+
4.0% 7.0% $26,330 to $35,340 $108,120 NOI must grow 34%+
4.5% 7.5% $27,760 to $36,960 $110,400 NOI must grow 33%+
3.5% 8.0% $24,945 to $38,590 $163,740 NOI must grow 55%+

These numbers reveal why the maturity wall is so challenging. A property that was comfortably covering its debt service at a 3.5% rate may be unable to support the same loan amount at 7% or 8%. Unless NOI has increased substantially since origination, the borrower will either need to pay down the principal (reducing the loan amount), bring in additional equity, or accept a smaller loan and cover the gap from other sources.

When Refinancing Works and When It Does Not

A refinance is likely to succeed when several conditions align. The property should have stable or growing NOI, a strong occupancy rate (generally 85% or higher for most property types, 90%+ for multifamily), and no major deferred maintenance or capital expenditure needs. The borrower should have clean financials, a solid payment history, and sufficient net worth to satisfy lender requirements. The loan-to-value ratio at the current appraised value should fall within the target range for the chosen lending source, typically 65% to 75% for conventional lenders and up to 80% for agency or HUD programs on multifamily properties.

Refinancing becomes difficult or impossible when one or more of the following conditions exist:

  • Property value has declined. If the appraised value has dropped since origination, the existing loan balance may exceed the maximum LTV that any lender will offer. This creates a "gap" that must be filled with additional equity.
  • NOI has fallen. Reduced income, whether from lower occupancy, declining rents, or rising expenses, means the property cannot support as much debt at current rates. The DSCR may fall below the minimum threshold (typically 1.20x to 1.25x) required by most lenders.
  • Credit or sponsor issues. Borrowers with recent delinquencies, bankruptcies, or legal problems will face limited refinance options and higher pricing.
  • Market conditions. Some property types (office, in particular) are facing structural challenges that make lenders reluctant to provide new financing regardless of individual property performance.
  • Lease rollover risk. If a significant portion of the property's leases expire within the next 12 to 24 months, lenders may be unwilling to underwrite at current rent levels.

The 12 to 18 Month Preparation Timeline

Starting early is the single most important factor in achieving a good outcome when your loan matures. The following timeline outlines what to do and when to do it.

18 Months Before Maturity

  • Review your existing loan documents. Identify the exact maturity date, any built-in extension options, prepayment penalties, and defeasance requirements. Note all conditions you must satisfy to exercise an extension.
  • Assess the property's current financial performance. Pull your trailing 12-month income and expense statement, calculate your current NOI, and compare it to the NOI at origination. Identify any trends that could concern a new lender.
  • Get an informal property valuation. Talk to a local broker or appraiser to understand where the market is and what your property might be worth today. This gives you an early read on your LTV position.

12 to 15 Months Before Maturity

  • Order a formal appraisal. A current appraisal tells you exactly where you stand on value. If the number is lower than expected, you have time to make improvements or explore alternatives.
  • Prepare a complete financial package. Assemble two to three years of property operating statements, rent rolls, tax returns (personal and entity), a current balance sheet, and a schedule of real estate owned. Having this package ready accelerates the lending process significantly.
  • Begin shopping lenders. Contact multiple lending sources: banks, CMBS lenders, agency lenders (for multifamily), debt funds, and life insurance companies. A commercial loan marketplace like Commercial Loan Help can present your deal to dozens of lenders simultaneously, helping you identify the best available terms.
  • Run a loan sizing analysis. Based on current rates and your property's NOI, calculate how much new debt the property can support. Compare this to your existing balance. If there is a gap, start planning how to fill it.

6 to 9 Months Before Maturity

  • Select a lender and begin the application process. Lock in terms if possible. The application, underwriting, and closing process for a commercial refinance typically takes 60 to 120 days, so starting at the 6-month mark provides a comfortable buffer.
  • Address any property issues. If the appraisal or lender feedback identified deferred maintenance, occupancy concerns, or other problems, use this time to make repairs, sign new leases, or otherwise improve the property's profile.
  • Negotiate with your existing lender if needed. If the refinance is not progressing as expected, open a dialogue with your current lender about extension or modification options. Lenders respond more favorably to proactive communication than to a borrower who waits until the last minute.

3 Months Before Maturity

  • Finalize the refinance or extension. By this point, your replacement loan should be in underwriting or approaching closing. If you have chosen to extend, the extension agreement should be documented and executed.
  • Prepare for closing costs. Budget for appraisal fees, legal costs, title insurance, environmental reports, and any lender origination fees. These can add up to 1% to 3% of the new loan amount.
  • Confirm payoff figures with your existing lender. Request a formal payoff statement that includes the exact balance, per diem interest, any prepayment or defeasance amounts, and wire instructions.

When Your Property Is Underwater

A property is considered "underwater" when its current appraised value is less than the outstanding loan balance. This situation has become more common in the post-2022 environment, particularly for office properties, properties in markets with declining fundamentals, and assets that have experienced tenant losses or deferred maintenance.

When a property is underwater, a standard refinance at the same loan amount is not possible because no lender will originate a loan that exceeds the property's value. Borrowers in this situation have several options to explore.

  • Bring in additional equity. The most straightforward solution is to inject cash to pay down the existing loan to a level that a new lender will finance. If you need $4 million to refinance but the property only supports $3.2 million in new debt, you must contribute $800,000 in additional equity. This capital can come from the borrower's own resources, a capital call to existing partners, or from new equity through a joint venture or preferred equity structure.
  • Mezzanine or preferred equity. If you cannot fund the gap from your own pocket, a mezzanine lender or preferred equity provider can fill the difference. These capital sources are more expensive (typically 10% to 18% annually), but they can bridge the gap between the senior loan amount and the existing balance. This approach works when the property has a clear path to value recovery and the borrower has a credible plan to improve performance.
  • Negotiate a discounted payoff. In some cases, lenders (particularly CMBS special servicers) may accept less than the full loan balance to resolve the debt. This is sometimes called a "note sale" or "discounted payoff." Lenders agree to this when they believe the alternative (foreclosure, prolonged workout, or REO ownership) would produce a worse economic outcome. Discounted payoffs are more common in distressed situations and require skilled negotiation.
  • Deed in lieu of foreclosure. As a last resort, the borrower can offer to transfer the property to the lender in exchange for being released from the loan obligation. This avoids the time and cost of a formal foreclosure for both parties. However, it results in a total loss of the borrower's equity and has significant tax implications, including potential cancellation of debt income.

CMBS Loan Maturities: Special Considerations

Commercial mortgage-backed securities (CMBS) loans represent a significant portion of the 2026 maturity wall, and they come with unique challenges that borrowers must understand. Unlike a bank loan where you can pick up the phone and call your relationship manager, a CMBS loan is serviced by a third-party master servicer and, in the event of a default or imminent default, transferred to a special servicer.

CMBS loans typically include a defeasance or yield maintenance requirement rather than a simple prepayment penalty. Defeasance involves purchasing a portfolio of government securities that replicates the remaining cash flows of the loan, allowing the borrower to release the collateral while keeping the bond structure intact. This process can cost hundreds of thousands of dollars and adds weeks to the closing timeline. The cost of defeasance fluctuates with interest rates and the remaining loan term.

If you are unable to refinance or defease your CMBS loan by maturity, the loan will be transferred to the special servicer. The special servicer's mandate is to maximize recovery for the bondholders, which does not always align with the borrower's interests. Special servicers charge workout fees, legal fees, and other costs that are added to the loan balance. They also have broad discretion over whether to extend, modify, or foreclose, and the process can be slow and unpredictable.

For borrowers with CMBS loans maturing in 2026, starting the refinance process 18 months out is especially important. Factor in the time and cost of defeasance, identify replacement financing early, and budget for the transactional friction that is inherent in the CMBS structure. If your loan is likely to be transferred to special servicing, consider engaging a workout advisor or attorney who specializes in CMBS matters. Their expertise can make a significant difference in the outcome.

The Bridge Loan Solution

When a conventional refinance is not available but the borrower believes the property has long-term value, a bridge loan can serve as a strategic interim solution. Bridge lenders (including debt funds, private lenders, and specialty finance companies) provide short-term financing (typically 12 to 36 months) that pays off the maturing loan and gives the borrower time to stabilize the property, complete renovations, sign new leases, or wait for more favorable permanent financing conditions.

Bridge loans for maturity situations typically offer:

  • Higher leverage than conventional lenders. Bridge lenders may go up to 70% to 80% of current value, compared to 60% to 65% from banks in a stressed market.
  • Faster execution. Bridge loans can close in 2 to 4 weeks, which is critical when maturity is imminent.
  • Flexible underwriting. Bridge lenders focus more on the property's potential and exit strategy than on trailing financials. A property with 70% occupancy that is expected to reach 90% within 18 months may qualify for a bridge loan even if it does not meet conventional DSCR requirements.
  • Interest-only payments. Most bridge loans are interest-only during the term, which reduces monthly cash outflow while the borrower works to improve the property.

The trade-off is cost. Bridge loan rates typically range from 8% to 12% or higher, with origination fees of 1 to 3 points. The borrower must also have a clear and credible exit strategy: either a refinance into permanent debt or a sale. Taking a bridge loan without a realistic plan for repayment simply delays the problem at a higher cost.

Working With Your Existing Lender

Your current lender is often your most important ally in navigating a loan maturity, provided you approach the conversation correctly. Lenders do not want to foreclose on properties if a reasonable alternative exists. Foreclosure is expensive, time-consuming, and forces the lender to manage or sell a real estate asset, which is not their core business. In the context of the 2026 maturity wall, lenders are processing an unusually high volume of maturing loans and are generally more willing to work with borrowers who are proactive, transparent, and well-prepared.

Here is how to approach your existing lender:

  1. Communicate early. Contact your lender 12 to 18 months before maturity to signal that you are aware of the upcoming date and are actively planning. This builds trust and positions you as a responsible borrower.
  2. Present updated financials. Show the lender your current property performance, including trailing 12-month financials, a current rent roll, and any capital improvement plans. Transparency about challenges (declining occupancy, rising expenses) is better than surprises at the last minute.
  3. Propose a plan. Whether you want to refinance with a new lender, extend the existing loan, or sell, present a credible timeline and action plan. Lenders respond favorably to borrowers who have thought through their options.
  4. Know your leverage. If the property is performing well and you have refinancing alternatives, you have negotiating power. If the property is struggling and you need an extension, your leverage is more limited, but the lender's desire to avoid foreclosure costs works in your favor.
  5. Get everything in writing. Any extension, modification, or workout agreement should be formally documented. Verbal assurances are not enforceable, and loan servicer personnel can change without notice.

Understanding Maturity Default

A maturity default is one of the most serious events in commercial real estate finance. It occurs when the borrower fails to repay the outstanding loan balance by the contractual maturity date. Unlike a payment default (where the borrower misses monthly installments), a maturity default can happen even when the borrower has never missed a single payment. The entire remaining principal balance is simply due, and if it is not paid, the loan is in default.

The consequences of a maturity default are significant and escalate quickly:

  • Default interest. Most loan agreements impose a default interest rate that is 3 to 5 percentage points above the contract rate. On a $5 million balance, this adds $150,000 to $250,000 per year in additional interest charges.
  • Late fees and penalties. Additional fees are assessed on top of default interest, compounding the financial burden.
  • Acceleration. The lender has the right to declare the entire loan balance immediately due and payable, eliminating any remaining payment schedule.
  • Foreclosure. The lender can initiate foreclosure proceedings to take ownership of the property. The foreclosure timeline varies by state (from a few months to over a year) and is costly for all parties.
  • Cross-default provisions. A maturity default can trigger default provisions in the borrower's other loan agreements, creating a cascading effect across the borrower's entire portfolio.
  • Recourse liability. If the loan includes a personal guarantee (full or partial recourse), the lender can pursue the borrower's personal assets to satisfy any deficiency after the property is sold.
  • Credit damage. A maturity default damages the borrower's credit profile and reputation with lenders, making future financing more difficult and expensive to obtain.

The most effective way to avoid a maturity default is to begin planning 12 to 18 months before your maturity date. Even borrowers in challenging situations can usually find a resolution (extension, bridge loan, additional equity, or negotiated sale) if they act early enough. Waiting until the maturity date is approaching or has passed dramatically reduces your options and bargaining power.

For owner-occupied commercial properties, SBA programs (particularly the 504 refinance option) can provide below-market fixed rates with up to 90% financing. For all other property types, banks, CMBS lenders, and life companies are the primary conventional options, with debt funds and bridge lenders serving as the transitional alternative when conventional financing is not immediately available.

Your Action Plan: Steps to Take Right Now

Regardless of when your loan matures, here are the steps you should take immediately to put yourself in the strongest possible position.

  1. Pull your loan documents. Confirm your exact maturity date, any extension options, prepayment or defeasance requirements, and all financial covenants. Know what your loan allows and requires.
  2. Calculate your current position. Determine your property's current NOI, your DSCR at current market rates, and your approximate LTV based on a current valuation. This tells you whether a standard refinance is feasible or whether you need to explore alternatives.
  3. Assemble your financial package. Gather two to three years of operating statements, current rent rolls, personal and entity tax returns, a net worth statement, and a schedule of real estate owned. Having this ready saves weeks when you begin the lending process.
  4. Get a loan sizing estimate. Use a commercial loan marketplace or work with a mortgage broker to get an estimate of how much new debt your property can support at current rates. Compare this to your existing loan balance to identify any gap.
  5. Start conversations with lenders. Even if maturity is 18 months away, having preliminary discussions with lenders gives you market intelligence, helps you identify potential issues early, and establishes relationships that can be activated when you are ready to move forward.
  6. Plan for the gap. If your sizing estimate shows that the new loan will be smaller than your existing balance, start planning now for how to fill the gap: additional equity, mezzanine financing, property improvements to boost NOI, or a combination of these strategies.
  7. Engage professional help. A commercial mortgage advisor can present your deal to multiple lending sources, negotiate terms on your behalf, and help you navigate the complexities of a maturity event. For CMBS borrowers, consider also engaging a workout attorney who specializes in securitized debt.

The borrowers who achieve the best outcomes in a maturity event are the ones who start early, understand their numbers, and explore every available option before time runs out. In the current environment, with hundreds of billions of dollars in loans competing for refinance capital, preparation and speed are more valuable than ever.
