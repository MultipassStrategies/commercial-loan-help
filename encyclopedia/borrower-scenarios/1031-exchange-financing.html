1031 Exchange Financing: How Commercial Loans Work in Tax-Deferred Exchanges

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Identification period: 45 days
  • Closing deadline: 180 days
  • Debt replacement rule: Equal or greater
  • Boot risk: Taxable if not matched
  • Qualified Intermediary: Required
  • Like-kind rule: Any CRE to CRE

What Is a 1031 Exchange?

A 1031 exchange, named after Section 1031 of the Internal Revenue Code, is a tax-deferral strategy that allows commercial real estate investors to sell one property and reinvest the proceeds into a replacement property while deferring capital gains taxes. Rather than paying federal capital gains taxes (which can reach 20%, plus the 3.8% net investment income tax and any applicable state taxes) at the time of sale, the investor rolls the equity into a new property, effectively postponing the tax liability until a future taxable sale occurs.

The concept has been part of the U.S. tax code since 1921, though the rules have evolved significantly over the decades. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 limited 1031 exchanges exclusively to real property, eliminating the ability to exchange personal property such as equipment, aircraft, and artwork. For commercial real estate investors, however, the program remains one of the most powerful wealth-building tools available. Any type of investment or business-use real estate can be exchanged for any other type of investment or business-use real estate. This "like-kind" requirement is broadly defined: an apartment complex can be exchanged for an office building, a retail center for raw land, or a warehouse for a hotel. The key requirement is that both the relinquished property (the one being sold) and the replacement property (the one being acquired) are held for investment or business use, not personal use.

A Qualified Intermediary (QI) must facilitate every 1031 exchange. The QI is an independent third party who holds the sale proceeds from the relinquished property and uses those funds to acquire the replacement property on the exchanger's behalf. The investor never takes constructive receipt of the funds. If the proceeds touch the investor's hands at any point, the exchange is disqualified and the full capital gains tax becomes due immediately.

How Debt Works in a 1031 Exchange

Financing adds a layer of complexity to every 1031 exchange because the IRS tracks not only the equity moving between properties but also the debt. The fundamental rule is straightforward: to achieve full tax deferral, the investor must replace both the equity and the debt from the relinquished property. This means the purchase price and the mortgage on the replacement property must each be equal to or greater than the corresponding amounts on the relinquished property.

Consider a practical example. An investor sells a property for $1,000,000 and pays off a $600,000 mortgage at closing. The net equity held by the QI is $400,000 (minus closing costs). To fully defer taxes, the investor must acquire a replacement property worth at least $1,000,000 and take on new debt of at least $600,000. If the replacement property is worth $1,200,000, the investor would use the $400,000 in exchange equity as the down payment and finance the remaining $800,000 with a new commercial loan. Because the new debt ($800,000) exceeds the old debt ($600,000), the debt replacement rule is satisfied.

The debt does not need to come from the same lender, and the loan terms can be entirely different. The investor can switch from a short-term bridge loan to a 10-year fixed-rate mortgage, move from one bank to another, or change loan structures entirely. What matters to the IRS is the dollar amount of the liability assumed, not the specific characteristics of the financing.

The Mortgage Boot Trap

Mortgage boot is one of the most common and costly mistakes in 1031 exchanges. Boot refers to any value received in an exchange that does not qualify for tax deferral. While cash boot (receiving actual cash proceeds from the sale) is the most obvious form, mortgage boot is more subtle and catches many investors off guard.

Mortgage boot occurs when the debt on the replacement property is less than the debt that was paid off on the relinquished property. The difference is treated as taxable income. Using the earlier example: if the investor paid off a $600,000 mortgage but only took on a $400,000 loan for the replacement property, the $200,000 gap is mortgage boot, and the investor owes capital gains tax on that amount.

There are several ways mortgage boot can arise unexpectedly. Sometimes a lender's appraisal comes in lower than expected, reducing the available loan amount. In other cases, the investor's financial profile may have changed since the last loan was originated, resulting in a smaller approval. Interest rate increases can also reduce borrowing capacity; if rates have risen since the original loan was placed, the investor may qualify for a smaller loan even on a more valuable property because the higher payments affect debt service coverage ratios.

The good news is that mortgage boot can be offset with additional cash. If the investor contributes personal funds to cover the gap between the old debt and the new debt, the boot is eliminated. For instance, if the new loan is $100,000 less than the old mortgage, the investor can add $100,000 in cash to the transaction (above and beyond the exchange equity) to avoid triggering a taxable event. This flexibility makes careful advance planning essential. Every exchanger should model multiple financing scenarios before the sale closes to understand exactly how much cash may be needed to prevent boot.

Timeline Challenges for Financing

The 1031 exchange timeline is strict and unforgiving. Once the relinquished property closes, two clocks start running simultaneously. The investor has exactly 45 calendar days to identify up to three potential replacement properties (or more under certain valuation rules) and exactly 180 calendar days to close on one or more of those properties. These deadlines are absolute. There are no extensions, even for weekends, holidays, or unforeseen circumstances. The only recognized exceptions are federally declared disasters affecting the exchange area.

This timeline creates significant pressure on the financing process. A typical commercial mortgage takes 45 to 90 days from application to closing, depending on the property type, the borrower's financial complexity, and the lender's pipeline. When you overlay that timeline on the 180-day exchange window, it becomes clear that financing cannot be left until the last minute. If the investor waits until day 45 to begin the loan process (after identifying the replacement property), only 135 days remain to complete underwriting, appraisal, environmental review, title work, and closing. That schedule is achievable but tight, and any delay (a low appraisal, a title defect, or a slow third-party report) can threaten the entire exchange.

Experienced exchangers begin the financing process well before the relinquished property is even listed for sale. By securing a lender pre-approval and gathering documentation in advance, the investor can move quickly once a replacement property is identified and dramatically reduce the risk of missing the 180-day deadline.

Pre-Approval Strategies for Exchangers

Pre-approval is arguably the single most important financing step in a 1031 exchange. A strong pre-approval accomplishes three goals at once: it confirms how much the investor can borrow, it signals to sellers that the buyer can perform, and it gives the lender a head start on underwriting so the loan can close faster once a property is identified.

The ideal pre-approval process for an exchange works as follows. First, the investor selects a lender or lending marketplace with experience in 1031 exchange transactions. Not every lender understands the nuances of exchange financing, and working with one that does can prevent costly missteps. Second, the investor provides standard financial documentation (tax returns, a personal financial statement, a schedule of real estate owned, and an entity organization chart if applicable) so the lender can assess creditworthiness and borrowing capacity. Third, the lender issues a pre-approval letter specifying the maximum loan amount, approximate terms, and any conditions that must be met before final commitment.

Armed with a pre-approval letter, the investor can identify replacement properties with confidence, knowing that financing is available up to a defined limit. When offers are submitted, the pre-approval letter accompanies the purchase agreement, giving the seller assurance that the buyer has the financial backing to close. In competitive markets where sellers receive multiple offers, a pre-approved buyer with exchange deadlines often stands out because the seller knows the buyer is highly motivated to close on time.

Reverse Exchanges

In a standard (forward) 1031 exchange, the investor sells the relinquished property first and then acquires the replacement. A reverse exchange flips this order: the replacement property is purchased before the relinquished property is sold. Reverse exchanges are governed by IRS Revenue Procedure 2000-37, which establishes a safe harbor for these transactions.

In a reverse exchange, an Exchange Accommodation Titleholder (EAT) temporarily takes title to either the replacement property (the "exchange last" structure) or the relinquished property (the "exchange first" structure) while the investor completes the other side of the transaction. The EAT is typically a single-purpose entity affiliated with the QI. Once the investor sells the relinquished property, the exchange is completed and the EAT transfers the replacement property to the investor.

The timelines in a reverse exchange mirror those of a forward exchange: 45 days to identify the property that will be relinquished and 180 days to complete the entire transaction. However, the financing is considerably more complex. Because the EAT holds title to the replacement property during the exchange period, the investor cannot directly obtain a mortgage on a property they do not own. Some lenders will lend directly to the EAT; others require the investor to provide a guaranty and fund the purchase through a combination of cash, a line of credit, or a short-term loan that is later replaced with permanent financing.

Reverse exchanges are more expensive than forward exchanges due to the additional legal, accounting, and holding costs associated with the EAT structure. Typical additional costs range from $5,000 to $15,000 or more, depending on the deal's complexity and how long the EAT must hold title. Despite the added cost, a reverse exchange can be invaluable when the perfect replacement property appears before the relinquished property has sold, especially in markets where desirable investment properties are scarce.

Common Mistakes Exchangers Make

Even experienced real estate investors stumble during 1031 exchanges, often because they underestimate the interaction between financing and exchange rules. Here are the most frequent errors and how to avoid them.

  • Waiting too long to engage a lender. Many investors focus on identifying properties during the first 45 days and only begin the financing process after identification. This leaves a compressed window for underwriting and closing. The fix: begin lender conversations before the relinquished property sells.
  • Ignoring the debt replacement requirement. Investors sometimes assume that reinvesting all the cash equity is sufficient for full tax deferral. It is not. The debt must also be replaced. An investor who downsizes their mortgage without adding offsetting cash will trigger mortgage boot.
  • Failing to coordinate with the QI. The Qualified Intermediary must be involved before the sale closes. If the sale closes and the proceeds go directly to the investor (even briefly), the exchange is disqualified. Lenders, title companies, and the QI must coordinate closing instructions carefully.
  • Choosing a lender unfamiliar with exchanges. Lenders who rarely handle 1031 transactions may not understand the strict deadlines, the role of the QI, or the special wiring requirements. Delays caused by lender confusion can jeopardize the entire exchange.
  • Not accounting for closing costs. Exchange proceeds used to pay closing costs on the replacement property can sometimes create unexpected boot. All closing costs, loan fees, and prepaid items should be modeled in advance.
  • Overidentifying properties. While the IRS allows identification of up to three properties (or more under the 200% or 95% rules), identifying too many can create confusion and split the investor's attention during the critical financing period. Focus is key.
  • Mixing exchange funds with personal funds. The QI must maintain strict separation of exchange proceeds. If exchange funds are commingled with the investor's personal accounts, the IRS may disqualify the entire exchange.

Lender and QI Coordination

Successful 1031 exchange financing depends on seamless coordination between three parties: the lender, the Qualified Intermediary, and the title or escrow company. Each party has a specific role at closing, and miscommunication between them is one of the most common causes of exchange failures.

The QI's primary responsibility is to receive the net sale proceeds from the relinquished property, hold them in a segregated account, and deploy them toward the purchase of the replacement property. When a loan is involved, the QI's funds and the lender's funds must arrive at the closing table through separate wires, with specific instructions about how each source is applied. The lender funds the mortgage portion, and the QI funds the equity portion (the exchange proceeds). The title company coordinates the disbursement, ensuring that all funds are applied correctly and that the closing statement accurately reflects the exchange structure.

Several coordination points require particular attention. First, the lender's closing instructions must acknowledge the 1031 exchange and permit the QI to direct the equity portion of the purchase price. Some lenders have standard exchange addenda for their closing documents; others require custom language. Second, the timing of wire transfers must be synchronized. If the QI's wire arrives a day late, the closing may be delayed, pushing dangerously close to the 180-day deadline. Third, the lender must understand that the borrower's down payment is coming from the QI's escrow account, not from the borrower's personal bank account. Lenders accustomed to sourcing and seasoning requirements for down payment funds sometimes flag QI wires as irregular. Advance communication prevents this issue.

Working with a lender who has handled 1031 exchange transactions before greatly reduces the risk of coordination failures. Ask prospective lenders directly whether they have closed exchange-financed deals and whether they have established procedures for working with QIs. A lender who answers yes to both questions will save significant time and stress during the closing process.

Financing Considerations by Property Type

The type of replacement property an investor targets affects the available financing options and the likelihood of closing within the exchange timeline. Different property types carry different risk profiles in the eyes of lenders, which influences loan terms, underwriting speed, and approval rates.

  • Multifamily (5+ units). Apartment buildings are among the easiest commercial properties to finance because they generate stable, diversified rental income. Agency lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac), banks, and CMBS lenders all compete in this space, offering competitive rates and relatively fast closings. Multifamily properties are well-suited to 1031 exchanges because the broad lender appetite makes it easier to secure financing within the 180-day window.
  • Retail. Retail properties vary widely in financing difficulty. Single-tenant net-leased properties with creditworthy tenants (pharmacies, dollar stores, fast food chains) are straightforward to finance and popular among exchangers. Multi-tenant strip centers require more underwriting scrutiny due to tenant rollover risk. Malls and large retail centers face the most challenging financing environment and may require longer underwriting timelines.
  • Office. Office properties have faced increased lender caution since the shift to remote and hybrid work patterns. Investors exchanging into office buildings should expect more conservative underwriting, lower leverage, and potentially longer approval timelines. Fully leased office buildings with long-term tenants remain financeable, but speculative or partially vacant office properties may require bridge financing or private capital.
  • Industrial and warehouse. Industrial properties are currently among the most favored asset classes for commercial lenders, driven by strong demand from e-commerce, logistics, and manufacturing tenants. Financing is broadly available, terms are competitive, and underwriting is generally efficient. This makes industrial properties an excellent target for 1031 exchangers who need certainty of execution.
  • Hospitality. Hotels and motels are considered specialty assets with higher risk profiles due to their sensitivity to economic cycles and the operational complexity of running a hospitality business. Fewer lenders participate in hospitality financing, and those that do typically require higher down payments, stronger borrower experience, and more detailed feasibility analysis. Exchangers targeting hospitality properties should start the financing process early and consider SBA or specialized hospitality lenders.
  • Land. Raw land is the most difficult commercial asset to finance because it generates no income and carries high development risk. Most banks will not lend against vacant land at leverage levels that satisfy the debt replacement requirements of a 1031 exchange. Investors exchanging into land often need to fund the purchase primarily with exchange equity and personal cash, or use seller financing to supplement a small bank loan.

Improvement Exchanges

An improvement exchange (also called a build-to-suit or construction exchange) allows an investor to use 1031 exchange proceeds not only to acquire a replacement property but also to make improvements to it before the exchange period ends. This is a powerful strategy for investors who want to upgrade, renovate, or even construct a new building on a replacement site as part of the exchange.

The mechanics of an improvement exchange involve the Exchange Accommodation Titleholder (EAT) taking title to the replacement property during the construction period. The QI directs exchange proceeds to the EAT, which uses those funds (along with any additional financing) to pay for the improvements. Once the improvements are complete, or once the 180-day exchange period expires (whichever comes first), the EAT transfers the improved property to the investor. The value of the improvements made before the transfer counts toward the exchange, helping the investor meet the price and equity requirements for full tax deferral.

Financing an improvement exchange requires a construction loan or a renovation loan in addition to the acquisition financing. The lender must be comfortable lending to (or through) the EAT structure, which not all construction lenders are willing to do. The timeline pressure is even more intense than in a standard exchange because the improvements must be substantially completed within the 180-day window. Investors considering an improvement exchange should work with a general contractor who can commit to an aggressive schedule and a lender experienced in EAT-held construction loans.

Deal Example Walkthrough

The following example illustrates how financing, exchange rules, and tax strategy come together in a real-world 1031 exchange.

The situation. An investor owns a 12-unit apartment building purchased 10 years ago for $900,000. The current mortgage balance is $520,000. The property has appreciated to a market value of $1,400,000. The investor wants to sell and exchange into a larger multifamily property to increase cash flow and defer the capital gains tax on roughly $500,000 in gains.

Step 1: Preparing for the exchange. Before listing the property, the investor engages a Qualified Intermediary and consults with a tax advisor to model the exchange. The investor also contacts a commercial lending marketplace to obtain a pre-approval for the replacement purchase. Based on the investor's financials, the lender pre-approves a loan of up to $1,100,000 at 65% loan-to-value on a stabilized multifamily property.

Step 2: Selling the relinquished property. The apartment building sells for $1,400,000. At closing, the $520,000 mortgage is paid off, and the net proceeds (approximately $830,000 after closing costs) are wired directly to the QI. The investor never touches the funds. The 45-day identification clock and the 180-day closing clock begin.

Step 3: Identifying replacement properties. Within 45 days, the investor identifies two potential replacement properties: a 24-unit apartment complex listed at $1,700,000 and a 20-unit building listed at $1,500,000. Both properties would allow the investor to reinvest all the exchange equity and take on debt equal to or greater than the $520,000 that was retired.

Step 4: Financing the replacement. The investor negotiates a purchase price of $1,650,000 on the 24-unit complex. With $830,000 in exchange equity from the QI and a new loan of $820,000 (approximately 50% LTV), the purchase is fully funded. Because the new mortgage ($820,000) exceeds the old mortgage ($520,000), the debt replacement rule is satisfied with room to spare. No mortgage boot is triggered, and no additional cash is required from the investor.

Step 5: Closing. The lender completes underwriting, orders an appraisal and environmental report, and issues a commitment letter. The QI coordinates with the title company to ensure exchange funds are wired correctly. The deal closes on day 112, well within the 180-day deadline. The investor defers the entire capital gain, acquires a larger property with stronger cash flow, and begins the holding period on the new asset.

This example highlights the importance of advance planning, lender pre-approval, and QI coordination. The exchange succeeded because the investor began the financing process before the relinquished property was even listed, selected a replacement property type (multifamily) that is easy to finance, and worked with parties experienced in exchange transactions.

This is why advance planning and loan pre-approval are so important: they give you time to arrange supplemental funds if needed.

Some investors intentionally accept partial boot when the tax cost is manageable and the alternative (taking on more debt than they are comfortable with) is unattractive. Consult your tax advisor to weigh the trade-off between accepting a limited tax bill and overleveraging the replacement property.

A bridge loan provides short-term funding (typically 6 to 24 months) to acquire the replacement property quickly, and you can then refinance into a permanent long-term loan after the exchange is complete. This two-step approach separates the urgency of the exchange deadline from the longer process of securing optimal permanent financing.

Bridge loans are especially useful when the replacement property needs renovations before it can qualify for conventional permanent financing, when the borrower's tax returns or financial documentation require extra time to package, or when the lender's pipeline is backed up and permanent loan closings are taking longer than usual. By closing the bridge loan within the 180-day window, the investor preserves the exchange, and the subsequent refinance can proceed at a more deliberate pace.

Keep in mind that bridge loans carry higher interest rates (typically 8% to 12%) and origination fees (1 to 3 points) compared to permanent financing. Factor these costs into your exchange analysis. The bridge loan amount should still meet the debt replacement requirement to avoid mortgage boot. If the bridge loan is smaller than the retired debt, you will need to contribute additional cash to cover the gap.
