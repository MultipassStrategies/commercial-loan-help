Bridge-to-Permanent Financing: How to Plan Your Exit from Day One

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Bridge term: 12 to 36 months
  • Permanent loan: Fixed rate, lower cost
  • Bridge rate: 8% to 12%+
  • Key metric: Stabilized DSCR
  • Start planning: 6 to 9 months before maturity
  • Success factor: Clear stabilization plan

What Is Bridge-to-Permanent Financing?

Bridge-to-permanent financing is a two-step capital strategy in which a borrower first uses a short-term bridge loan to acquire, reposition, or stabilize a commercial property, and then replaces that bridge loan with a long-term permanent mortgage once the property meets certain performance benchmarks. The bridge loan provides speed and flexibility during the transitional phase, while the permanent loan delivers lower rates, longer terms, and predictable debt service for the holding period ahead.

This approach is one of the most common strategies in commercial real estate for properties that are not yet ready to qualify for permanent financing. A newly acquired apartment complex with 60% occupancy, an office building undergoing a major renovation, or a retail center transitioning to new tenancy all share the same fundamental challenge: permanent lenders need to see stable income and occupancy before they will provide long-term debt. The bridge loan fills that gap, giving the borrower time and capital to execute their business plan and bring the property to stabilization.

The term "bridge" is descriptive. The loan is intended to bridge a temporary period between acquisition (or another triggering event) and the point at which the property qualifies for permanent debt. It is not designed to be held indefinitely. Every successful bridge-to-permanent strategy starts with a clear exit plan, and the best operators begin planning that exit on the same day they close the bridge loan.

Why the Two-Step Approach Exists

Permanent lenders, including agency programs (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac), CMBS conduits, banks, and HUD/FHA, underwrite based on stabilized property performance. They want to see consistent occupancy (typically 85% to 95%), reliable net operating income, and a debt service coverage ratio that demonstrates the property can comfortably support the proposed loan payments. Properties in transition, by definition, cannot demonstrate these metrics yet.

Bridge lenders operate under a different framework. They underwrite primarily on the as-stabilized value of the property and the borrower's ability to execute a credible business plan. Because they accept the elevated risk of lending against a property that has not yet reached its potential, bridge lenders charge higher interest rates (typically 8% to 12% or more) and offer shorter terms (12 to 36 months). They are compensated for the risk through pricing, and they expect the borrower to refinance into permanent debt as quickly as possible.

The two-step approach exists because it aligns each lender's risk appetite with the corresponding phase of the property's lifecycle. The bridge lender provides capital during the high-risk transitional period. The permanent lender steps in once the property is performing and the risk profile has shifted to a stable, income-producing asset. For the borrower, the two-step structure means paying a premium for short-term flexibility but then locking in long-term savings through lower permanent rates.

The Stabilization Phase

Stabilization is the central milestone in any bridge-to-permanent strategy. It refers to the point at which the property has achieved a level of occupancy and income that permanent lenders consider sufficient for long-term underwriting. While the exact definition varies by lender and property type, most permanent lenders define stabilization as the property reaching at least 85% to 90% economic occupancy with net operating income that supports a minimum DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x.

The path to stabilization depends on the specific business plan. For a value-add multifamily acquisition, stabilization might involve renovating units, raising rents to market levels, and leasing the improved units to new tenants. For a ground-up development, stabilization means completing construction, obtaining a certificate of occupancy, and leasing the property to a target occupancy level. For a distressed acquisition, stabilization could involve resolving deferred maintenance, addressing code violations, and rebuilding a tenant base after a period of mismanagement.

Regardless of the specific plan, the borrower must track stabilization metrics throughout the bridge period. Key performance indicators to monitor monthly include physical occupancy, economic occupancy (accounting for concessions and vacancies), gross rental income, operating expenses, net operating income, and the projected DSCR based on anticipated permanent loan terms. Maintaining a real-time stabilization dashboard is not optional; it is the single most important management tool during the bridge period.

Planning the Takeout from Day One

The most common mistake in bridge-to-permanent financing is treating the permanent takeout as a future problem. Borrowers who focus exclusively on the acquisition and renovation phase and defer permanent loan planning until the bridge is nearly mature often find themselves scrambling to close a permanent loan under intense time pressure, or worse, paying costly extension fees because they started too late.

Planning the takeout from day one means establishing several things at the outset of the bridge loan.

  • Target permanent loan program. Identify which permanent loan programs are most likely to fit the property type and business plan. A multifamily property will have different permanent options (agency, HUD, bank) than a retail center (CMBS, bank, life company). Knowing the target program shapes every decision during the stabilization phase, from how you structure leases to what occupancy threshold you need to reach.
  • Stabilization benchmarks. Work backward from the permanent lender's requirements. If the target permanent lender needs 90% occupancy and a 1.25x DSCR, those become the milestones you must hit during the bridge period. Build a month-by-month pro forma that tracks your progress toward those benchmarks.
  • Timeline alignment. Map the stabilization schedule against the bridge loan maturity date, including any extension options. If your bridge loan matures in 24 months and you expect stabilization to take 18 months, you have a six-month cushion. If stabilization will take 22 months, that two-month buffer is dangerously thin once you factor in permanent loan processing time.
  • Permanent lender engagement. Begin building relationships with permanent lenders early in the bridge period. Even if you cannot submit a formal application until the property is closer to stabilization, you can share your business plan, get indicative term sheets, and understand exactly what each lender will need to see before they issue a commitment.

Permanent Loan Programs for Takeout Financing

The right permanent loan program depends on the property type, loan size, borrower profile, and desired terms. Here are the most common takeout options for bridge-to-permanent borrowers.

Agency Loans (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac)

Agency loans are the gold standard for multifamily permanent financing. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac offer fixed-rate and adjustable-rate options with terms of 5 to 30 years, amortizations up to 30 years, and leverage up to 80% LTV. Rates are typically the most competitive in the market for qualifying properties. The agencies require stabilized occupancy (usually 90% for at least 90 days) and a minimum DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x. Agency loans are non-recourse for qualifying borrowers, which is a significant advantage over bank financing. These programs work best for stabilized multifamily properties with 5 or more units.

CMBS (Commercial Mortgage-Backed Securities)

CMBS loans are available for a wide range of stabilized property types, including office, retail, industrial, hospitality, and mixed-use. Terms are typically 5 or 10 years with 25- to 30-year amortization, and leverage can reach 75% LTV. CMBS lenders require stabilized income and typically look for a DSCR of 1.25x or higher. These loans are non-recourse but include standard "bad boy" carve-outs. CMBS is a strong option for larger stabilized properties ($2 million and above) where the borrower wants non-recourse terms and competitive fixed rates across property types that do not qualify for agency programs.

Bank and Credit Union Loans

Banks and credit unions offer permanent commercial mortgages with more flexible underwriting than agency or CMBS programs. Terms typically range from 5 to 10 years (with 20- to 25-year amortization), and leverage varies from 65% to 80% LTV depending on the institution and property type. Bank loans may be fixed or adjustable rate, and they often require personal recourse. The advantage of bank financing is flexibility: banks can accommodate unique property types, smaller loan sizes, and borrowers who may not meet the stricter agency or CMBS eligibility requirements. The disadvantage is that bank loans usually carry higher rates than agency programs and shorter terms, meaning the borrower will face another refinance event in 5 to 10 years.

HUD/FHA Multifamily Programs

HUD offers several permanent loan programs for multifamily properties, including Section 223(f) for acquisitions and refinances and Section 221(d)(4) for new construction and substantial rehabilitation. HUD loans feature the longest terms in the market (up to 35 years for existing properties, 40 years for new construction), fully amortizing structures, non-recourse terms, and rates that are typically competitive with agency loans. The trade-off is a longer processing timeline (often 6 to 12 months) and more extensive regulatory requirements. For borrowers who can plan ahead, HUD permanent financing offers some of the lowest all-in costs available.

Timeline and Milestones

A well-structured bridge-to-permanent strategy follows a clear timeline with defined milestones. While every deal is different, the following framework applies to most scenarios.

Phase Timeline Key Activities
Bridge close and business plan launch Month 1 Close bridge loan, begin renovations or lease-up, engage property management
Active stabilization Months 2 to 18 Execute renovations, lease units, build occupancy, track NOI monthly
Permanent lender engagement Months 12 to 15 Request indicative terms from permanent lenders, share trailing financials
Formal permanent application Months 15 to 18 Submit full application, order appraisal and third-party reports
Underwriting and commitment Months 18 to 21 Permanent lender underwrites, issues commitment letter
Permanent loan closing Months 21 to 24 Close permanent loan, pay off bridge, begin permanent debt service

This timeline assumes a 24-month bridge term with stabilization achieved around month 18. If your bridge term is shorter (12 to 18 months), the schedule compresses accordingly, and you need to begin permanent lender engagement even earlier. The critical takeaway is that permanent loan processing takes 60 to 120 days (or longer for HUD), so you must begin the application process well before the bridge maturity date.

Rate Lock Strategies

Interest rate risk is one of the most significant variables in a bridge-to-permanent strategy. Because the bridge period can last 12 to 36 months, the interest rate environment at the time of permanent loan closing may be very different from the rate environment when the bridge loan was originated. Rising rates can reduce the permanent loan amount (because the DSCR must be maintained at a higher payment) and increase the borrower's long-term cost of capital.

Several strategies help manage this risk.

  • Forward rate locks. Some permanent lenders offer the ability to lock a rate 30, 60, or 90 days before closing. This is the most straightforward approach, but it requires the permanent loan to be in the final stages of underwriting. The lock period is limited, so timing must be precise.
  • Early rate lock programs. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac offer early rate lock programs on certain multifamily loans that allow borrowers to lock a rate further in advance (sometimes up to 180 days) for a refundable or non-refundable deposit. These programs are particularly valuable in rising rate environments.
  • Treasury rate locks (T-locks). A Treasury lock is a hedging agreement that fixes the Treasury yield component of a future loan rate. If permanent loan pricing is quoted as a spread over the 10-year Treasury, a T-lock removes the risk that the Treasury yield moves against you before closing. The spread component remains unhedged, but Treasury yield movements account for the majority of rate volatility.
  • Interest rate caps on the bridge loan. While this does not directly lock the permanent rate, purchasing an interest rate cap on a floating-rate bridge loan limits your maximum interest expense during the bridge period. This protects cash flow and ensures you reach stabilization with the financial resources you projected, even if short-term rates rise during the bridge term.

The right rate lock strategy depends on your loan size, the permanent program you are targeting, and the current rate environment. Discuss these options with your permanent lender and, if the loan is large enough to justify it, with an interest rate hedging advisor.

What Happens When You Cannot Stabilize

Not every bridge-to-permanent strategy goes according to plan. Construction delays, slower-than-expected lease-up, market downturns, or unexpected capital needs can all prevent a property from reaching stabilization by the bridge loan maturity date. Knowing your options in advance is essential.

Bridge Loan Extensions

Most bridge loans include one or two built-in extension options, each typically lasting 6 to 12 months. These extensions are usually available if the borrower has been current on all payments and meets certain pre-negotiated conditions (such as achieving a minimum occupancy threshold or completing renovations). Extension fees typically range from 0.25% to 1.00% of the outstanding loan balance per extension period. If your bridge loan includes extension options, make sure you understand the exact conditions and fees before you need them.

Loan Modifications

If the property is making progress but not enough to meet the original extension conditions, the bridge lender may be willing to negotiate a loan modification. This could involve adjusting the maturity date, modifying performance covenants, or restructuring the payment terms. Bridge lenders generally prefer to work with a cooperative borrower rather than pursue foreclosure, especially if the property is trending in the right direction. Open and transparent communication with your lender is the key to keeping modification options available.

Refinancing into a New Bridge Loan

If the current bridge lender is unwilling to extend or modify, refinancing into a new bridge loan with a different lender is another option. This comes with additional costs (new origination fees, new third-party reports, legal fees) and resets the clock, but it provides the additional time needed to complete stabilization. The availability and cost of a new bridge loan will depend on current market conditions, the property's progress, and the borrower's overall financial position.

Capital Injection or Partial Paydown

In some cases, the bridge lender may agree to extend or modify the loan if the borrower makes a partial paydown or injects additional equity into the project. This reduces the lender's risk exposure and demonstrates the borrower's continued commitment to the business plan. While this requires additional capital, it can be a practical solution when the property is close to stabilization but needs more time.

Common Deal Structures

Bridge-to-permanent financing can be structured in several ways depending on the borrower's goals, the property type, and the lender landscape.

  • Two-close structure. The most common approach, where the bridge loan and permanent loan are originated separately by different lenders at different times. The borrower closes the bridge loan first, executes the business plan, and then applies for and closes the permanent loan when the property is stabilized. This structure offers maximum flexibility in choosing the best permanent lender at the time of takeout.
  • Single-lender bridge-to-permanent. Some lenders offer both the bridge and permanent loan as a packaged product. The borrower closes the bridge loan with the understanding that the same lender will provide the permanent takeout once stabilization benchmarks are met. This approach can simplify the process and reduce closing costs, but it limits the borrower's ability to shop for the best permanent terms at the time of takeout.
  • Forward commitment. In this structure, the permanent lender issues a commitment to provide the takeout loan at a future date, contingent on the property meeting specified performance benchmarks. The borrower uses this commitment to secure the bridge loan (and the bridge lender gains comfort knowing the permanent exit is pre-arranged). Forward commitments provide certainty but come with commitment fees and may lock the borrower into terms that are less competitive than what the market offers at the time of actual takeout.

Case Study: 120-Unit Value-Add Multifamily

Consider a borrower acquiring a 120-unit apartment complex for $12 million. The property is 70% occupied with below-market rents and deferred maintenance. The borrower's business plan calls for $1.5 million in unit renovations over 12 months, rent increases averaging $200 per unit, and a lease-up to 93% occupancy within 18 months.

Bridge Phase

The borrower secures a $10.5 million bridge loan (78% of acquisition cost plus renovation budget) at a 9.5% interest rate with a 24-month term and two 6-month extension options. Monthly interest-only payments are approximately $83,125. Total bridge interest over 18 months equals roughly $1.5 million, and the origination fee (2 points) adds $210,000.

Stabilization Results

After 16 months, the renovation is complete. Occupancy has reached 92%, and the average rent has increased from $950 to $1,150 per unit. Gross potential rent is now approximately $1.66 million annually. After operating expenses of $620,000, the stabilized net operating income is approximately $1.04 million.

Permanent Takeout

The borrower applies for a Fannie Mae permanent loan at month 15. After underwriting, the lender offers a 10-year fixed-rate loan at 5.75% with 30-year amortization, 75% LTV based on a new appraised value of $17.3 million (using a 6.0% cap rate). The permanent loan amount is $12.97 million, which pays off the $10.5 million bridge loan and returns a portion of the borrower's equity. The annual debt service on the permanent loan is approximately $908,000, producing a DSCR of 1.15x. The lender agrees to the deal based on projected income growth, and the loan closes at month 20.

The borrower has successfully executed the bridge-to-permanent strategy: acquiring a transitional asset with bridge debt, executing a value-add business plan, and refinancing into a long-term, lower-cost permanent loan that both pays off the bridge and returns equity.

Cost Comparison: Two-Loan vs. Single-Close

Borrowers often ask whether the two-step bridge-to-permanent approach is worth the additional cost compared to a single-close construction-to-permanent loan. Here is a side-by-side comparison.

Cost Component Two-Loan (Bridge + Permanent) Single-Close Construction-to-Permanent
Bridge origination fee 1% to 3% N/A
Permanent origination fee 0.50% to 1.00% 0.75% to 1.50%
Bridge interest (12 to 24 months) 8% to 12% N/A (construction draw rate applies)
Construction/interim rate N/A 6% to 9% (during construction only)
Third-party reports Two sets (bridge + permanent) One set
Legal and closing costs Two closings One closing
Total cost premium 2% to 5% higher total cost Lower total cost
Flexibility to choose best permanent terms High Low (locked to one lender)

The two-loan approach is more expensive in direct financing costs. However, it offers significantly more flexibility. The borrower can choose the best permanent loan program and lender available at the time of takeout, rather than being locked into terms negotiated before the property was stabilized. For many borrowers, the ability to shop the permanent market at the optimal moment more than offsets the additional costs.

When Single-Close Construction-to-Permanent Is Better

Despite the flexibility advantages of the two-loan approach, there are scenarios where a single-close construction-to-permanent loan is the superior choice.

  • New construction with clear demand. If the borrower is building a new property in a market with strong, demonstrable demand (for example, affordable housing with a waiting list, or a build-to-suit for a creditworthy tenant), the risk of failing to stabilize is low. A single-close loan eliminates the refinance risk entirely and reduces total financing costs.
  • HUD/FHA 221(d)(4) eligibility. HUD's 221(d)(4) program offers a single-close construction-to-permanent loan with a 40-year fully amortizing term, non-recourse structure, and highly competitive rates. For qualifying multifamily projects, this program delivers better economics than almost any bridge-to-permanent combination.
  • Borrower preference for certainty. Some borrowers place a high value on knowing their permanent terms from the outset. A single-close loan removes the uncertainty of future rate environments, permanent lender availability, and refinance risk. The borrower knows the permanent rate, term, and amortization before the first shovel goes into the ground.
  • Smaller projects with limited lender appetite. For smaller projects (under $3 million), the cost of two separate closings, two sets of legal fees, and two rounds of third-party reports can represent a disproportionately large share of the total project cost. A single-close structure consolidates these expenses and is often more economical for smaller deals.
  • Properties with predictable cash flow. If the property type has a highly predictable income stream (such as a net-leased property with a long-term tenant in place), a single-close loan makes sense because the stabilization risk that justifies the two-loan approach simply does not exist.

The choice between a two-loan bridge-to-permanent strategy and a single-close approach should be driven by the specific risk profile of the project. When stabilization risk is high and the borrower needs flexibility, the two-loan structure is usually worth the extra cost. When the path to stable income is clear and predictable, a single-close loan offers simplicity and savings.

Frequently Asked Questions

You should begin planning your permanent takeout at least six to nine months before your bridge loan matures. This timeline allows sufficient room to select a permanent lender, complete the application and underwriting process, order third-party reports (appraisal, environmental, survey), and close the new loan before the bridge deadline.

Many experienced borrowers start even earlier, engaging permanent lenders during the active stabilization phase so they have indicative terms and a clear understanding of what benchmarks must be met. If you wait until the final few months of the bridge term, you risk being forced into an extension (with additional fees and higher rates) or accepting less favorable permanent terms because of time pressure.

The best practice is to identify your target permanent loan program and begin lender conversations at the halfway point of your bridge term. This gives you time to course-correct if the property is not tracking toward the stabilization benchmarks that permanent lenders require.

If your property has not reached the stabilization benchmarks required for permanent financing by the time your bridge loan matures, you generally have several options. The most common is negotiating an extension with your bridge lender, which typically involves an extension fee of 0.50% to 1.00% of the outstanding balance and a continued or increased interest rate. Many bridge loans include built-in extension options (for example, two six-month extensions) that are contractually available if you meet certain conditions, such as being current on payments and demonstrating progress toward stabilization.

If an extension is not available or not sufficient, you may need to refinance into another short-term bridge loan with a new lender, which involves new origination costs and resets the timeline. In some cases, the bridge lender may agree to a loan modification that adjusts the maturity date or performance covenants.

In a worst-case scenario where the lender is unwilling to extend and no refinance is available, the lender may initiate foreclosure proceedings. This is why planning the permanent exit early and maintaining open communication with your bridge lender throughout the loan term is essential. Lenders strongly prefer to work with cooperative borrowers who are transparent about challenges rather than borrowers who avoid communication until the last moment.

In most cases, you cannot lock a permanent rate until you are close to closing on the permanent loan, because lenders require the property to meet stabilization and underwriting criteria before offering a firm rate lock. However, there are strategies to manage rate risk during the bridge period.

Some permanent lenders offer forward rate locks for a fee, allowing you to secure a rate 30 to 90 days (and sometimes longer) before closing. Agency lenders like Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac sometimes offer early rate lock programs on multifamily properties for an upfront deposit. Another approach is to use interest rate hedging instruments, such as Treasury locks or interest rate caps, to limit your exposure to rising rates while you complete stabilization.

The cost and availability of these tools depend on your loan size, property type, and market conditions. Discussing rate lock timing with your permanent lender early in the bridge period helps you understand your options and build rate risk management into your overall strategy.

The debt service coverage ratio required for permanent financing varies by loan program and property type, but most permanent lenders look for a minimum DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x based on stabilized net operating income. Here is a breakdown by program type.

Agency lenders (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac) typically require 1.20x to 1.25x for multifamily properties. CMBS lenders generally require 1.25x or higher. Bank and credit union lenders may require 1.25x to 1.35x depending on the property type and their internal risk guidelines. HUD/FHA multifamily programs often require around 1.18x to 1.20x, which is among the most borrower-friendly thresholds in the market.

These ratios are calculated using actual or stabilized income against the proposed permanent loan payment, including principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and sometimes reserves. During your bridge period, tracking your property's DSCR on a monthly basis is essential for understanding when you will be ready to transition to permanent financing. Your permanent lender can provide a precise DSCR target based on your specific loan program and property type.

The total cost of a bridge-to-permanent strategy includes the costs of both loans plus any interim expenses during the bridge period. For the bridge loan, expect an origination fee of 1% to 3% of the loan amount, an interest rate of 8% to 12% or higher, and potential extension fees if you need additional time. For the permanent loan, costs typically include an origination fee (0.50% to 1.00%), a new appraisal and third-party reports ($10,000 to $25,000 or more depending on property size), legal fees, title insurance, and any rate lock deposits.

When combined, the two-loan approach typically adds 2% to 5% to the total project cost compared to a scenario where permanent financing is available from the start. However, this premium is the cost of executing a value-add or lease-up strategy that could not have been financed with permanent debt alone.

Many borrowers find that the increase in property value and income achieved during the bridge period far exceeds the additional financing costs. In the case study above, for example, the borrower spent roughly $1.7 million in bridge loan costs but created over $5 million in additional property value through renovations and lease-up. The two-step approach was a strong net positive when measured against the total return on the investment.
