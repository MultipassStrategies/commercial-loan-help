How to Get a Commercial Loan with a Low Credit Score

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Most lenders require: 680+ credit score
  • Some programs accept: 600 to 640
  • Best options: Hard money and private lenders
  • Compensating factors: Cash flow, equity, experience
  • Improvement timeline: 6 to 12 months
  • Key strategy: Focus on the property

How Credit Scores Work in Commercial Lending

In commercial lending, your personal credit score plays a different role than it does in residential mortgages. When you apply for a home loan, the lender focuses almost entirely on your individual creditworthiness: your FICO score, your debt-to-income ratio, and your payment history. Commercial lenders look at these factors too, but they also evaluate the income-producing potential of the property itself, the financial health of the business, and the overall structure of the deal. This distinction is critical for borrowers with lower credit scores because it means there are more levers you can pull to get approved.

Most commercial lenders will pull the personal credit reports of every guarantor or principal with 20% or more ownership in the borrowing entity. The FICO scores that matter most in commercial lending are typically the middle scores from the three major bureaus (Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion). If you have multiple guarantors, many lenders will use the lowest middle score among all guarantors as the qualifying score, though some will average them or focus on the score of the controlling member. Understanding how your particular lender handles multiple guarantors is an important part of your pre-application strategy.

Beyond the raw FICO number, commercial underwriters examine the content of your credit report in detail. They look for patterns of late payments, the age and nature of any derogatory marks, the total amount of outstanding debt, and the ratio of revolving balances to available credit limits. A borrower with a 640 score caused by a single medical collection from three years ago will be viewed very differently from a borrower with a 640 score caused by a pattern of recent missed payments on business credit cards. Context matters enormously, and experienced loan officers know how to read a credit report beyond the headline number.

The SBA uses its own credit assessment tool called the FICO Small Business Scoring Service (SBSS), which blends personal credit data with business credit information from Dun and Bradstreet and other commercial bureaus. The SBSS generates a score on a scale of 0 to 300, and most SBA lenders look for a minimum SBSS score of around 155. This composite approach means that strong business credit can partially offset weaker personal credit, giving SBA applicants an additional pathway to qualification that is not available with conventional bank loans.

Your job as a borrower is to match your credit profile to the right lending channel rather than applying broadly and collecting rejections that can further damage your score through hard credit inquiries.

Why Commercial Lending Differs from Residential

One of the most common mistakes borrowers make is assuming that commercial lending works the same way as residential lending. In the residential world, your personal credit score is the gatekeeper. Below a certain threshold, you simply cannot get a conforming mortgage, and there is very little room for negotiation. Commercial lending operates on a fundamentally different model.

Commercial loans are underwritten based on three pillars: the borrower (your credit and financial strength), the property (its income, condition, and market position), and the deal structure (loan-to-value ratio, debt service coverage, and equity contribution). A weakness in one pillar can often be offset by strength in the other two. This is why a borrower with a 620 credit score can still secure financing for a well-located, fully stabilized multifamily property with a 1.40x DSCR and 35% equity. The property and the deal structure carry the weight that the borrower's credit cannot.

This three-pillar approach exists because commercial loans are fundamentally asset-backed investments. The lender is betting on the property's ability to generate enough income to service the debt, not solely on your personal promise to repay. When a commercial loan defaults, the lender's recovery depends on the value and income of the underlying real estate, not on garnishing the borrower's wages. This reality shapes every underwriting decision and creates opportunities for borrowers whose personal credit does not tell the full story of their ability to manage a successful commercial real estate investment.

Compensating Factors That Offset Low Credit

If your credit score falls below the typical threshold for your target loan type, compensating factors become your most important tool. These are strengths in other areas of your application that give the lender confidence despite the credit weakness. Here are the most powerful compensating factors in commercial lending.

Strong Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR)

The DSCR measures the property's net operating income relative to its annual debt service. A DSCR of 1.25x means the property generates 25% more income than needed to cover the loan payments. Most lenders require a minimum DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x, but if your property delivers a DSCR of 1.40x or higher, many underwriters will view this as a meaningful offset to a weaker credit profile. The logic is straightforward: a property with a substantial cash flow cushion is less likely to default regardless of the guarantor's personal credit history. When presenting your deal, lead with the DSCR if it is strong.

Higher Equity or Lower Loan-to-Value

Putting more money down reduces the lender's risk exposure and signals your commitment to the deal. If a lender typically requires 75% LTV (25% down) for a borrower with a 700 credit score, offering 35% to 40% down can persuade the same lender to approve a borrower with a 640 score. The additional equity provides a larger cushion against declining property values and gives the lender a more favorable recovery position in a worst-case scenario. For borrowers with credit challenges, extra equity is one of the most direct and effective ways to get to yes.

Strong Property Fundamentals

A well-located property with strong occupancy, quality tenants, and a history of stable or growing income is inherently less risky from the lender's perspective. Properties in supply-constrained markets with high barriers to entry, long-term leases with creditworthy tenants, and recent capital improvements all strengthen your application. Lenders are more willing to overlook personal credit weaknesses when the underlying asset provides reliable and predictable income. If you are acquiring a property with these characteristics, make sure your loan package highlights them prominently.

Real Estate or Business Experience

Lenders take comfort in borrowers who have a proven track record of successfully owning and operating commercial properties or businesses. If you can demonstrate that you have managed similar assets, navigated market cycles, and maintained solid occupancy and cash flow in previous investments, your experience becomes a powerful counterweight to a lower credit score. Prepare a detailed resume of your real estate holdings or business operations, including property types, hold periods, returns, and any value-add improvements you have executed.

Co-Guarantor with Strong Credit

Adding a co-guarantor (sometimes called a key principal or co-sponsor) with a strong credit profile can dramatically improve your chances of approval. The co-guarantor's credit score, net worth, and liquidity are factored into the underwriting, effectively supplementing your own financial profile. This strategy is especially effective with SBA loans and conventional bank products where personal guarantees are central to the credit decision. The co-guarantor must understand that they are accepting full liability for the loan, so this arrangement requires a high degree of trust and a clear legal agreement between the parties.

Available Programs for Lower Credit Borrowers

Not all lenders view credit the same way. Several lending channels are specifically designed to serve borrowers who do not fit the conventional bank mold. Understanding these options is the first step toward finding the right financing.

Hard Money and Private Lenders

Hard money lenders are asset-based lenders who make credit decisions primarily on the value and income potential of the property rather than the borrower's personal credit profile. Many hard money lenders will fund deals for borrowers with credit scores in the 550 to 600 range, and some do not even pull personal credit if the LTV is low enough and the property fundamentals are strong. The tradeoff is cost: hard money loans typically carry interest rates of 9% to 14%, origination fees of 1 to 3 points, and shorter terms of 12 to 36 months. These loans work best as bridge financing, giving you time to stabilize a property or improve your credit before refinancing into a lower-cost permanent loan.

Private Lenders and Debt Funds

Private lenders and commercial debt funds occupy a middle ground between hard money and conventional banks. They offer more flexible underwriting than banks but typically charge less than traditional hard money lenders. Many private lenders will work with borrowers in the 600 to 650 credit range if the deal makes sense on its merits. Terms usually range from 2 to 5 years, with rates in the 8% to 12% range depending on the property type and leverage. These lenders can often close faster than banks because they use their own capital and do not need to follow the same regulatory framework.

Portfolio Lenders

Portfolio lenders are banks and credit unions that originate loans and hold them on their own balance sheets rather than selling them to the secondary market. Because they keep the loans, they have more flexibility in their underwriting standards and can make exceptions that secondary-market lenders cannot. A portfolio lender may approve a borrower with a 640 credit score if the relationship, the property, and the deal structure are all favorable. Community banks and regional banks are the most common portfolio lenders, and they often value long-term deposit relationships and local market knowledge as much as raw credit metrics. Building a relationship with a community banker is one of the best long-term strategies for borrowers with imperfect credit.

Community Development Financial Institutions (CDFIs)

CDFIs are mission-driven lenders certified by the U.S. Treasury to serve underserved communities and borrowers who have difficulty accessing conventional financing. CDFIs include nonprofit loan funds, community development banks, and credit unions that receive federal funding to support economic development in low-income and distressed areas. Because their mission is to expand access to capital, CDFIs often have more flexible credit requirements than traditional banks. Many will work with borrowers who have credit scores in the 575 to 640 range, and some will consider applicants with scores below 575 if the borrower can demonstrate strong repayment capacity through business cash flow or property income. CDFIs also frequently offer technical assistance, helping borrowers strengthen their applications and build the financial skills needed for long-term success.

Credit Unions

Some credit unions with commercial lending programs offer more personalized underwriting than larger banks. As member-owned institutions, credit unions may consider the full picture of a borrower's financial situation rather than relying solely on automated scoring models. If you are a member of a credit union that offers commercial real estate loans, it is worth having a conversation with their commercial lending team even if your credit score is below the typical threshold. Credit union commercial loan officers often have more discretion to make character-based lending decisions, particularly for smaller deals and local properties.

Strategies to Improve Your Credit Before Applying

If your credit score is below 680 and you have the flexibility to wait six to twelve months before applying, targeted credit improvement can save you tens of thousands of dollars in interest over the life of your loan. Here are the highest-impact strategies.

  • Pay down revolving balances. Credit utilization (the ratio of your balances to your credit limits) is the second most influential factor in your FICO score after payment history. Reducing your utilization below 30% can produce a significant score improvement within one to two billing cycles. Below 10% is ideal. If you cannot pay off the balances entirely, focus on the cards and lines with the highest utilization ratios first.
  • Dispute errors on your credit report. Studies have shown that a meaningful percentage of credit reports contain errors that negatively affect scores. Pull your reports from all three bureaus at AnnualCreditReport.com and review them line by line. Dispute any inaccurate late payments, incorrect balances, accounts that are not yours, and outdated derogatory marks. The bureaus have 30 days to investigate and respond to disputes.
  • Become current on all accounts. If you have any accounts that are currently past due, bring them current immediately. While the late payment will remain on your report, stopping the bleeding prevents additional derogatory marks and begins the clock on recovery. Consistent on-time payments going forward will gradually rebuild your score.
  • Avoid opening new credit accounts. Each new credit application generates a hard inquiry that can temporarily reduce your score by a few points. More importantly, new accounts lower your average account age, which is another factor in your score. In the months leading up to a commercial loan application, avoid applying for new credit cards, auto loans, or personal loans unless absolutely necessary.
  • Negotiate with creditors. If you have collections or charge-offs, contact the original creditor or collection agency and negotiate a pay-for-delete agreement, where the creditor agrees to remove the negative entry from your report in exchange for payment. Not all creditors will agree to this, but it is always worth asking because removing a collection can produce a meaningful score increase.
  • Keep old accounts open. The length of your credit history matters. Even if you no longer use an old credit card, keeping the account open maintains your average account age and contributes to your total available credit (which helps your utilization ratio). Closing old accounts is one of the most common mistakes borrowers make when trying to "clean up" their credit.

Using a Co-Guarantor or Key Principal

For borrowers who need to move forward on a deal without the luxury of waiting months to improve their credit, bringing in a co-guarantor is one of the most effective strategies available. A co-guarantor (sometimes referred to as a key principal in CMBS or agency lending) is an individual who joins the loan as a guarantor, pledging their personal creditworthiness and assets to support the deal.

The ideal co-guarantor has a personal credit score above 700, significant liquid assets (typically at least 10% of the loan amount in post-closing liquidity), a strong net worth, and ideally some experience in commercial real estate. The co-guarantor does not need to be involved in the day-to-day management of the property, but they must be willing to sign a personal guarantee and accept responsibility for the debt in the event of default.

There are several common arrangements for co-guarantor relationships. Some co-guarantors are family members who have strong credit and are willing to support the borrower's business goals. Others are business partners or investors who take an equity stake in the deal in exchange for providing their credit support. In some cases, experienced real estate operators serve as co-sponsors on deals where the primary borrower brings the property and the capital, while the co-sponsor provides the credit profile and operating experience that lenders require.

When structuring a co-guarantor arrangement, it is essential to have a clear written agreement that defines each party's rights, responsibilities, and economic interests. Both parties should consult with an attorney before signing any loan documents to ensure they understand the full scope of the personal guarantee and any recourse provisions in the loan agreement.

The Cost of Borrowing with Low Credit

Borrowing with a low credit score is possible, but it comes at a price. Understanding the true cost helps you make an informed decision about whether to proceed now or wait to improve your position.

The most obvious cost is a higher interest rate. As discussed above, borrowers with credit scores below 680 typically pay 1 to 4 percentage points more than those with strong credit, depending on the loan type. On a $2 million loan with a 10-year term, an extra 2 percentage points in interest adds approximately $400,000 in total interest expense over the life of the loan. For hard money or private loans, where the rate premium can be 4 to 6 points above conventional rates, the cost is even more significant.

Beyond the interest rate, low-credit borrowers often face higher origination fees (typically 1 to 3 points compared to 0.5 to 1 point for conventional loans), more restrictive loan terms (shorter maturities, lower LTVs, higher reserves), and more onerous reporting requirements. Some lenders also require interest reserves or debt service escrows that tie up additional capital.

There are also opportunity costs to consider. The higher debt service payments reduce your property's cash flow, which limits your ability to make improvements, build reserves, or reinvest in your portfolio. And because low-credit loans often have shorter terms, you face refinance risk at maturity: if your credit has not improved or market conditions have worsened, you may have difficulty refinancing on favorable terms.

The key calculation for every borrower is whether the potential returns from proceeding now outweigh the additional costs of borrowing with impaired credit. In some cases, the answer is clearly yes. If you are acquiring a value-add property at a significant discount, the upside potential may far exceed the extra interest cost. In other cases, waiting six to twelve months to improve your credit score by 40 to 60 points can save enough in interest to justify the delay.

Rebuilding Credit While Operating

If you have already secured a commercial loan with less-than-ideal terms, you can begin rebuilding your credit immediately with the goal of refinancing into better terms within two to three years. Here are practical steps to follow while operating your property.

  • Automate all payments. Set up automatic payments for every personal and business obligation to ensure nothing falls through the cracks. Payment history accounts for roughly 35% of your FICO score, and even a single 30-day late payment can set your rebuilding efforts back significantly.
  • Systematically reduce revolving debt. Use a portion of your property's cash flow (after reserves and operating expenses) to pay down personal and business credit card balances. Focus on the accounts with the highest utilization ratios first. As your utilization drops below 30%, then below 10%, your score will climb steadily.
  • Monitor your credit regularly. Use a free credit monitoring service to track your score monthly and catch any new derogatory marks or errors as soon as they appear. Early detection allows you to address issues before they compound.
  • Build business credit separately. Establish and build your business credit profile with Dun and Bradstreet, Experian Business, and Equifax Business. Paying vendors and suppliers on time, opening a business credit card and keeping utilization low, and registering with the major business credit bureaus all contribute to a stronger business credit profile that can supplement your personal score on future loan applications.
  • Plan your refinance timeline. Work backward from your loan's maturity date to determine when you need to begin the refinance process. If your loan matures in 36 months and the refinance process takes 60 to 90 days, you should be actively improving your credit for the first 30 months and engaging lenders no later than month 30. Having a clear timeline keeps you disciplined and focused.

When to Wait vs. When to Proceed

The decision to move forward with a commercial loan application or wait to improve your credit is one of the most important judgment calls a borrower can make. Here are guidelines to help you evaluate your situation.

Consider proceeding now if:

  • You have identified a property with exceptional value-add potential or a below-market purchase price that will not be available if you wait.
  • Your credit issues are isolated and explainable (a medical event, a one-time business disruption), and you can present strong compensating factors such as high DSCR, substantial equity, or an experienced co-guarantor.
  • You have a clear refinance strategy and are confident you can improve your credit within the term of the initial loan.
  • The cost of higher-rate financing is justified by the deal's projected returns.

Consider waiting if:

  • Your credit score is within 40 to 60 points of the threshold you need, and you can realistically close that gap within six to twelve months through utilization reduction and dispute resolution.
  • You do not have a specific deal under contract and are exploring the market generally. Improving your credit before engaging lenders gives you access to better programs, lower rates, and more negotiating leverage.
  • Your credit report contains recent and severe derogatory marks (multiple recent late payments, a very recent bankruptcy, active collections) that will be difficult for any lender to overlook regardless of compensating factors.
  • You cannot afford the higher costs of low-credit financing without putting the deal's viability at risk.

In many cases, the best approach is a combination: begin improving your credit immediately while simultaneously identifying potential deals and building relationships with lenders who serve your credit tier. That way, when the right property appears, you will be ready to move quickly with the best terms your improving credit profile can command.

The time required to improve your credit score depends on what is causing the low score and the actions you take.
