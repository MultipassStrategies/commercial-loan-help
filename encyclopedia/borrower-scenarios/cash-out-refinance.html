Cash-Out Refinance for Commercial Real Estate: Access Your Equity

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Max LTV: 65-75% typically
  • Cash-out uses: Acquisitions, improvements, reserves, distributions
  • Rate impact: May be slightly higher
  • Available programs: Bank, CMBS, agency, SBA
  • Tax benefit: Cash-out proceeds are not taxable income
  • Processing time: 45-90 days

What Is a Cash-Out Refinance?

A cash-out refinance is a financing strategy in which a commercial property owner replaces an existing mortgage with a new, larger loan and receives the difference between the two loan amounts as liquid cash. Unlike a rate-and-term refinance, where the goal is simply to secure better loan terms without changing the principal balance, a cash-out refinance is specifically designed to unlock the equity that has accumulated in a property over time. That equity may have built up through regular mortgage payments that reduced the outstanding balance, through appreciation in the property's market value, or through both.

For commercial real estate owners, a cash-out refinance represents one of the most efficient ways to access capital without selling an asset. Because the proceeds come from a loan rather than a sale, the property owner retains full ownership, continues to collect rental income, and avoids triggering capital gains taxes. This makes cash-out refinancing a cornerstone strategy for investors and business owners looking to grow their portfolios, fund property improvements, or redeploy capital into higher-return opportunities.

The concept is straightforward in principle: if your property is worth $3 million and your existing loan balance is $1.2 million, you have $1.8 million in equity. A cash-out refinance allows you to take a new loan at, say, 70% LTV ($2.1 million), pay off the existing $1.2 million balance, and pocket the remaining $900,000. Of course, the specifics depend on the loan program, the property type, the borrower's financial profile, and the lender's underwriting standards.

How Cash-Out Refinancing Works in Commercial Real Estate

The mechanics of a commercial cash-out refinance follow a predictable sequence, though the details vary depending on the loan type and lender. Understanding this process from start to finish helps borrowers set realistic expectations and prepare the documentation they will need.

The process begins with an assessment of your current equity position. You need to know your property's current market value and your existing loan balance. The difference between these two figures is your gross equity. However, you will not be able to extract all of that equity; lenders impose maximum loan-to-value (LTV) ratios that limit how much of the property's value they will lend against. For most commercial cash-out transactions, the maximum LTV falls between 65% and 75%, depending on the loan program and the property type.

Once you have identified a target loan amount, you apply with a lender (or multiple lenders) for the new financing. The lender will order a new appraisal to establish the property's current market value, review your financial statements and tax returns, analyze the property's income and expenses, and calculate the debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) to confirm that the property's cash flow can support the larger loan. If the numbers work, the lender issues a commitment, the loan closes, and the existing mortgage is paid off from the new loan proceeds. The remaining funds are distributed to you, typically within a few business days of closing.

One critical detail that many borrowers overlook: cash-out refinancing resets your loan terms. You are starting a new loan from scratch, which means new origination fees, new closing costs, and potentially a new interest rate that may be higher or lower than your current rate. The total cost of the transaction, including fees and the ongoing cost of the larger loan balance, must be weighed against the benefits of accessing the cash.

Maximum Cash-Out LTV by Loan Type

One of the most important variables in any cash-out refinance is the maximum LTV the lender will allow. This single number determines how much equity you can actually extract. Different loan programs have different LTV limits, and some impose additional restrictions based on property type, borrower experience, or seasoning (how long you have owned the property). The following table summarizes the typical maximum LTV for cash-out refinances across the major commercial loan programs.

Loan Program Max Cash-Out LTV Property Types Key Restrictions
Conventional Bank 65-70% All commercial Varies by bank; DSCR floor of 1.20x-1.30x
CMBS 65-70% Stabilized income-producing 12+ month seasoning; limited flexibility post-close
Fannie Mae Up to 75% Multifamily (5+ units) 12-month seasoning; DSCR minimum 1.25x
Freddie Mac Up to 75% Multifamily (5+ units) 12-month seasoning; stabilized properties
SBA 504 Up to 75% Owner-occupied commercial Cash-out limited to 20% of appraised value for eligible business expenses
Bridge / Private 65-75% All commercial Higher rates; shorter terms; minimal seasoning

It is important to note that these are general ranges. Individual lenders within each category may be more conservative or more aggressive depending on the deal's specifics. A well-located, fully stabilized multifamily property with strong cash flow will receive more favorable LTV treatment than a single-tenant retail building with a short remaining lease term. Always request specific LTV guidance from your lender based on the actual property and borrower profile.

Eligible Uses for Cash-Out Proceeds

One of the advantages of a cash-out refinance (particularly through non-SBA programs) is the flexibility in how you can deploy the proceeds. While SBA programs restrict cash-out to eligible business expenses, most conventional, CMBS, and agency lenders place fewer limitations on how you use the funds once they are disbursed.

  • Portfolio expansion: Many investors use cash-out proceeds as down payments on additional properties, effectively leveraging the equity in one asset to acquire another. This is one of the most common and strategically valuable uses of cash-out refinancing.
  • Capital improvements: Renovating or upgrading a property can increase rental income, attract higher-quality tenants, and boost the property's long-term value. Cash-out funds are frequently used for major renovations, tenant improvements, energy efficiency upgrades, and deferred maintenance.
  • Cash reserves: Building up operating reserves provides a buffer against vacancies, unexpected repairs, or economic downturns. Lenders generally view healthy reserves as a positive factor in underwriting.
  • Partner distributions: In partnership or syndication structures, cash-out refinancing can be used to return capital to investors without triggering a taxable event. This is a popular strategy in value-add real estate investing, where operators improve a property, refinance to extract the increased equity, and distribute proceeds to limited partners.
  • Debt consolidation: Cash-out proceeds can be used to pay off higher-interest debt, such as mezzanine loans, bridge loans, or credit lines, simplifying your capital structure and reducing your overall cost of capital.
  • Business operations: For owner-occupants, cash-out funds can support hiring, inventory, equipment purchases, or other operational needs.

Cost-Benefit Analysis Framework

Before proceeding with a cash-out refinance, every borrower should run a disciplined cost-benefit analysis. Extracting equity is not free; it comes with transaction costs, a larger ongoing debt obligation, and potentially a higher interest rate. The question is whether the value you create with the extracted cash exceeds the total cost of obtaining it.

Costs to Account For

  • Origination fees: Typically 0.5% to 1.0% of the new loan amount.
  • Appraisal, legal, and closing costs: These can range from $10,000 to $50,000 or more depending on the deal size and complexity.
  • Prepayment penalty on the existing loan: If your current loan has a yield maintenance, defeasance, or step-down prepayment provision, the cost of exiting early can be significant. This is often the single largest expense in a cash-out refinance.
  • Higher interest rate: Cash-out refinances sometimes carry a rate premium of 10 to 25 basis points compared to rate-and-term refinances. Over the life of the loan, this can add up.
  • Increased monthly payments: A larger loan balance means higher debt service, which reduces your property's net cash flow.

Benefits to Quantify

  • Return on deployed capital: If you invest the cash-out proceeds into a new property or improvement that generates a return higher than your cost of borrowing, the refinance creates positive leverage.
  • Tax-free access to equity: Compared to selling the property, which would trigger capital gains and depreciation recapture taxes, a cash-out refinance provides liquidity without a tax event.
  • Portfolio growth: The ability to acquire additional assets accelerates wealth building and diversification.
  • Improved property value: Capital improvements funded by cash-out proceeds can increase the property's NOI and market value, potentially creating a virtuous cycle for future refinancing.

A simple rule of thumb: if the expected return on the cash-out proceeds exceeds your blended borrowing cost by a meaningful margin (at least 200 to 300 basis points), the transaction is likely worth pursuing. If the spread is thin or negative, the risk may outweigh the reward.

Tax Implications of Cash-Out Refinancing

The tax treatment of cash-out refinance proceeds is one of the most frequently misunderstood aspects of this strategy. The core rule is simple: cash-out proceeds are not taxable income. Because you are borrowing money (not earning it), the IRS does not treat the funds as income, capital gains, or any other form of taxable event. This is fundamentally different from selling a property, which would trigger capital gains taxes on any appreciation and depreciation recapture taxes on the accumulated depreciation deductions you have claimed.

This tax advantage is a major reason why experienced investors favor cash-out refinancing as a way to access equity. Consider an investor who purchased a property for $1.5 million ten years ago. The property is now worth $3 million, and the remaining loan balance is $900,000. If the investor sold the property, they would owe capital gains tax on the $1.5 million in appreciation plus depreciation recapture on the deductions taken over ten years. Depending on the investor's tax bracket and state, the combined tax bill could easily exceed $400,000. By contrast, a cash-out refinance at 70% LTV would provide $1.2 million in tax-free proceeds ($2.1 million new loan minus $900,000 payoff) with zero tax liability on the transaction itself.

Interest Deductibility Considerations

While the proceeds themselves are not taxed, the interest you pay on the new loan has its own tax implications. Mortgage interest on commercial properties used for business or investment purposes is generally deductible against the income generated by those activities. However, the deductibility of interest on the cash-out portion specifically depends on how you use the proceeds.

  • Business or investment use: If you use the cash-out proceeds to acquire another property, make capital improvements, fund business operations, or invest in income-producing assets, the interest on the entire loan (including the cash-out portion) is generally deductible.
  • Personal use: If the proceeds are used for personal expenses unrelated to business or investment activity, the interest on the cash-out portion may not be deductible. The IRS traces the use of loan proceeds when determining deductibility.
  • Mixed use: If proceeds are split between business and personal purposes, you may need to allocate the interest deduction proportionally.

Given the complexity of interest tracing rules, consult a qualified tax advisor before closing a cash-out refinance. Proper structuring of the transaction and clear documentation of how proceeds are used can help maximize your deductions.

Cash-Out vs. HELOC vs. Supplemental Loan

A cash-out refinance is not the only way to access equity in a commercial property. Two other common options are commercial lines of credit (sometimes called HELOCs, though the term is more common in residential lending) and supplemental loans. Each has distinct advantages and trade-offs, and the right choice depends on your specific circumstances.

Feature Cash-Out Refinance Commercial Line of Credit Supplemental Loan
How it works Replace existing loan with a larger one; receive difference as cash Second-position revolving credit line secured by the property Additional fixed loan layered behind the existing first mortgage
Existing loan impact Fully replaced Stays in place Stays in place
Interest rate Fixed or variable; first-lien pricing Variable; typically higher than first-lien rates Fixed or variable; higher than first-lien rates
Prepayment penalty Must pay off existing loan (potential penalty) None on the existing loan None on the existing loan
Max combined LTV 65-75% 65-75% combined 70-80% combined (agency programs)
Flexibility Lump sum at closing Draw and repay as needed Lump sum at closing
Best for Large equity extraction; rate improvement; loan restructuring Ongoing or uncertain capital needs; short-term flexibility Accessing equity without disturbing a favorable existing loan
Availability All property types Limited; not all lenders offer on commercial Primarily agency (Fannie/Freddie) multifamily

The supplemental loan option deserves special attention for borrowers with existing Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac multifamily loans. These agency programs allow borrowers to take a second loan behind the existing first mortgage, which means you can access equity without paying off a loan that may have an attractive below-market interest rate. The supplemental loan must be originated by the same servicer as the first loan, and the combined LTV of both loans cannot exceed the agency's maximum (typically 75% to 80%). This option is not available for all property types or loan programs, but when it is, it can be more cost-effective than a full cash-out refinance, especially if the existing loan carries a steep prepayment penalty.

When a Cash-Out Refinance Makes Sense

Certain situations are particularly well suited for a cash-out refinance. Recognizing these scenarios can help you act at the right time and maximize the value of your equity.

  • Significant equity growth: If your property has appreciated substantially or you have paid down a meaningful portion of the loan balance, you may have equity that is effectively sitting idle. A cash-out refinance puts that equity to work generating additional returns.
  • Portfolio expansion opportunity: When you identify an acquisition opportunity but lack the down payment, a cash-out refinance on an existing property can provide the capital you need. This is one of the most common paths to scaling a commercial real estate portfolio.
  • Capital improvements with strong ROI: If you have identified renovations or upgrades that will increase the property's NOI by more than the cost of the additional debt service, cash-out financing creates value. Examples include unit renovations in multifamily, energy efficiency upgrades, or adding amenities that support higher rents.
  • Favorable rate environment: If current interest rates are lower than the rate on your existing loan, a cash-out refinance can simultaneously improve your rate and provide liquidity. Even if rates are slightly higher, the combination of fresh capital and a reset loan term may be worthwhile.
  • Partner return of capital: In syndication or partnership structures, refinancing to distribute equity allows sponsors to return invested capital to partners without selling the asset, which preserves long-term upside and avoids taxable events.
  • Loan maturity approaching: If your current loan is nearing maturity and you would need to refinance anyway, adding a cash-out component to the new loan is an efficient way to access equity without incurring a separate set of transaction costs.

When a Cash-Out Refinance Does Not Make Sense

Not every equity position should be tapped. There are situations where a cash-out refinance can actually harm your financial position or expose you to unnecessary risk.

  • Low equity cushion: If the cash-out transaction would push your LTV to the maximum allowed, you are left with very little equity buffer. A modest decline in property value could put you underwater (owing more than the property is worth), making it difficult to refinance again or sell without bringing cash to the table.
  • Rate increase makes payments unaffordable: If current interest rates are significantly higher than the rate on your existing loan, the combination of a larger balance and a higher rate can dramatically increase your monthly debt service. Run the numbers carefully: if the new payment pushes your DSCR below 1.20x, the property's cash flow is under stress, and a single vacancy or unexpected expense could create a shortfall.
  • Steep prepayment penalty on the existing loan: Yield maintenance and defeasance penalties on CMBS and agency loans can cost hundreds of thousands of dollars. If your existing loan has years of prepayment protection remaining, the cost of exiting may consume most or all of the cash-out benefit. In this case, a supplemental loan (if available) may be a better option.
  • No clear use for the proceeds: Extracting equity without a defined, productive use for the funds is generally unwise. You are increasing your debt obligation and paying transaction costs for cash that will sit in a bank account earning a fraction of what it costs you to borrow.
  • Declining market fundamentals: If your property's market is softening (rising vacancies, declining rents, or oversupply), taking cash out and increasing leverage amplifies your downside risk. It is safer to maintain a conservative LTV position when market conditions are uncertain.
  • Short hold period: If you plan to sell the property within the next one to two years, a cash-out refinance may not make financial sense after accounting for closing costs on the new loan and the brief time you will hold the larger balance.

Application Process and Required Documents

The application process for a commercial cash-out refinance is similar to any commercial mortgage application, with a few additional considerations related to the cash-out component. Here is a step-by-step overview of what to expect.

  1. Preliminary assessment. Before engaging lenders, evaluate your current equity position by estimating your property's market value and comparing it to your outstanding loan balance. Review your existing loan documents for prepayment provisions, as these will affect the total cost of the transaction.
  2. Lender outreach and term sheets. Contact multiple lenders or use a matching service to obtain preliminary term sheets. Provide a brief summary of the property (location, type, size, current NOI), your existing loan details (balance, rate, maturity date, prepayment terms), and the approximate amount of cash-out you are seeking. Lenders will issue indicative terms based on this information.
  3. Full application submission. Once you select a lender, submit a complete application package, which typically includes:
    • Two to three years of property operating statements (income and expenses)
    • Current rent roll with lease expiration dates
    • Two to three years of personal and/or entity tax returns
    • Personal financial statement for all guarantors
    • Organizational documents (operating agreement, articles of incorporation)
    • Existing loan documents (note, mortgage, prepayment terms)
    • Property photos and a description of any recent improvements
    • A brief explanation of how you plan to use the cash-out proceeds
  4. Third-party reports. The lender will order an appraisal (to determine current market value), an environmental assessment (Phase I, and Phase II if warranted), and a property condition report. These reports protect the lender and are standard for all commercial mortgage transactions.
  5. Underwriting and approval. The lender's underwriting team analyzes the property's cash flow, calculates the DSCR and LTV at the requested loan amount, evaluates the borrower's creditworthiness and experience, and reviews the third-party reports. If the deal meets the lender's criteria, a formal commitment letter is issued.
  6. Closing. After commitment, the loan moves to closing. Legal documents are prepared, title insurance is obtained, and the transaction is finalized. The existing loan is paid off from the new loan proceeds, and the remaining funds are disbursed to the borrower. Total processing time from application to funding typically ranges from 45 to 90 days.

Seasoning Requirements by Loan Type

Seasoning refers to the minimum length of time a borrower must have owned a property before a lender will consider a cash-out refinance. Seasoning requirements exist because lenders want to see that the property's current value reflects genuine market conditions rather than a recent, potentially inflated purchase. The rules vary by loan program, and understanding them can help you plan the timing of your refinance.

Loan Program Minimum Seasoning Appraisal Basis During Seasoning Period
Conventional Bank 12-24 months (varies) Some banks use the lower of cost or appraised value during the first 12-24 months
CMBS 12+ months Lower of cost or appraised value for the first 24 months
Fannie Mae 12 months Lower of cost or appraised value if owned less than 24 months
Freddie Mac 12 months Lower of cost or appraised value if owned less than 24 months
SBA 504 6 months (existing debt) Current appraised value; existing debt must have been for eligible purpose
Bridge / Private 0-6 months Current appraised value in most cases

The "lower of cost or appraised value" rule is particularly important for borrowers who recently purchased a property. Even if a new appraisal shows significant value appreciation, the lender may cap the LTV calculation at your original purchase price until the seasoning period has passed. This policy is designed to prevent situations where a borrower buys a property and immediately refinances at an inflated value, extracting cash that exceeds any genuine equity contribution.

For borrowers who acquired a property at a below-market price (such as through a distressed sale or off-market deal) and have made substantial improvements, the seasoning restriction can feel limiting. In these cases, a portfolio bank or private lender with more flexible policies may be willing to recognize the current appraised value earlier, though typically at a higher interest rate. Planning your refinance timeline around these seasoning windows is an important part of any cash-out strategy.
