Ground-Up Commercial Development Financing: A Complete Guide for Developers

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Loan-to-cost: 60% to 75%
  • Equity required: 25% to 40%
  • Loan type: Construction loan
  • Typical term: 18 to 36 months
  • Interest: Capitalized (interest reserve)
  • Exit strategy: Permanent loan or sale

Development Financing Overview

Ground-up commercial development is one of the most capital-intensive activities in real estate. Unlike acquiring an existing property with established cash flow, building from scratch requires a developer to assemble land, design the project, secure entitlements, manage construction, and ultimately deliver a stabilized asset before any meaningful revenue is generated. Financing this process demands a specialized loan product: the construction loan.

A construction loan is a short-term, interest-only facility designed to fund the building of a new commercial property. Unlike a permanent mortgage, which is based on the income an existing property produces, a construction loan is underwritten against the future value of the completed project and the developer's ability to execute the business plan on time and on budget. Because the lender is funding a property that does not yet exist, construction lending carries more risk than conventional commercial mortgage lending. That elevated risk is reflected in higher interest rates, lower leverage, more documentation requirements, and tighter borrower qualification standards.

In 2026, construction lending remains active across most U.S. markets, though lender appetite varies significantly by property type and geography. Industrial and multifamily projects continue to attract the broadest pool of capital, while speculative office and retail development face more scrutiny. Regardless of property type, the fundamentals of ground-up financing follow a consistent framework that every developer needs to understand before approaching a lender.

The Capital Stack Explained

The capital stack is the combination of all funding sources used to finance a development project, arranged by priority of repayment. Understanding the capital stack is essential because it determines how risk and return are distributed among the parties involved.

Senior Construction Debt

At the base of the capital stack sits the senior construction loan, which is the primary debt instrument funding the project. The senior lender holds the first lien on the property and has the highest priority claim in the event of a default. Because of this protected position, senior construction loans carry the lowest interest rates in the stack. Typical loan-to-cost (LTC) ratios for senior construction debt range from 60% to 75%, meaning the lender will fund up to 60% to 75% of total project costs. The remaining gap must be filled by other capital sources.

Mezzanine Debt and Preferred Equity

When the senior loan does not cover enough of the project cost, developers may introduce a second layer of capital through mezzanine debt or preferred equity. Mezzanine debt is a subordinate loan that sits behind the senior construction loan in repayment priority. It carries a higher interest rate (often 10% to 15%) to compensate for the increased risk of being in a junior position. Preferred equity functions similarly but is structured as an equity investment with a fixed preferred return rather than as debt. Both instruments allow developers to reduce the amount of their own cash in the deal, but they also increase total leverage and carrying costs, which adds pressure to complete and stabilize the project quickly.

Developer Equity

The top of the capital stack is the developer's own equity contribution. This is the last money to be repaid and the first money at risk if the project underperforms. Most senior construction lenders require the developer to contribute between 25% and 40% of total project costs as equity. The equity is typically funded first (sometimes called "equity in first"), meaning the developer must invest their own capital before the construction loan begins to fund. This structure protects the lender by ensuring the developer has meaningful financial exposure from day one.

Construction Loan Structure and the Draw Process

Unlike a permanent mortgage, where the full loan amount is disbursed at closing, a construction loan is funded incrementally through a series of draws. This draw process is the defining structural feature of construction lending and serves as the lender's primary tool for managing risk during the building process.

How Draws Work

After the construction loan closes, the developer submits draw requests to the lender at regular intervals (typically monthly) as construction progresses. Each draw request includes documentation of the work completed since the last draw, usually in the form of a progress report from the general contractor and an inspection report from the lender's third-party inspector. The lender's inspector physically visits the site to verify that the reported work has actually been performed and that the quality meets specifications. Only after the inspection is approved does the lender release funds for that period's work.

This structure ensures that loan proceeds are disbursed in proportion to the actual progress of construction. If the project stalls or falls behind schedule, the lender has the ability to withhold draws until the issues are resolved. Draw schedules are typically aligned with the construction budget, with specific dollar amounts allocated to each phase of the build (site work, foundation, framing, mechanical systems, interior finishes, and so on).

Retainage

Most construction loans include a retainage provision, where the lender withholds a percentage (usually 5% to 10%) of each draw until the project reaches substantial completion. Retainage serves as an additional safeguard, ensuring that funds remain available to address any deficiencies or punch-list items at the end of construction. The retained amount is released after the project passes its final inspection and all lien waivers have been collected from contractors and subcontractors.

How Lenders Evaluate Developers

Construction lenders evaluate four primary dimensions when deciding whether to finance a ground-up project: the developer's experience, the strength of pre-leasing, local market conditions, and the project budget.

Developer Experience

Track record is the single most important factor in construction loan underwriting. Lenders want to see that the developer (or the development team) has successfully completed similar projects in terms of property type, scale, and market. A developer with five completed multifamily projects in the same metro area will find it far easier to secure financing for a sixth than a first-time developer attempting the same project. Lenders review the developer's portfolio, request references from previous lenders and contractors, and evaluate the financial outcomes of past projects. Developers without a strong independent track record can overcome this hurdle by partnering with an experienced co-developer or hiring a seasoned project manager with a verifiable history of successful completions.

Pre-Leasing and Pre-Sales

Signed leases or letters of intent from future tenants dramatically reduce the lender's perception of risk. Pre-leasing demonstrates that the market has validated the project's concept, location, and pricing. For office and retail development, lenders commonly require 40% to 60% pre-leasing before approving a construction loan. Industrial projects may require less, and multifamily projects (where units are leased individually after completion) are evaluated based on market absorption data rather than signed leases. For condominiums or for-sale products, lenders look for pre-sales with executed contracts and deposits.

Market Conditions

Lenders conduct their own analysis of the local real estate market, examining vacancy rates, absorption trends, new supply in the pipeline, employment growth, and demographic patterns. A project in a market with 3% vacancy and limited new construction will receive more favorable treatment than the same project in a market with 15% vacancy and several competing developments under way. Lenders also consider macro-economic factors such as interest rate trends, construction cost inflation, and the overall trajectory of the commercial real estate cycle.

Budget Review

Every line item in the project budget is scrutinized. Lenders compare the proposed costs against industry benchmarks and recent comparable projects to verify that the budget is realistic. They look for adequate contingency reserves, reasonable soft-cost estimates, and a construction timeline that accounts for seasonal weather, permitting delays, and supply chain disruptions. An unrealistically low budget is a red flag because it suggests the developer may run out of money before the project is complete.

Hard Costs vs. Soft Costs vs. Contingency

A ground-up development budget is divided into three main categories. Understanding each category is critical for both budgeting and communicating with lenders.

Category Typical Share of Budget Examples
Hard costs 60% to 70% Site work, foundation, structural framing, roofing, mechanical/electrical/plumbing, interior finishes, landscaping, paving
Soft costs 20% to 30% Architectural and engineering fees, permits, legal fees, appraisals, environmental studies, insurance, property taxes during construction, loan interest (interest reserve), lender fees
Contingency 5% to 10% Unplanned cost overruns, material price escalation, weather delays, unforeseen site conditions, change orders

Hard costs represent the physical construction of the building itself, including all labor and materials. These are the largest component of the budget and are typically documented through a general contractor's bid or a guaranteed maximum price (GMP) contract. Soft costs cover all non-construction expenses required to bring the project to completion, including professional services, governmental fees, financing costs, and carrying expenses during the build period. Contingency is a reserve set aside to absorb unexpected costs. Lenders universally require a contingency line item, and projects without one will not be approved.

Interest Reserve Mechanics

Because a construction project does not generate income during the building period, borrowers cannot service the loan from property cash flow the way they would with a permanent mortgage. To address this, construction loans include an interest reserve: a dedicated pool of funds set aside at closing to cover interest payments during the construction period.

The interest reserve is built into the total loan amount. As the lender disburses construction draws and the outstanding loan balance increases, accrued interest is paid from the reserve rather than from the borrower's operating funds. This structure is referred to as capitalized interest because the interest expense is effectively rolled into the cost of the project rather than paid out of pocket on a monthly basis.

The size of the interest reserve is calculated based on the projected draw schedule, the loan's interest rate, and the estimated construction timeline. If construction takes longer than expected, the interest reserve may be exhausted before the project is complete, at which point the borrower must fund interest payments directly. This is one of the reasons lenders stress realistic construction timelines and adequate contingency budgets. A well-structured interest reserve typically covers the full construction period plus two to three months of additional cushion to account for delays.

Guarantor and Recourse Requirements

Nearly all construction loans for ground-up development require personal guarantees from the project's principals. This means the guarantor's personal assets (real estate, bank accounts, investment portfolios) are at risk if the project defaults and the sale of the property does not fully repay the loan. The guarantee requirement reflects the inherently speculative nature of construction lending: unlike an existing income-producing property, a project under construction has limited liquidation value until it is substantially complete.

Lenders evaluate guarantors based on net worth, liquidity, and creditworthiness. A common benchmark is that the guarantor's net worth should be at least equal to the loan amount, and liquid assets (cash and readily marketable securities) should be sufficient to cover 10% to 20% of the loan. Strong guarantors can sometimes negotiate limited or "burn-off" guarantees, where the personal recourse is reduced or eliminated once the project reaches certain milestones such as certificate of occupancy, a target occupancy rate, or a minimum debt service coverage ratio.

In the current lending environment, full recourse remains the standard for construction loans originated by banks and credit unions. Debt funds and certain non-bank lenders may offer non-recourse or limited-recourse terms, but typically at higher interest rates and lower leverage. Developers should weigh the cost of recourse against the savings in interest rate and higher available leverage when structuring the capital stack.

Lender Types for Construction Financing

Several categories of lenders participate in the ground-up construction lending market, each with distinct characteristics, advantages, and limitations.

Commercial Banks

Banks remain the primary source of construction financing for most developers. They offer the most competitive interest rates (typically SOFR plus 200 to 350 basis points) and are familiar with the draw process, inspection requirements, and guarantor structures. Bank construction loans usually require full recourse and carry strict underwriting standards around developer experience, pre-leasing, and market conditions. Approval timelines at banks can range from 45 to 90 days depending on the institution's internal processes and the complexity of the deal.

Debt Funds

Private debt funds have become an increasingly important source of construction capital, particularly for projects that do not fit neatly into bank underwriting boxes. Debt funds are more flexible on pre-leasing requirements, borrower experience, and recourse structures. They can often close faster than banks (30 to 45 days in many cases) and are willing to lend on property types or in markets that banks may avoid. The trade-off is cost: debt fund construction loans typically carry interest rates of SOFR plus 400 to 700 basis points, plus origination fees of 1% to 2%. For developers who need speed, flexibility, or higher leverage, debt funds fill a critical gap.

Credit Unions

Some larger credit unions participate in construction lending, particularly for projects in their local service areas. Credit unions can offer competitive rates (sometimes slightly below bank pricing) and tend to take a relationship-oriented approach to underwriting. However, their construction lending programs are typically smaller in scale, with loan size limits that may not accommodate larger developments. Credit unions are best suited for smaller projects (under $10 million) where the developer has an existing relationship with the institution.

Construction-to-Permanent Options

One of the most significant decisions a developer faces is whether to obtain separate construction and permanent loans or to combine them into a single construction-to-permanent (C-to-P) facility.

In a two-loan structure, the developer closes a construction loan to fund the building phase and then, upon completion, closes a separate permanent mortgage to pay off the construction loan and provide long-term financing. This approach offers maximum flexibility because the developer can shop the permanent market at the time of completion and select the most competitive terms available. The risk is that market conditions may change between the start of construction and project completion. If interest rates rise, credit markets tighten, or the property does not achieve projected occupancy, the permanent loan may be more expensive than expected, or it may not be available at all.

In a C-to-P structure, both the construction and permanent loans are originated with the same lender in a single closing. The loan functions as a construction facility during the build phase, with interest-only payments on drawn funds. Once the project meets predefined conversion conditions (typically certificate of occupancy plus a minimum occupancy or debt service coverage threshold), it automatically converts to a permanent mortgage with a fixed or adjustable rate and a standard amortization schedule. The key advantage is certainty: the developer knows the permanent loan terms before breaking ground, which eliminates refinancing risk. The disadvantage is that C-to-P loans may carry a modest rate premium, and the lender pool is smaller because the same institution must be comfortable with both the construction risk and the long-term credit profile.

For developers who prioritize certainty and simplicity, C-to-P financing is often the preferred path. For those who are confident in their ability to secure favorable permanent financing upon completion (or who plan to sell the asset rather than hold it), the two-loan structure offers more flexibility and potentially lower total costs.

Exit Strategies

Every construction loan requires a clearly defined exit strategy, and lenders evaluate this plan as part of the underwriting process. The two primary exit paths for ground-up development are permanent financing and sale.

Permanent Loan Takeout

The most common exit strategy is refinancing into a permanent mortgage once the project is stabilized. Stabilization means the property has achieved sufficient occupancy and income to qualify for long-term financing based on its debt service coverage ratio (DSCR). Permanent loan options include conventional bank mortgages, CMBS loans, life insurance company loans, and agency financing (for multifamily properties). The developer retains ownership and benefits from ongoing cash flow, tax advantages, and long-term appreciation.

Sale

Some developers build with the intention of selling the completed, stabilized asset to an investor. This strategy is common in markets with strong institutional demand and for property types (such as Class A multifamily or net-leased industrial) where buyer pools are deep. The developer's profit comes from the spread between total development cost and the sale price. Lenders view a sale exit favorably when the developer can demonstrate a realistic valuation supported by comparable transactions and current market conditions.

Hybrid Approach

Experienced developers often structure their projects with optionality, positioning the asset for either a permanent loan takeout or a sale depending on which path offers the best return at the time of completion. This flexibility requires conservative underwriting assumptions and a capital structure that works under both scenarios.

Common Pitfalls in Ground-Up Development

Even experienced developers encounter challenges that can derail a ground-up project. The most common pitfalls include the following.

  • Underestimating costs. Optimistic budgets are the leading cause of construction loan distress. Material prices, labor shortages, and supply chain delays can push costs well beyond initial estimates. Developers who rely on preliminary bids rather than executed contracts are especially vulnerable.
  • Inadequate contingency. Projects without sufficient contingency reserves have no buffer for the unexpected. Lenders typically require 5% to 10% contingency, but experienced developers know that actual cost overruns can exceed this range on complex projects.
  • Timeline overruns. Delays in permitting, weather-related stoppages, labor shortages, and inspection failures all push the completion date further out. Every month of delay increases carrying costs (interest, property taxes, insurance) and can exhaust the interest reserve before the project is finished.
  • Market timing risk. A project that takes 24 to 30 months to build and stabilize is exposed to changes in market conditions between groundbreaking and delivery. Rising vacancy rates, new competing supply, or an economic downturn can reduce achievable rents and property values below the assumptions used to underwrite the deal.
  • Contractor disputes. Disagreements with the general contractor over scope, quality, or payment can bring construction to a halt. Developers should invest in thorough contractor vetting, detailed contracts, and clear dispute resolution mechanisms before breaking ground.
  • Entitlement and permitting delays. Zoning approvals, environmental clearances, and building permits can take months longer than expected, particularly in jurisdictions with complex regulatory requirements or active community opposition. Delays at this stage burn through pre-development capital and may trigger expiration of rate locks or financing commitments.
  • Over-leveraging the capital stack. Using excessive mezzanine debt or preferred equity to reduce the developer's cash contribution can work well when the project performs as planned. If the project underperforms, however, the high cost of subordinate capital can quickly erode returns and create cash flow pressure that threatens the entire investment.

Development Timeline Overview

A typical ground-up commercial development project moves through several distinct phases, each with its own timeline and capital requirements.

  1. Pre-development (3 to 12 months). This phase includes site identification and acquisition, market research and feasibility analysis, architectural design and engineering, environmental due diligence, and the entitlement and permitting process. Most of the spending during this period falls into the soft-cost category, funded by the developer's own capital or a pre-development loan.
  2. Financing (1 to 3 months). Once the project is fully entitled and permits are in hand, the developer approaches lenders to secure the construction loan. The loan application, underwriting, and closing process typically takes 30 to 90 days depending on the lender and project complexity.
  3. Construction (12 to 24 months). The building phase begins after loan closing and issuance of a notice to proceed to the general contractor. Duration varies widely based on the size and complexity of the project, the property type, and local conditions. A 150-unit garden-style apartment complex might take 14 to 18 months, while a 10-story mixed-use tower could require 20 to 24 months or more.
  4. Lease-up and stabilization (3 to 12 months). After the project receives its certificate of occupancy, the developer (or a hired leasing team) works to fill the building with tenants. Stabilization is typically defined as reaching 90% to 95% occupancy, which triggers the ability to refinance into permanent financing or market the property for sale.
  5. Exit (1 to 3 months). The final phase involves either closing the permanent mortgage to pay off the construction loan or completing a sale transaction to a buyer. Most construction loans include extension options (typically two six-month extensions) to accommodate lease-up and exit timing.

From initial site acquisition through permanent financing or sale, the total development cycle for a typical commercial project runs 24 to 48 months. Developers should plan their capital, partnerships, and personal commitments with this extended timeline in mind. The most successful developers are those who build conservative assumptions into every phase of the process and maintain sufficient reserves to absorb the unexpected.

Frequently Asked Questions

Most construction lenders require between 25% and 40% equity for ground-up commercial development projects. The exact amount depends on several factors, including the property type, the developer's track record, local market conditions, and the lender's risk appetite. A well-capitalized developer with multiple successful completions and strong pre-leasing may negotiate closer to the 25% threshold, while a first-time developer or a project in an untested submarket will likely need 35% to 40%.

Equity can come from the developer's own cash, partner capital, or in some cases land equity if the site is owned free and clear. Mezzanine debt or preferred equity can also fill part of the capital stack, though senior lenders may place limits on total leverage. Regardless of the source, lenders want to see that the developer has meaningful "skin in the game" to ensure their interests are aligned throughout the project.

Non-recourse construction loans are rare but not impossible. Most banks and credit unions require full personal recourse on construction loans because of the elevated risk involved in building a project from the ground up. The property does not yet exist as a stabilized asset, which limits the lender's ability to recover its investment through foreclosure alone.

However, certain debt funds, insurance company lenders, and CMBS-adjacent programs offer non-recourse or limited-recourse construction financing for experienced developers with strong project fundamentals. In a limited-recourse structure, the guarantee may burn off once the project reaches certain milestones such as certificate of occupancy or a minimum occupancy threshold. To qualify for non-recourse terms, expect to contribute more equity (often 35% to 40%), demonstrate a strong development track record, and present substantial pre-leasing or pre-sales.

Even in non-recourse deals, lenders typically carve out specific "bad boy" acts (such as fraud, environmental contamination, or unauthorized transfers) that trigger full personal recourse. The pricing on non-recourse construction loans is also typically higher than recourse alternatives, so developers should weigh the trade-offs carefully.

Cost overruns are one of the most common risks in ground-up development, and lenders plan for them by requiring a contingency reserve (typically 5% to 10% of total project costs) in the initial budget. This reserve is specifically designed to absorb unexpected expenses such as material price increases, unforeseen site conditions, or change orders during construction.

If costs exceed even the contingency reserve, the developer is generally responsible for funding the shortfall out of pocket. The construction lender will not increase the loan amount simply because costs have risen. In severe cases, a cost overrun can trigger a default under the loan agreement if the developer cannot demonstrate the ability to complete the project. This is why lenders carefully evaluate the guarantor's liquidity and net worth: they need confidence that the borrower has the financial capacity to cover overages.

To protect against this risk, developers should obtain fixed-price or guaranteed-maximum-price contracts from their general contractor, conduct thorough due diligence on material and labor costs before breaking ground, and maintain a personal liquidity cushion beyond the required equity contribution.

Construction-to-permanent (C-to-P) financing combines a construction loan and a permanent mortgage into a single closing. During the construction phase, the loan functions like a standard construction facility with interest-only payments on drawn funds. The lender disburses proceeds through the normal draw process as construction progresses.

Once the project is completed and meets certain conditions (such as achieving a certificate of occupancy or a minimum occupancy level), the loan automatically converts into a permanent mortgage with a fixed or adjustable rate and a standard amortization schedule. There is no need to apply for a separate loan, pay a second round of closing costs, or worry about qualifying for permanent financing in a potentially different market environment.

The primary advantage of C-to-P financing is that it eliminates refinancing risk and locks in permanent loan terms before the project begins. The trade-off is that C-to-P loans may carry slightly higher rates than if the developer sourced construction and permanent financing separately, and the lender pool is smaller since the same institution must be comfortable with both the construction risk and the long-term credit profile. C-to-P is most popular among developers who prioritize certainty over maximum cost optimization.

Pre-leasing requirements vary by lender and property type. For speculative office or retail development, many banks require 40% to 60% pre-leasing before they will fund a construction loan. This means signed leases or binding letters of intent covering that percentage of the building's rentable square footage must be in place before closing. Industrial and warehouse projects, which have seen sustained demand in recent years, may face lower thresholds of 20% to 30%, or in some cases none at all.

Multifamily construction loans generally do not require pre-leasing because apartment units are leased individually after completion. Instead, lenders scrutinize the local rental market, analyzing vacancy rates, absorption trends, and competing supply to assess lease-up risk. For condominiums or for-sale projects, lenders look for pre-sales with executed contracts and deposits.

Debt funds and private lenders are often more flexible on pre-leasing than traditional banks, though they compensate for that flexibility with higher interest rates. Even when a lender does not mandate pre-leasing, having signed letters of intent or executed leases in hand significantly strengthens the loan application and may lead to better pricing, higher leverage, or more favorable recourse terms.
