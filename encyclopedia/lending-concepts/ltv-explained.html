Loan-to-Value (LTV) Ratio in Commercial Real Estate: What Borrowers Need to Know

LTV is one of the two primary metrics that determine how much you can borrow on a commercial property. Understanding how lenders calculate and apply LTV will help you structure stronger deals and negotiate better terms.

Key Facts at a Glance

Definition Loan amount as percentage of property value
Typical CRE Range 60% to 80%
How Calculated Loan Amount / Appraised Value x 100
Why It Matters Determines down payment and lender risk
Max LTV by Type SBA up to 90%, CMBS 65-75%
Relationship Higher LTV = higher rate, more risk

What LTV Means in Commercial Lending

Loan-to-Value ratio (LTV) measures the percentage of a property's value that is financed by debt. If you purchase a $2,000,000 property and borrow $1,500,000, your LTV is 75%. That means you are putting 25% down as equity, and the lender is financing the remaining 75%.

LTV is one of the foundational metrics for loan sizing in commercial real estate. Along with Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) and debt yield, it determines how large a loan a lender will offer on any given property. In most transactions, the lender calculates the maximum loan amount using each of these three metrics and then offers the borrower the lowest result. This approach, sometimes called the "binding constraint" method, ensures the loan is appropriately sized from every angle.

For borrowers, LTV directly translates to one question: how much cash do I need to bring to the table? A 75% LTV means a 25% down payment. A 65% LTV means 35% down. The difference between these two numbers on a $3,000,000 property is $300,000 in additional equity required. Understanding what drives LTV limits, and how to influence them, can save you significant capital on every deal.

How to Calculate LTV

The formula is straightforward:

LTV Formula

LTV = (Loan Amount / Appraised Value) x 100

Worked Example

Consider a borrower purchasing a retail strip center:

  • Purchase Price: $2,000,000
  • Appraised Value: $2,000,000
  • Loan Amount: $1,500,000
  • Down Payment: $500,000
LTV Calculation

LTV = $1,500,000 / $2,000,000 x 100 = 75%

This 75% LTV falls within the acceptable range for most conventional commercial lenders and many CMBS programs.

The "Lower Of" Rule for Acquisitions

When financing a purchase, lenders use the lower of the purchase price or appraised value to calculate LTV. This is a critical detail that catches many first-time borrowers off guard. If you negotiate a purchase at $1,800,000 but the appraisal comes back at $2,000,000, the lender will use $1,800,000 as the value for LTV purposes. You do not get credit for the "instant equity" created by buying below appraised value.

Conversely, if the appraisal comes in lower than your purchase price, the lender will use the appraisal figure. If you agreed to pay $2,000,000 but the property appraises at $1,850,000, a 75% LTV loan would be based on $1,850,000, limiting you to a $1,387,500 loan instead of $1,500,000. You would need to bring an additional $112,500 in equity or renegotiate the purchase price.

For refinances, the lender uses the appraised value since there is no purchase price to compare. This is one reason why refinancing after property improvements can be so advantageous: a higher appraised value directly increases the available loan amount. LTV Requirements by Loan Type

Maximum LTV varies significantly across loan programs. The table below shows typical ranges for the most common commercial real estate loan types:

Loan Type Typical Max LTV Notes
SBA 504 Up to 90% Owner-occupied only; CDC provides second lien
SBA 7(a) 85% to 90% Owner-occupied; flexible use of proceeds
Bank / Conventional 70% to 80% Varies by bank, property type, and borrower strength
CMBS 65% to 75% Non-recourse; constrained by DSCR and debt yield
Agency (Fannie/Freddie) 75% to 80% Multifamily only; higher LTV for affordable housing
HUD/FHA Up to 83% to 85% Multifamily; long processing time but excellent terms
Bridge 65% to 80% (as-is), up to 90% LTC Short-term; sized on value-add business plan
Life Company 60% to 65% Most conservative; lowest rates in exchange

These ranges are general guidelines. Actual maximum LTV will depend on the specific lender's appetite, the borrower's financial strength, the property's condition and market, and current economic conditions. In tighter credit environments, lenders may reduce their maximum LTV by 5% to 10% across the board.

Notice the tradeoff pattern: loan programs that offer higher LTV (such as SBA 504) come with other requirements like owner-occupancy or business use mandates. Programs with the lowest LTV limits (life companies) typically offer the best rates and terms. Borrowers must weigh leverage against cost and flexibility.

How Appraisals Determine the "V" in LTV

The "value" in Loan-to-Value comes from a professional appraisal, and the appraisal methodology directly impacts how much you can borrow. Commercial appraisers use three approaches to value, though not all three carry equal weight for every property type.

The Three Appraisal Approaches

  • Income Approach (most important for commercial): This method capitalizes the property's Net Operating Income (NOI) at an appropriate capitalization rate to derive value. For example, a property with $200,000 NOI in a market where similar properties trade at a 7% cap rate would be valued at approximately $2,857,000 ($200,000 / 0.07). Since most commercial properties are purchased for their income-generating potential, the income approach is typically the primary valuation method and carries the most weight in the final opinion of value.
  • Sales Comparison Approach: This method compares the subject property to recently sold comparable properties, making adjustments for differences in size, condition, location, and amenities. It works best for property types with frequent sales activity (such as small multifamily or owner-occupied commercial) and is less reliable for unique or specialized properties.
  • Cost Approach: This method estimates the cost to rebuild the property from scratch, minus depreciation, plus land value. It is most useful for newer construction, special-purpose properties, and situations where comparable sales and income data are limited.

How Borrowers Can Prepare for Appraisals

Since the appraisal determines the "V" in LTV, borrowers have a direct interest in ensuring the appraiser has complete and accurate information:

  • Provide a current rent roll showing all tenants, lease terms, and rental rates
  • Submit trailing 12-month (T12) income and expense statements
  • Share information about recent capital improvements or renovations
  • Provide copies of signed leases, especially for new or recently renewed tenants
  • Document any above-market lease terms or rent escalation clauses
  • Identify comparable sales that support the expected value

A well-prepared appraisal package does not guarantee a higher value, but it does ensure the appraiser has everything needed to produce an accurate and well-supported opinion.

LTV vs. LTC for Construction and Value-Add

For stabilized acquisitions and refinances, LTV is the standard leverage metric. But for construction projects and heavy renovations, lenders shift to Loan-to-Cost (LTC), which measures the loan against the total project cost rather than the current property value.

LTC Formula

LTC = (Loan Amount / Total Project Cost) x 100

Why LTC Matters for Value-Add Projects

Consider a borrower who purchases a vacant office building for $1,000,000 and plans to invest $500,000 in renovations to convert it into creative office space. The total project cost is $1,500,000. After renovation, the stabilized value is projected at $2,200,000.

  • If a lender uses 75% LTV based on as-is value: Maximum loan = $750,000 (75% of $1,000,000)
  • If a lender uses 80% LTC based on total cost: Maximum loan = $1,200,000 (80% of $1,500,000)
  • If a lender uses 70% of after-renovation value (ARV): Maximum loan = $1,540,000 (70% of $2,200,000)

Bridge lenders and construction lenders commonly use LTC as their primary sizing metric, often paired with LTV on the stabilized (after-renovation) value as a secondary check. The concept of "cost basis" is central here. Lenders want to ensure that even in a worst-case scenario, the total loan amount does not exceed what the project cost to build or renovate, providing a natural floor for recovery.

Typical LTC limits range from 75% to 85% for construction loans and 80% to 90% for bridge loans with value-add business plans. These limits are higher than standard LTV because the lender is underwriting the future stabilized value, not just the current condition.

Impact of LTV on Interest Rate and Terms

LTV and interest rate have a direct, predictable relationship: higher LTV means higher rates. Lenders charge a risk premium for higher-leverage loans because their exposure increases while the borrower's equity cushion decreases.

How the Risk Premium Works

Most lenders price loans in LTV tiers. A simplified rate sheet might look like this:

LTV Tier Rate Spread Over Index Rate Impact
Below 55% +150 bps Best available pricing
55% to 60% +165 bps Still highly competitive
60% to 65% +185 bps Standard good pricing
65% to 70% +210 bps Moderate risk premium
70% to 75% +240 bps Noticeable premium increase
Above 75% +280 bps or higher Significant premium; fewer lenders

On a $2,000,000 loan, the difference between 65% LTV and 75% LTV pricing could mean an additional 30 to 55 basis points on the interest rate. Over a 10-year term, that adds up to tens of thousands of dollars in additional interest expense.

Terms Beyond Rate

LTV also affects other loan terms beyond just the interest rate:

  • Interest-only availability: Many lenders offer interest-only (IO) periods for lower-LTV loans. At 60% LTV, you might qualify for 3 to 5 years of IO. At 75% LTV, full amortization from day one is more common.
  • Recourse requirements: Non-recourse loans (where the borrower is not personally liable) are more available at lower LTV levels. Higher LTV often triggers full or partial recourse guarantees.
  • Prepayment flexibility: Some lenders offer more flexible prepayment terms at lower LTV since their risk is reduced.
  • Reserve requirements: Higher LTV loans may require larger escrow reserves for taxes, insurance, and capital improvements.

Strategies to Improve Your LTV Position

If your target loan exceeds the lender's LTV limit, or if you want to qualify for better pricing, here are proven strategies to improve your leverage position:

  1. Increase your down payment: The most direct approach. Every additional dollar of equity reduces LTV. If 75% LTV is the limit and you are at 80%, bringing an extra 5% of the property value in cash solves the problem.
  2. Complete value-add improvements before refinancing: Renovating units, improving curb appeal, stabilizing occupancy, and raising rents all increase property value. A higher appraised value on refinance means lower LTV on the same loan amount, or a larger loan at the same LTV.
  3. Cross-collateralize with other properties: Some lenders will consider additional properties as collateral, effectively lowering the combined LTV across the portfolio. This works best with community banks and relationship lenders.
  4. Add mezzanine financing or preferred equity: Rather than increasing the senior mortgage, layer subordinate capital behind it. The senior lender sees a lower LTV on their piece, while your total leverage is higher. (See the section on Combined LTV below.)
  5. Use SBA programs for owner-occupied properties: If you occupy 51% or more of the property, SBA 504 and SBA 7(a) loans allow up to 85% to 90% LTV, far exceeding what conventional lenders offer.
  6. Challenge the appraisal: If the appraised value comes in lower than expected, you may be able to provide additional comparable sales data or correct factual errors in the report. Some lenders allow a second appraisal or a reconsideration of value.
  7. Bring in an equity partner: A joint venture partner who contributes equity reduces the amount you need to borrow, lowering LTV. This dilutes your ownership but can make an otherwise impossible deal work.
  8. Negotiate seller financing for part of the equity: A seller willing to carry a second lien can reduce the cash equity you need. However, many senior lenders restrict or prohibit subordinate debt, so this must be structured carefully and disclosed to all parties.

Combined LTV (CLTV) with Mezzanine Financing

In many commercial real estate transactions, the borrower uses more than one layer of debt. The senior mortgage might provide 65% LTV, while a mezzanine loan adds another 15% to 20%, bringing total leverage to 80% to 85%. This total leverage is measured by Combined Loan-to-Value (CLTV).

CLTV Formula

CLTV = (Senior Loan + Mezzanine Loan) / Appraised Value x 100

How CLTV Works in Practice

Consider a $10,000,000 office acquisition:

  • Senior mortgage: $6,500,000 (65% LTV)
  • Mezzanine loan: $2,000,000 (20% of value)
  • Borrower equity: $1,500,000 (15% of value)
  • CLTV: 85% ($8,500,000 / $10,000,000)

From the senior lender's perspective, their exposure is only 65% LTV, which is comfortable. The mezzanine lender takes on additional risk in the 65% to 85% range of the capital stack, and they charge accordingly. Mezzanine rates typically run 10% to 15% or higher, reflecting their subordinate position.

What Senior Lenders Think About CLTV

Most senior lenders have explicit policies regarding subordinate debt:

  • CMBS lenders: Generally prohibit additional debt on the property. If mezzanine is desired, it must be structured as a separate equity interest in the borrowing entity, and CMBS loan documents typically cap total leverage at 80% to 85% CLTV.
  • Agency lenders (Fannie/Freddie): Typically do not allow mezzanine or subordinate financing.
  • Bank lenders: Some banks allow subordinate debt on a case-by-case basis, especially if the total CLTV remains at 80% or below.
  • Life company lenders: Rarely permit additional leverage beyond their senior mortgage.

The key consideration is that while mezzanine financing reduces your cash equity requirement, it increases your total debt service obligation. Lenders will often stress-test the deal at the CLTV level to ensure the property can service all layers of debt with an adequate cushion. The total cost of capital also rises, since the blended rate across senior and mezzanine debt will be higher than a single, higher-LTV first mortgage.
