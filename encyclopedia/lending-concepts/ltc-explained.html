Loan-to-Cost (LTC) Ratio: How Construction and Development Loans Are Sized

The Loan-to-Cost ratio is the primary metric lenders use to size construction and development loans. Understanding how LTC works, what costs are included, and how it differs from LTV will help you structure your project for maximum financing.

Key Facts at a Glance

Definition Loan amount as percentage of total project cost
Typical Range 60% to 80%
Primary Use Construction and development loans
Formula Loan Amount / Total Project Cost x 100
Difference from LTV Uses cost basis, not appraised value
Cost Components Land + hard costs + soft costs

What LTC Means and When It Applies

Loan-to-Cost (LTC) measures the percentage of a project's total cost that the lender is willing to finance. If a development project costs $10 million and the lender provides a $7.5 million loan, the LTC ratio is 75%. The remaining 25% must come from the borrower's equity.

LTC is the primary loan sizing metric for construction loans, ground-up development projects, and heavy value-add renovations. It applies whenever the property does not yet have stabilized income that can be measured with traditional metrics like Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR). Because a building under construction generates no rental income, lenders cannot underwrite based on cash flow. Instead, they evaluate how much of the total investment they are willing to fund.

Lenders prefer LTC over Loan-to-Value (LTV) for projects under construction because cost is a known, verifiable number. The total project budget is documented in detail before closing, with every line item reviewed. Appraised value, by contrast, is an estimate of what the property will be worth after completion. That estimate involves assumptions about rental rates, occupancy, cap rates, and market conditions that may change during a 12- to 24-month construction period. By anchoring the loan to cost rather than projected value, the lender limits its exposure to a concrete figure.

That said, most construction lenders evaluate both LTC and the projected LTV at completion. The loan must satisfy both constraints. For example, a lender might cap the loan at 80% LTC and 65% of the projected completed value, whichever produces the smaller loan amount.

How to Calculate LTC

The LTC formula is straightforward:

LTC Formula

LTC = Loan Amount / Total Project Cost x 100

The critical step is defining "Total Project Cost" correctly. Project cost consists of three main categories:

1. Land Cost

This is the acquisition price of the land or, if the borrower already owns the land, its appraised or contributed value. If a developer purchased a parcel two years ago for $2 million and it is now appraised at $3 million, the lender may use either figure depending on their policy. Some lenders use the lower of cost or appraised value, while others credit the borrower with the current appraised value. Land equity is an important consideration because it can reduce or even eliminate the cash equity a borrower must invest.

2. Hard Costs

Hard costs are the direct expenses of physical construction. These include:

  • General contractor fees and labor
  • Building materials (concrete, steel, lumber, roofing, etc.)
  • Site work (excavation, grading, utilities)
  • Mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems
  • Permits and impact fees
  • Landscaping and exterior improvements

Hard costs typically represent 60% to 70% of total project cost and are the largest single component. They are documented in a detailed construction budget prepared by the general contractor and reviewed by the lender's third-party cost consultant.

3. Soft Costs

Soft costs cover everything outside of physical construction:

  • Architecture and design fees
  • Engineering (structural, civil, geotechnical)
  • Legal fees for entity formation, contracts, and closings
  • Lender fees (origination, appraisal, inspection, legal)
  • Interest reserve (capitalized interest during construction)
  • Insurance during construction (builder's risk policy)
  • Environmental studies and surveys
  • Accounting and project management
  • Marketing and lease-up costs
  • Contingency reserve

Soft costs typically represent 15% to 25% of total project cost. The interest reserve is often the single largest soft cost item, as it funds the loan's interest payments during the construction period when the property produces no income.

Worked Example

Consider a developer planning a 60-unit apartment building:

  • Land acquisition: $3,000,000
  • Hard costs (construction): $12,000,000
  • Soft costs (design, legal, financing, interest reserve, contingency): $3,000,000
  • Total Project Cost: $18,000,000

The lender offers 80% LTC:

LTC Calculation

$18,000,000 x 80% = $14,400,000 loan amount

The developer must contribute $3,600,000 in equity (20% of total project cost). If the developer already owns the land free and clear, the $3,000,000 land value counts toward that equity requirement, leaving only $600,000 in additional cash equity needed.

LTC vs LTV: When Each Metric Matters

LTC and LTV are related but serve different purposes. Understanding when each applies is essential for structuring your financing correctly.

Metric Best Used For Based On Key Advantage
LTV (Loan-to-Value) Stabilized acquisitions, refinances Appraised value Reflects market value
LTC (Loan-to-Cost) Construction, development, heavy renovation Total project cost Reflects actual investment

The key distinction is that a property's post-completion appraised value often exceeds its total development cost. That difference represents the developer's anticipated profit. For example, a project that costs $18 million to build might appraise at $24 million upon completion. An 80% LTC loan of $14.4 million represents only 60% of the completed appraised value ($14.4M / $24M = 60% LTV).

This gap between cost and value is precisely why lenders prefer LTC for construction financing. It is a more conservative measure that protects the lender during the riskiest phase of the project. If something goes wrong during construction (cost overruns, delays, market softening), the lender's exposure is tied to what was actually spent, not to a theoretical future value that may never materialize.

Once construction is complete and the property is stabilized with tenants and income, financing shifts to LTV-based metrics. The permanent loan or refinance that replaces the construction loan will be sized based on the property's appraised value and its DSCR.

Typical LTC Maximums by Lender Type

LTC limits vary significantly depending on the lender type, property type, and project risk profile. The following table provides general ranges:

Lender Type / Scenario Typical LTC Range Notes
Bank Construction Loan 70% to 80% Recourse; requires personal guarantee
SBA 504 (Owner-Occupied) Up to 90% Includes CDC and bank portions combined
Private / Bridge Construction 80% to 90% Higher rates; faster closing; flexible terms
Life Company 60% to 65% Most conservative; lowest rates; select projects only
Ground-Up Development (Bank) 65% to 75% Higher risk due to entitlement and construction exposure
Heavy Renovation 75% to 85% Existing structure reduces risk vs. ground-up

Several factors influence where a specific project falls within these ranges. Borrower experience is paramount: a developer with a strong track record of similar completed projects will typically receive higher LTC than a first-time developer. Pre-leasing or pre-sales also matter. A project with 40% of its units pre-leased before construction begins demonstrates market demand and may qualify for higher leverage. Location quality, property type, and the strength of the general contractor all play a role as well.

How Lenders Verify Costs: Budget Review and Draw Schedules

Construction loans are fundamentally different from permanent loans in how funds are disbursed. Rather than funding the entire loan amount at closing, the lender releases money in stages called "draws" as construction progresses. This process protects the lender by ensuring that loan funds are used for their intended purpose and that the project is being built according to plan.

Pre-Closing Budget Review

Before approving the loan, the lender conducts a thorough review of the construction budget. This typically involves hiring a third-party cost consultant (sometimes called a construction monitor or cost reviewer) who evaluates whether the budget is realistic and complete. The cost consultant compares the proposed budget to market rates for similar construction in the area and flags any line items that appear too low or too high.

The budget must account for every dollar of project cost, from land acquisition through lease-up. Lenders require detailed line-item breakdowns, not just summary totals. A typical construction budget might have 50 to 100 individual line items covering everything from demolition to final landscaping.

The Draw Process

Once construction begins, the borrower submits draw requests (typically monthly) as work is completed. Each draw request includes:

  • A detailed schedule of values showing work completed to date
  • Invoices from subcontractors and suppliers
  • Lien waivers from contractors who have been paid
  • Updated project timeline and completion estimates

Before releasing funds, the lender sends an inspector to the site to verify that the work described in the draw request has actually been completed. The inspector compares the physical progress against the budget and the draw request. Only after the inspection confirms the work is the draw approved and funded.

Contingency Requirements

Lenders universally require a contingency line item in the construction budget, typically ranging from 5% to 10% of hard costs. This contingency serves as a buffer against unexpected cost increases, change orders, and unforeseen conditions (such as discovering unsuitable soil or hidden environmental contamination). The contingency is included in the total project cost for LTC calculation purposes but is only drawn upon if actual costs exceed the original budget line items. If construction is completed under budget, unused contingency funds are not disbursed.

Impact on Equity Requirement

The LTC ratio directly determines how much equity the borrower must contribute to the project. The formula is simple:

Equity Requirement

Required Equity = Total Project Cost x (100% - LTC)

At 75% LTC on an $18 million project, the borrower needs $4.5 million in equity. At 85% LTC, that drops to $2.7 million. The difference is significant, and it explains why developers actively seek the highest LTC available. Less equity per deal means the developer can pursue more projects simultaneously or preserve capital for contingencies.

Forms of Equity Lenders Accept

Not all equity must come as cash. Lenders may accept several forms of equity contribution:

  • Cash equity: Direct cash investment by the borrower or equity partners. This is the most straightforward form and is always accepted.
  • Land equity: If the borrower already owns the land, its value (or a portion of it) can count toward the equity requirement. For example, if a developer owns land appraised at $3 million and total project cost is $18 million, the land equity of $3 million satisfies most or all of the equity requirement at 80% LTC (where $3.6 million is needed).
  • Sweat equity: Some lenders, particularly those working with owner-operators, may give partial credit for the developer's own labor or management. This is less common and typically limited to a small percentage of total equity.
  • Third-party equity: Equity from joint venture partners, limited partners, or preferred equity investors counts toward the requirement. The lender will want to understand the capital stack and the terms of any equity investments that sit between the senior loan and the developer's ownership.

Lender policies vary on how they value non-cash equity. Some lenders require a minimum percentage of the equity to be cash (often called "cash-in-deal" requirements), even if the borrower has sufficient land equity. Others are flexible as long as the total equity meets or exceeds the LTC threshold.

LTC in Value-Add vs Ground-Up Scenarios

The LTC concept applies to both value-add renovations and ground-up construction, but the calculation and risk profile differ in important ways.

Value-Add Projects

In a value-add scenario, the borrower acquires an existing property and renovates it to increase its income. Total project cost in this case equals the purchase price plus renovation costs (both hard and soft). For example:

  • Purchase price: $8,000,000
  • Renovation hard costs: $3,000,000
  • Renovation soft costs: $1,000,000
  • Total project cost: $12,000,000

At 80% LTC, the lender provides $9,600,000. This loan covers both the acquisition and renovation, often structured as a single bridge loan with a renovation holdback. The acquisition portion funds at closing, while the renovation portion is disbursed through draws as work is completed.

Value-add projects typically qualify for higher LTC ratios (75% to 85%) because the existing structure reduces construction risk. There is no entitlement risk, no foundation or structural uncertainty, and the renovation timeline is generally shorter than a ground-up build. The property may also generate some income during renovation, providing an additional margin of safety.

Ground-Up Development

Ground-up development starts with raw land (or a site requiring demolition) and builds from scratch. Total project cost includes land, all construction costs, and all soft costs from initial design through lease-up. These projects carry higher risk due to longer timelines, greater construction complexity, entitlement and permitting uncertainties, and zero income during the building phase.

Because of this elevated risk, lenders typically cap ground-up LTC at lower levels (65% to 75% for banks). The developer needs more equity, which aligns the developer's financial interest with the project's success. If construction costs run over budget or the market softens, the developer has significant capital at risk, motivating them to manage the project carefully.

Some lenders differentiate further based on the stage of entitlement. A fully entitled site with approved plans and building permits is less risky than a site still going through the approval process. Pre-entitled projects may qualify for higher LTC than those where zoning approval is still pending.

How Lenders View Each Scenario

From the lender's perspective, the key question is how much can go wrong and how quickly they can recover their capital if it does. Value-add projects offer faster recovery because the existing property has some baseline value even if the renovation fails. Ground-up projects have more downside risk because a partially completed building has limited value. This risk differential is reflected in both the LTC maximums and the interest rates lenders charge for each scenario.
