The Capital Stack in Commercial Real Estate: Senior Debt, Mezzanine, and Equity Layers

The capital stack defines how every dollar in a commercial real estate deal is sourced, structured, and prioritized. Understanding each layer of the stack is essential for borrowers, investors, and developers who want to optimize leverage, manage risk, and negotiate effectively with capital providers.

Key Facts at a Glance

Definition The layered structure of debt and equity funding a deal
Primary Layers Senior debt, mezzanine, preferred equity, common equity
Senior Debt Portion Typically 50% to 75% of total capitalization
Risk Direction Risk increases from bottom (debt) to top (equity)
Return Direction Expected returns increase from bottom to top
Key Document Intercreditor agreement governs layer priorities

What the Capital Stack Is

The capital stack is a framework that represents every source of funding used to acquire, develop, or refinance a commercial real estate asset. Think of it as a vertical diagram where each layer represents a different class of capital, organized by repayment priority and risk level. The bottom holds the most senior, lowest-risk capital. The top holds the most junior, highest-risk capital.

Every commercial real estate deal has a capital stack, even if the borrower does not think of it in those terms. A simple acquisition financed with a 70% LTV bank loan and 30% borrower equity has a two-layer stack. A development project funded by a construction loan, mezzanine debt, preferred equity, and sponsor equity has four layers. The number of layers, their sizes, and their terms define the deal's risk profile, cost of capital, and return potential for each participant.

Understanding the capital stack matters because it directly determines three things: who gets paid and in what order when cash flow is distributed, who absorbs losses first if the property underperforms, and what return each participant can expect relative to the risk they are taking.

Layers of the Capital Stack

The capital stack in commercial real estate typically consists of four main layers. Each layer has distinct characteristics regarding security, repayment priority, risk exposure, and return expectations.

Senior Debt

Senior debt sits at the base of the capital stack and holds the highest priority for repayment. This is the first mortgage on the property, secured by a lien on the real estate itself. In the event of default, the senior lender can foreclose on the property and recover their investment before any other capital provider receives a dollar. Because of this protected position, senior debt carries the lowest risk and the lowest cost of capital.

Senior lenders are typically banks, life insurance companies, CMBS conduits, or government-sponsored agencies like Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. They generally provide 50% to 75% of the total capitalization. Senior debt terms include fixed or floating interest rates, amortization schedules, maturity dates, and covenants. The interest rate on senior debt in a typical commercial deal ranges from 5% to 8%, depending on market conditions and the specific loan program.

Subordinate (Mezzanine) Debt

Mezzanine debt occupies the layer directly above the senior mortgage. Unlike senior debt, mezzanine financing is not secured by a lien on the property itself. Instead, it is secured by a pledge of the borrower's ownership interest in the entity that owns the property. The mezzanine lender's foreclosure remedy involves taking over ownership of the borrowing entity rather than foreclosing on the real estate directly.

Mezzanine debt fills the gap between what the senior lender will provide and the total capital needed, minus the sponsor's equity. For example, if a $10,000,000 acquisition has a senior loan at 65% LTV ($6,500,000) and the sponsor contributes 15% equity ($1,500,000), a mezzanine loan of $2,000,000 fills the remaining 20%. Mezzanine lenders charge significantly higher rates, typically 10% to 15% or more, reflecting their subordinate position. Learn more in our mezzanine loans guide.

Preferred Equity

Preferred equity sits above mezzanine debt and below common equity in the stack. Preferred equity investors contribute capital in exchange for a priority return that must be paid before common equity holders receive any distributions. Unlike debt, preferred equity does not create a lending relationship. There is no promissory note, no lien, and no traditional foreclosure remedy. The preferred equity holder's rights are governed by the operating agreement of the property-owning entity.

Preferred equity returns typically range from 10% to 18%, depending on the deal's risk profile. The return is usually structured as a fixed preferred return plus a potential participation in upside profits. If the project underperforms, the preferred equity investor absorbs losses after all debt has been satisfied but before the common equity sponsor loses their investment. Many institutional investors and family offices participate at this level because it offers a defined return with debt protection below it.

Common Equity

Common equity sits at the top of the capital stack. This is the sponsor's (and their equity partners') investment in the deal. Common equity holders are the last to receive distributions and the first to absorb losses. If the property's value declines, every dollar of loss reduces common equity before touching any other layer. If the property outperforms, common equity captures all upside above what is owed to other layers.

Because of this risk profile, common equity has the highest expected return in the capital stack. Sponsors typically target 15% to 25% or higher internal rates of return (IRR), and limited partners generally expect returns in the 12% to 20% range. Common equity usually represents 10% to 35% of the total capitalization. This is the position where value creation, operational expertise, and market timing are either rewarded or punished.

How Each Layer Is Priced Based on Risk

The pricing of each layer in the capital stack follows a fundamental principle: higher risk demands higher returns. The table below summarizes the typical characteristics of each layer across key dimensions.

Layer Typical % of Stack Typical Rate / Return Risk Level Repayment Priority
Senior Debt 50% to 75% 5% to 8% Lowest First (secured by property lien)
Mezzanine Debt 10% to 20% 10% to 15% Moderate to High Second (secured by ownership pledge)
Preferred Equity 5% to 15% 10% to 18% High Third (priority per operating agreement)
Common Equity 10% to 35% 15% to 25%+ IRR Highest Last (residual after all other layers)

This risk-return relationship creates a natural market for each layer. Conservative investors like insurance companies and pension funds gravitate toward senior debt. Specialized credit funds operate in the mezzanine space. Institutional investors and family offices often participate at the preferred equity level. Experienced sponsors occupy the common equity position where operational skill can generate outsized returns.

The blended cost of capital across all layers determines the deal's total financing expense. A four-layer stack with 60% senior debt at 6%, 15% mezzanine at 12%, 10% preferred equity at 14%, and 15% common equity targeting 20% has a significantly higher weighted average cost than a simple two-layer stack. Borrowers must evaluate whether the additional leverage justifies the increased cost and complexity.

Typical Percentages by Deal Type

The composition of the capital stack varies depending on the transaction type. Here are typical structures for three common deal types.

Stabilized Acquisition

A stabilized property with established cash flow and high occupancy typically has the simplest capital stack. Most acquisitions are financed with just two layers: senior debt at 65% to 75% LTV and common equity at 25% to 35%. Some sponsors add mezzanine to reduce their equity contribution, pushing total leverage to 80% to 85%. This structure works because stabilized properties generate predictable income that services multiple debt layers.

Value-Add Repositioning

Value-add deals require capital for both acquisition and renovation. The stack is more complex because senior lenders underwrite conservatively on the current condition. A typical structure includes a bridge loan at 65% to 75% of cost, mezzanine debt or preferred equity covering 10% to 15%, and sponsor equity at 15% to 20%. The bridge loan often includes a holdback for renovation funds, released as work is completed. Total leverage on a cost basis may reach 80% to 90%.

Ground-Up Development

Development deals have the most complex capital stacks because they involve the highest risk and longest time horizons. No income is generated until the project is completed and leased. A typical stack includes a construction loan at 55% to 65% of total project cost, mezzanine debt at 10% to 15%, and common equity at 25% to 35%. Some developers layer in preferred equity from institutional partners who want development exposure with downside protection. The construction lender disburses proceeds in draws as work progresses, and the entire stack is refinanced into permanent financing once stabilized.

How the Stack Affects Returns and Risk for Each Participant

The capital stack creates a waterfall of risk and return that flows from bottom to top. Understanding this waterfall is critical for borrowers structuring deals and investors evaluating where to deploy capital.

Downside Scenario: Property Value Declines

Consider a $10,000,000 property with $6,500,000 senior debt, $1,500,000 mezzanine debt, and $2,000,000 common equity. If the property value drops 15% to $8,500,000, common equity absorbs the entire $1,500,000 loss. The equity position is now worth $500,000, a 75% loss. The mezzanine lender's position remains intact, and the senior lender is unaffected.

If the property drops by 25% to $7,500,000, the entire $2,000,000 common equity is wiped out, and the mezzanine lender absorbs $500,000 of the remaining decline. The senior lender remains protected because the property value still exceeds their $6,500,000 position. Only if the property drops by more than 35% does the senior lender begin to experience losses. This layered loss absorption is precisely why senior debt carries the lowest interest rate.

Upside Scenario: Property Value Increases

Using the same property, if the value increases 20% to $12,000,000, the $2,000,000 gain accrues entirely to common equity holders. Their position grows from $2,000,000 to $4,000,000, a 100% return on invested equity. The mezzanine lender still receives only their contractual interest rate regardless of appreciation, and the senior lender continues receiving mortgage payments. This asymmetric return profile is why sponsors accept the risk of common equity: upside potential is unlimited while downside is capped at invested capital.

Intercreditor Agreements

When a capital stack includes multiple layers of debt, the relationship between those lenders must be formally documented in an intercreditor agreement (ICA). This contract is one of the most important and most heavily negotiated documents in any multi-layered capital structure.

What the Intercreditor Agreement Covers

The ICA typically addresses the following critical issues:

  • Payment priority: Confirms that the senior lender receives all scheduled payments before the subordinate lender receives any. During a default, the senior lender may block all payments to the mezzanine lender until the default is cured.
  • Standstill period: Requires the mezzanine lender to wait a specified period (typically 60 to 180 days) before exercising any remedies after a default, giving the senior lender time to pursue their own workout or foreclosure.
  • Cure rights: Grants the mezzanine lender the right to cure defaults on the senior loan to protect their position. If the borrower misses a senior mortgage payment, the mezzanine lender can step in and make that payment.
  • Purchase option: Often gives the mezzanine lender the right to purchase the senior loan at par (or at a discount in some cases) if the senior lender decides to sell or if a default occurs. This allows the mezzanine lender to take control of the entire debt position.
  • Modification restrictions: Limits either lender's ability to modify their loan terms without the other's consent. The senior lender typically cannot increase their loan amount or extend maturity without mezzanine approval, and the mezzanine lender cannot change terms that would adversely affect the senior position.

Why the ICA Matters for Borrowers

Borrowers often underestimate the time and cost required to negotiate the intercreditor agreement. ICA negotiations commonly take 30 to 60 days and involve significant legal fees. Some senior loan programs (particularly CMBS and agency loans) have rigid ICA requirements that leave little room for negotiation. Borrowers should confirm that their senior lender accepts subordinate financing before pursuing mezzanine capital.

When to Add Layers to the Stack

Not every deal needs a complex capital stack. Adding layers increases cost, introduces additional parties, and requires more time to close. Here are the situations where adding layers makes strategic sense.

  1. Insufficient senior loan proceeds: If the senior mortgage does not provide enough capital to close the deal, and you cannot or do not want to increase your equity contribution, a mezzanine or preferred equity layer fills the gap. This is the most common reason for adding a layer.
  2. Preserving equity for multiple deals: Experienced sponsors often prefer to spread their equity across several deals. Adding leverage through subordinate capital allows a sponsor to close more transactions with the same pool of equity, diversifying portfolio risk.
  3. Improving equity returns through leverage: When the property's yield exceeds the cost of subordinate capital, additional leverage amplifies equity returns. If a property generates an 8% unlevered return and mezzanine debt costs 12%, the math only works if the incremental leverage creates enough equity return enhancement to justify the cost. In many value-add and development scenarios, it does.
  4. Bringing in strategic partners: Preferred equity can attract institutional capital from partners who want deal exposure but not the full risk of common equity. These partners may also bring operational expertise or tenant relationships that enhance the project's success.
  5. Managing timeline risk: In development deals, a more complex stack can distribute risk across parties with different time horizons. Construction lenders want repayment in 24 to 36 months, while equity investors may hold for five to seven years for the full value creation cycle.

Capital Stack Examples

The following worked examples illustrate how the capital stack is structured across three common deal types.

Example 1: Stabilized Multifamily Acquisition ($8,000,000)

A sponsor acquires a 60-unit apartment complex that is 95% occupied with stable cash flow and a net operating income (NOI) of $560,000.

  • Senior debt (agency loan): $5,600,000 (70% LTV) at 5.75% fixed for 10 years, 30-year amortization
  • Common equity: $2,400,000 (30%) from the sponsor and limited partners

Annual debt service on the senior loan is approximately $392,000, resulting in a DSCR of 1.43x ($560,000 / $392,000). Cash flow after debt service is $168,000, delivering an 7.0% cash-on-cash return to equity investors in year one. This simple two-layer stack works well because the stabilized property generates reliable income and the sponsor has sufficient equity to meet the lender's LTV requirements.

Example 2: Value-Add Office Repositioning ($12,000,000 Total Cost)

A sponsor acquires a partially vacant office building for $9,000,000 and plans $3,000,000 in renovations. The projected stabilized value after renovation is $16,000,000.

  • Senior bridge loan: $8,400,000 (70% of total cost) at SOFR + 350 bps, interest-only, 3-year term with extensions
  • Mezzanine debt: $1,800,000 (15% of total cost) at 12% interest-only
  • Common equity: $1,800,000 (15% of total cost) from the sponsor

Total leverage is 85% of cost and approximately 64% of projected stabilized value. The senior lender is comfortable because their $8,400,000 position represents only 52.5% of the projected $16,000,000 stabilized value. The sponsor contributes only $1,800,000 to control a $12,000,000 project, and if the stabilized value reaches $16,000,000, the equity upside is substantial. Upon stabilization, the sponsor would refinance into a permanent non-recourse loan and retire both the bridge and mezzanine layers.

Example 3: Ground-Up Mixed-Use Development ($25,000,000 Total Cost)

A developer builds a mixed-use project with ground-floor retail and 80 apartments above. Total development cost including land, hard costs, and soft costs is $25,000,000. Projected stabilized value upon completion and lease-up is $35,000,000.

  • Construction loan: $15,000,000 (60% of cost) at SOFR + 400 bps, interest-only, 30-month term
  • Mezzanine debt: $3,750,000 (15% of cost) at 13% interest, accruing during construction
  • Preferred equity: $2,500,000 (10% of cost) at 15% preferred return
  • Common equity (sponsor): $3,750,000 (15% of cost)

This four-layer stack allows the developer to build a $25,000,000 project with only $3,750,000 of their own capital. The construction lender holds first priority at 60% of cost and approximately 43% of projected stabilized value. If the project is completed on budget and achieves projected rents, the developer stands to build significant equity. However, the layered debt creates substantial carrying costs during construction, making timely completion and lease-up critical. Upon stabilization, the developer would refinance into permanent financing, repaying the construction loan and mezzanine layer.

Common Mistakes in Structuring the Stack

Structuring the capital stack incorrectly can turn a good deal into a failed one. Here are the most frequent mistakes borrowers and sponsors make, along with how to avoid them.

  1. Over-leveraging with expensive subordinate debt: Adding mezzanine at 12% to 15% on top of senior debt can push the blended cost of capital so high that the property's cash flow cannot service all layers. Before adding subordinate capital, stress-test the deal at a combined DSCR of 1.10x or lower. If the property needs every dollar of projected income to make payments, the stack is too aggressive.
  2. Ignoring intercreditor timeline: Borrowers frequently commit to a closing date before confirming that lenders can agree on intercreditor terms. ICA negotiations can take weeks or months. Build this timeline into your deal schedule from the start, and confirm the senior lender accepts subordinate capital before engaging a mezzanine provider.
  3. Misunderstanding mezzanine vs. preferred equity: These are different legal structures with different remedies, tax implications, and impacts on the senior lender relationship. Using the wrong structure can trigger senior loan defaults or leave the subordinate capital provider without meaningful enforcement rights. Consult experienced real estate counsel before selecting between these options.
  4. Failing to model the full cost of capital: Sponsors sometimes focus on the rate of each individual layer without calculating the blended cost across the entire stack. A deal with 65% senior debt at 6% and 20% mezzanine at 13% has a weighted average debt cost of approximately 7.9%. Add preferred equity at 15% and the blended rate climbs further. If the property's cap rate is 7% and the blended cost of debt is 8%, the leverage is actually destroying value rather than creating it.
  5. Neglecting refinance risk: A complex capital stack assembled for a value-add or development project must eventually be refinanced into permanent financing. If the property does not stabilize as projected, the borrower may be unable to refinance the senior loan, let alone repay mezzanine and preferred equity layers. Build conservative assumptions into your exit strategy and have contingency plans if stabilization takes longer than expected.
  6. Skipping personal relationship management: Every layer of the stack involves a different capital provider with their own expectations and reporting requirements. Sponsors who fail to maintain clear communication with all capital partners risk losing trust and cooperation when problems arise. Establish regular reporting cadences and be transparent about progress and challenges.

Frequently Asked Questions

The capital stack is the layered structure of all financing sources used to fund a commercial real estate transaction. It typically includes senior debt at the base, followed by subordinate or mezzanine debt, preferred equity, and common equity at the top. Each layer has a different risk profile, return expectation, and priority of repayment.

The stack determines who gets paid first in the event of cash distributions or a sale, and who absorbs losses first if the property underperforms. Understanding the capital stack is essential for structuring deals that balance leverage, cost, and risk across all participants.

Each layer of the capital stack carries a different return profile based on its position and risk. Senior debt holders receive the lowest returns but have first priority on repayment and collateral. Mezzanine lenders earn higher interest rates to compensate for their subordinate position. Preferred equity investors receive a fixed return before common equity holders see any profit.

Common equity holders take the most risk but capture all remaining upside after other layers are satisfied. This structure means that higher positions in the stack offer greater potential returns alongside greater potential losses. The key principle is that risk and return are always correlated across the layers of the stack.

Mezzanine debt is a loan secured by a pledge of the borrower's ownership interest in the property-owning entity, not by the property itself. It carries a fixed interest rate and a maturity date, and the lender can foreclose on the ownership interest if the borrower defaults. Mezzanine debt creates a debtor-creditor relationship with all the legal protections that entails.

Preferred equity is an equity investment that receives a priority return before common equity holders. It does not have the same foreclosure remedies as debt and instead relies on operating agreement provisions for enforcement. From a senior lender's perspective, preferred equity is often more acceptable because it does not create an additional lien or debt obligation on the property. The choice between these structures depends on tax considerations, senior lender requirements, and the enforcement remedies each party needs.

An intercreditor agreement is a contract between the senior lender and any subordinate capital providers that governs the rights, priorities, and remedies of each party. It defines which lender gets paid first, what happens during a default, whether the subordinate lender can foreclose, and how the senior lender's consent is required for loan modifications.

These agreements protect the senior lender's position and are required in nearly every deal that involves multiple layers of debt. Negotiating the intercreditor agreement can take 30 to 60 days and requires experienced legal counsel for all parties. Borrowers should confirm early in the process that their senior lender will accept subordinate financing, as some loan programs restrict or prohibit additional debt layers.

Borrowers should consider adding layers when the senior loan alone does not provide enough proceeds to close the deal, or when doing so improves the overall risk-adjusted return on equity. Common scenarios include acquisitions where the borrower wants to minimize equity contribution, value-add projects where additional capital is needed for renovations, and development deals that require multiple funding sources.

However, adding layers increases the blended cost of capital, adds complexity to the deal structure, and requires intercreditor negotiations that can extend the closing timeline. Borrowers should weigh the cost of additional capital against the benefits of preserving their own equity for other investments. If the property's yield does not comfortably exceed the cost of the additional capital layer, adding leverage may destroy value rather than create it.
