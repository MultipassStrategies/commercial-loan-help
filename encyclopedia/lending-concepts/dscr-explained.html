DSCR Explained: What It Is, How to Calculate It, and Why Lenders Care

The Debt Service Coverage Ratio is the single most important metric in commercial loan underwriting. Understanding DSCR can mean the difference between loan approval and rejection.

Key Facts at a Glance

What Is DSCR and Why Does It Matter?

Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) measures whether a property generates enough income to cover its loan payments. It is expressed as a ratio: a DSCR of 1.25x means the property produces 25% more income than needed to service its debt.

DSCR matters because it is the primary metric lenders use to determine whether a commercial real estate loan is safe to make. A property could have excellent location, strong tenants, and a beautiful building, but if its DSCR falls below the lender's minimum threshold, the loan will not be approved.

In 2026, DSCR has become even more critical. As the $875 billion maturity wall forces widespread refinancing, many properties that comfortably met DSCR requirements at their original lower interest rates now struggle to qualify at today's higher rates. The math is straightforward: when interest rates rise, annual debt service increases, which pushes DSCR down even if property income has remained stable.

The DSCR Formula Explained Step by Step

The formula itself is simple:

DSCR Formula

DSCR = Net Operating Income (NOI) / Annual Debt Service

The key is understanding how each component is calculated.

How to Calculate Net Operating Income (NOI)

NOI represents the property's income after operating expenses but before debt service payments. Here is the standard calculation:

  1. Start with Gross Potential Rental Income: The total rent the property would generate if 100% occupied at market rates.
  2. Subtract Vacancy and Credit Loss: Lenders typically use 5% to 10%, even if the property is currently fully leased.
  3. Add Other Income: Parking fees, laundry revenue, storage income, late fees, pet rent, and similar ancillary income.
  4. Subtract Operating Expenses: Property management fees, real estate taxes, insurance, utilities (if landlord-paid), maintenance and repairs, landscaping, snow removal, and replacement reserves.

Important: NOI does not include debt service payments (mortgage principal and interest), depreciation, capital expenditures, or income taxes. These are excluded because NOI measures the property's operating performance independent of how it is financed or owned.

How to Calculate Annual Debt Service

Annual debt service is the total of all loan payments (principal plus interest) over a 12-month period. For a standard amortizing loan:

  • Calculate the monthly payment based on loan amount, interest rate, and amortization schedule
  • Multiply the monthly payment by 12 to get annual debt service
  • If the loan is interest-only, annual debt service equals the loan amount multiplied by the interest rate

Worked Example With Real Numbers

Consider a 24-unit apartment building with the following financial profile:

  • Gross Potential Rent: $432,000 per year ($1,500 per unit per month)
  • Vacancy Factor (7%): minus $30,240
  • Other Income (laundry, parking): plus $12,000
  • Effective Gross Income: $413,760
  • Operating Expenses: minus $165,504 (40% expense ratio)
  • NOI: $248,256

The borrower is seeking a $2,500,000 loan at 6.5% with a 30-year amortization:

  • Monthly Payment: $15,803
  • Annual Debt Service: $189,636
DSCR Calculation

DSCR = $248,256 / $189,636 = 1.31x

This property has a DSCR of 1.31x, which means it generates 31% more income than needed to cover debt payments. This would qualify with most lenders who require a minimum of 1.20x to 1.25x.

How Lenders Use DSCR in Underwriting

DSCR is not the only metric lenders evaluate, but it is often the most decisive. Here is how it fits into the broader underwriting process:

  • Loan sizing: Many lenders use DSCR to determine the maximum loan amount. They calculate how large a loan the property's NOI can support at their minimum DSCR threshold, then compare that to the LTV-based maximum. The borrower receives the lower of the two.
  • Rate determination: Higher DSCR properties may receive more favorable rates because they present less risk to the lender.
  • Stress testing: Some lenders calculate DSCR at a "stressed" interest rate (typically 1% to 2% above the actual rate) to ensure the property can handle rate increases or income declines.
  • Ongoing compliance: CMBS and some institutional loans require the borrower to maintain a minimum DSCR throughout the loan term. Falling below the threshold can trigger cash management provisions or even default.

Global DSCR vs. Property-Level DSCR

There are two ways lenders measure DSCR, and it is important to understand which one your lender uses:

  • Property-level DSCR: Uses only the subject property's NOI and the proposed loan's debt service. This is the standard for CMBS, agency (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac), HUD, and life company loans. It isolates the property's performance from the borrower's other activities.
  • Global DSCR: Includes all of the borrower's income (from other properties, businesses, W-2 employment, and investments) and all of the borrower's debt obligations (existing mortgages, credit cards, auto loans, and the proposed new loan). SBA lenders and many community banks use global DSCR because they are lending to the borrower as much as to the property.

Global DSCR can work in your favor if you have strong income from other sources. Conversely, heavy existing debt can drag your global DSCR below the threshold even if the subject property has strong cash flow on its own.

10 Strategies to Improve Your DSCR

If your DSCR falls short of lender requirements, consider these strategies before applying:

  1. Increase rents to market rate: If current rents are below market, raising them improves NOI directly. Even small increases across multiple units add up.
  2. Add ancillary income: Parking fees, storage units, pet rent, laundry, vending machines, and covered parking all boost NOI without major capital investment.
  3. Reduce vacancy: Improving marketing, offering move-in concessions, or upgrading units to attract tenants faster all reduce vacancy loss.
  4. Cut operating expenses: Renegotiate vendor contracts, challenge property tax assessments, shop insurance competitively, and implement energy-efficient upgrades to reduce utility costs.
  5. Increase the down payment: A larger down payment means a smaller loan, which means lower debt service, which improves DSCR. Each additional dollar of equity reduces the amount the property must support.
  6. Extend the amortization period: A 30-year amortization produces lower monthly payments than a 25-year schedule on the same loan amount, improving DSCR.
  7. Choose interest-only: Interest-only payments eliminate principal repayment from the debt service calculation, significantly boosting DSCR. This option is available on many bridge and CMBS loans.
  8. Negotiate a lower interest rate: Shopping multiple lenders and using competitive offers as leverage can reduce your rate and improve DSCR.
  9. Bring in mezzanine or preferred equity: If the DSCR shortfall means you need less senior debt, filling the gap with mezzanine debt or preferred equity reduces the first mortgage amount (and its debt service) that factors into the DSCR calculation.
  10. Wait and stabilize: If the property is not yet stabilized, taking additional time to improve occupancy and operations before applying can significantly improve your DSCR.

Common DSCR Mistakes Borrowers Make

  • Using pro forma instead of actuals: Lenders heavily discount pro forma projections. Most prefer to underwrite to T12 (trailing 12-month) actual income and expenses. Do not assume the lender will use your optimistic projections.
  • Ignoring vacancy: Even if your property is 100% occupied today, lenders will apply a vacancy factor (5% to 10%). Build this into your own calculations.
  • Forgetting management fees: Even if you self-manage, lenders add a management fee (typically 4% to 8% of gross income) to operating expenses. This ensures the property can support professional management if needed.
  • Excluding replacement reserves: Lenders include an annual reserve for capital replacements (typically $250 to $500 per unit for multifamily). This is deducted from NOI.
  • Calculating with current low rates: If you have a below-market rate that is about to adjust or expire, the lender will calculate DSCR using the new, higher rate for refinancing.

DSCR Considerations for Different Property Types

  • Multifamily: Strong rental demand supports stable NOI. Agency lenders (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac) typically require 1.20x to 1.25x. HUD programs accept as low as 1.11x.
  • Office: Higher vacancy risk in the post-pandemic environment means lenders apply larger vacancy factors, which reduces NOI and DSCR. Requirements have tightened to 1.30x or higher for many lenders.
  • Retail: Tenant rollover risk and anchor tenant dependency create volatility. Credit tenants on long-term NNN leases produce the most predictable DSCR.
  • Industrial: Long-term leases and strong tenant demand support stable, predictable DSCR. Lender favorite in the current market.
  • Hospitality: Seasonal revenue fluctuations and operating intensity make DSCR more volatile. Lenders typically require 1.40x or higher and focus on trailing performance.
  • Self-storage: Diversified tenant base with low operating costs supports consistent DSCR. Month-to-month leases create some income volatility.
