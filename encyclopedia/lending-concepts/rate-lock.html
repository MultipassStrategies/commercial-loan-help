Rate Lock on Commercial Mortgages: How to Lock Your Interest Rate

A rate lock protects your interest rate from market fluctuations while your commercial loan moves through underwriting and closing. Knowing when to lock, how long to lock for, and what it costs can mean the difference between a deal that pencils and one that falls apart.

Key Facts at a Glance

Definition A lender commitment to hold a specific rate until closing
Typical Lock Periods 30, 45, 60, or 90 days
Lock Deposit 0.5% to 2.0% of loan amount
When You Can Lock At application, commitment, or rate lock agreement

What Rate Lock Means in Commercial Lending

A rate lock is a binding agreement between a borrower and a lender that fixes the loan's interest rate for a specified period. Once the lock is in place, the lender guarantees that the borrower's rate will not change, regardless of what happens in the broader interest rate market. If Treasury yields spike or the Federal Reserve raises rates during your lock window, your locked rate stays the same.

In commercial real estate, where loans routinely take 45 to 120 days to close, rate exposure is a serious concern. A 50 basis point move in the 10-year Treasury yield can change the economics of a deal entirely. On a $5,000,000 loan, that translates to $25,000 per year in additional interest expense. Over a 10-year term, that is $250,000 in added cost. A rate lock eliminates this uncertainty by converting an unknown future rate into a known, fixed number.

Rate locks in commercial lending work differently from residential mortgages in several important ways. Commercial locks tend to require a deposit (sometimes called "good faith" money or an "at-risk" deposit) that the borrower forfeits if the loan does not close. Lock periods may be shorter or longer depending on the loan program. And the mechanics of when and how you can lock vary significantly by lender type. Understanding these differences is essential for protecting your deal.

When in the Process You Can Lock

The timing of a rate lock depends on the lender, the loan program, and the stage of your transaction. Unlike residential lending, where locks are commonly available at application, commercial lenders typically require more due diligence before allowing a lock.

Common Lock Timing by Stage

  • At application: Some bank lenders and credit unions allow early rate locks at the time of application. These locks usually require a deposit and come with conditions (such as satisfactory appraisal and underwriting). Early locks give borrowers the most protection against rate movements but carry the highest risk of forfeiture if the deal does not proceed.
  • At commitment or term sheet: Many commercial lenders allow rate locks once a term sheet or commitment letter has been issued. At this stage, the lender has completed a preliminary review and is comfortable with the deal's fundamentals. This is the most common lock point for conventional bank loans and life company loans.
  • At rate lock agreement: CMBS and agency lenders (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac) typically have a formal rate lock agreement that is a separate step from the loan application and commitment. The borrower signs the rate lock agreement, pays the required deposit, and the rate is fixed from that date forward. This often happens after initial underwriting but before final loan approval.
  • At closing (float to close): Some borrowers choose to "float" their rate, meaning they do not lock until very close to the closing date. Floating carries the risk that rates may rise, but it also allows the borrower to benefit if rates fall. This approach is most common in stable or declining rate environments.

The best time to lock depends on your risk tolerance, your view of the rate environment, and how far away your closing date is. If you are risk-averse or see signs that rates are trending upward, locking early provides certainty. If you believe rates are stable or may decline, floating longer may yield a better rate.

Lock Periods: 30, 45, 60, and 90 Days

Rate lock periods in commercial lending typically range from 30 to 90 days, though some lenders offer shorter or longer windows for specific programs. The lock period you choose should align with your realistic closing timeline, including a buffer for potential delays.

Lock Period Typical Cost Premium Best For Risk Level
30 days Lowest (often no premium) Refinances with clear title and appraisal in hand High (tight timeline)
45 days Minimal (0 to 5 bps) Standard acquisitions with motivated parties Moderate
60 days Moderate (5 to 15 bps) Most commercial transactions; allows for appraisal and legal review Low to moderate
90 days Higher (10 to 25 bps) Complex deals, 1031 exchanges, multi-property transactions Low (ample buffer)

Longer lock periods cost more because the lender bears rate risk for a longer window. The cost is typically expressed as a small addition to the interest rate (in basis points) or as a higher upfront fee. A 90-day lock might add 10 to 25 basis points to the rate compared to a 30-day lock on the same loan.

Choosing the right lock period requires balancing cost against risk. A 30-day lock is inexpensive but leaves almost no margin for closing delays. If your appraisal takes longer than expected, your title company uncovers an issue, or the lender's legal review runs behind schedule, you could face an expensive extension or expiration. A 60-day lock is the most popular choice for standard commercial transactions because it provides adequate time for the closing process while keeping costs reasonable.

Rate Lock Deposits and Fees

Commercial rate locks almost always require a deposit, and understanding how this deposit works is critical to managing your costs and risk.

How Lock Deposits Work

When you execute a rate lock agreement, the lender will require a deposit, typically ranging from 0.5% to 2.0% of the loan amount. On a $4,000,000 loan, this means a deposit between $20,000 and $80,000. This deposit serves two purposes: it compensates the lender for the cost of hedging their rate risk, and it demonstrates the borrower's serious commitment to closing the loan.

In most cases, the deposit is applied as a credit toward closing costs or returned to the borrower at closing. The key risk is what happens if the loan does not close. If the borrower defaults on the loan commitment, fails to meet conditions, or simply decides not to proceed, the rate lock deposit is typically forfeited to the lender. This is sometimes called the "at-risk" or "good faith" deposit for exactly this reason.

When Is the Deposit Refundable?

Lock deposit refund policies vary by lender and should be reviewed carefully before signing any rate lock agreement. Common scenarios include the following.

  • Loan closes as planned: Deposit is credited to closing costs or refunded in full.
  • Borrower walks away voluntarily: Deposit is forfeited.
  • Lender declines the loan (underwriting failure): In most cases, the deposit is refunded if the lender cannot deliver the loan through no fault of the borrower.
  • Appraisal shortfall: Policies vary. Some lenders refund the deposit if the appraisal comes in too low to support the loan. Others retain all or a portion.
  • Material adverse change: If market conditions or the borrower's financial situation change materially, the outcome depends on the specific language in the lock agreement.

Always read the rate lock agreement carefully and understand the exact conditions under which your deposit is at risk. If possible, negotiate for broader refund protections, especially for scenarios outside your control such as appraisal shortfalls or environmental issues discovered during due diligence.

What Happens If Rates Drop After You Lock

One of the most common concerns borrowers have about rate locks is the fear of locking in and then watching rates fall. This is a real risk, and it is important to understand your options.

Standard Rate Locks (No Float-Down)

With a standard rate lock, your rate is fixed in both directions. If rates go up, you are protected. If rates go down, you do not benefit. The rate you locked is the rate you get, period. Most commercial lenders operate on this basis, and it is the default unless you specifically negotiate otherwise.

Float-Down Options

Some lenders offer a "float-down" provision that allows the borrower to capture a lower rate if market rates decline during the lock period. Float-down provisions come with important conditions.

  • Minimum threshold: The rate must typically drop by at least 25 basis points (sometimes more) before the float-down kicks in.
  • Timing restrictions: Many float-down agreements only allow the rate adjustment within a specific window, such as 15 to 30 days before closing.
  • Cost: Float-down options may add 5 to 15 basis points to the locked rate or require an additional fee. Some lenders include this at no extra cost as a competitive feature.
  • One-time use: Most float-down provisions can only be exercised once during the lock period.

Float-down options are most commonly available on agency loans (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac multifamily programs) and some bank loans. They are less common on CMBS and life company loans, where rate lock mechanics are more rigid.

Practical Perspective

The purpose of a rate lock is certainty, not speculation. Borrowers who obsess over whether rates might drop after locking are approaching the decision from the wrong angle. The question is not "will rates go lower?" but rather "does this rate make my deal work?" If the answer is yes, locking eliminates the risk that a rate increase could derail your closing. Trying to time the exact bottom of the rate market is a gamble that even professional bond traders rarely win consistently.

Broken Locks and Extensions

A "broken lock" occurs when a borrower either cannot or chooses not to close within the lock period. This is an expensive problem with consequences that extend beyond the immediate deal.

What Causes a Broken Lock

  • Closing delays: Appraisal delays, title issues, environmental problems, legal review bottlenecks, or borrower documentation gaps can push the closing past the lock expiration.
  • Borrower default: The borrower decides not to proceed with the loan, loses financing for the equity portion, or fails to meet underwriting conditions.
  • Material changes: Significant changes in the property's condition, occupancy, or the borrower's financial situation that cause the lender to withdraw the commitment.

Lock Extensions

If your closing is delayed but still viable, requesting a lock extension is the standard remedy. Extension terms vary by lender but generally follow this pattern.

  • Extension period: 15 to 30 days per extension, with some lenders allowing multiple extensions.
  • Extension cost: Typically 0.125% to 0.375% of the loan amount per extension period. On a $5,000,000 loan, a single extension might cost $6,250 to $18,750.
  • Rate adjustment: Some lenders extend at the original locked rate. Others may extend at the "worse of" the original rate or current market rate, meaning you keep your locked rate only if rates have risen, but you get the current (higher) rate if rates have not changed or have dropped. Read the extension terms carefully.

Consequences of a Broken Lock

If the lock expires and you cannot extend or choose not to, you face re-locking at current market rates, which may be significantly higher. You also forfeit your rate lock deposit in most cases. Beyond the immediate financial impact, a broken lock can damage your relationship with the lender and affect your ability to obtain favorable terms on future transactions. Lenders track borrower lock behavior, and a pattern of broken locks signals unreliability.

Rate Lock vs. Rate Cap

Borrowers sometimes confuse rate locks with rate caps, but these are fundamentally different tools that serve different purposes at different stages of a loan.

Feature Rate Lock Rate Cap
Purpose Fixes rate during loan origination Limits rate increases during loan term
When Used Between commitment and closing After closing, during loan term
Loan Type Fixed-rate and floating-rate originations Floating-rate loans only
Duration 30 to 90 days (pre-closing) 1 to 5 years (post-closing)
Cost Structure Deposit (credited at close) plus possible rate premium Upfront premium based on notional amount, term, and strike rate
What It Protects Against rate changes before you close Against rate increases after you close

A rate lock is relevant during the origination process. Once you close, the rate lock has served its purpose and expires. A rate cap is relevant during the life of a floating-rate loan and is purchased separately, often required by the lender as a condition of the loan. Bridge loans, for example, frequently require borrowers to purchase a SOFR-based rate cap at closing to ensure debt service coverage even if short-term rates rise sharply.

Rate Lock Differences by Loan Type

Rate lock mechanics vary significantly depending on the type of commercial loan you are pursuing. Each loan program has its own conventions for when you can lock, what it costs, and how flexible the terms are.

Agency Loans (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac)

Agency multifamily loans offer some of the most borrower-friendly rate lock terms in the commercial market. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac allow rate locks relatively early in the process, and lock periods are flexible (typically 30 to 90 days). Rate lock deposits are standard, usually around 1% to 2% of the loan amount, and are credited at closing. Many agency lenders offer float-down provisions, allowing borrowers to capture lower rates if the market moves favorably during the lock period. The benchmark for agency loan rates is typically the 10-year Treasury yield plus a spread, and this is the rate that gets locked.

CMBS Loans

CMBS rate locks tend to be more structured and less flexible than agency or bank locks. The rate lock is typically executed as a formal agreement after the loan has been sized and the borrower has been approved through the lender's credit committee. Lock periods usually range from 30 to 60 days. CMBS rate lock deposits are typically 1% to 2% of the loan amount and are at risk if the borrower does not close. Float-down provisions are uncommon on CMBS loans. The locked rate is based on the applicable Treasury or swap rate plus the lender's spread, and once locked, there is very little room for negotiation.

Bank and Credit Union Loans

Bank loan rate locks are often the most flexible and informal of the commercial loan programs. Some community banks lock rates verbally or with a simple letter agreement, while larger banks have more formal processes. Lock periods vary widely, from 30 to 90 days or more. Rate lock deposits may be lower (0.5% to 1.0%) or in some cases waived entirely for existing banking relationships. Banks are also more likely to offer rate lock extensions at reasonable cost and to accommodate closing delays. The tradeoff is that bank loans may carry higher rates or less favorable terms than agency or CMBS alternatives, particularly for larger loans.

Life Company Loans

Life insurance company lenders typically offer rate locks at the time of formal commitment, after thorough underwriting. Lock periods tend to be 45 to 60 days. Deposits are standard, usually 1% of the loan amount. Life companies offer the lowest rates in the commercial market, but their rate lock terms tend to be rigid. Float-down provisions are rare, and lock extensions may be granted reluctantly and at a premium. The advantage is that life company closing processes are generally efficient, so the lock period is usually sufficient if both parties are well prepared.

Strategies for Timing Your Rate Lock

Timing a rate lock is part analysis, part risk management, and part practical planning. While no one can predict rate movements with certainty, there are strategies that can help you make a more informed decision.

Monitor the Rate Environment

Commercial mortgage rates are primarily driven by the 10-year Treasury yield (for fixed-rate loans) and SOFR (for floating-rate loans). Before deciding when to lock, develop a basic understanding of the current rate trend. Are Treasury yields rising, falling, or stable? Is the Federal Reserve signaling rate changes? Are economic indicators pointing toward inflation or recession? You do not need to become a bond trader, but following the 10-year Treasury yield on a daily basis during your loan process will give you context for your lock decision.

Lock When the Deal Works

The most reliable strategy is to lock when the offered rate makes your deal economics work. Run your numbers at the available locked rate and confirm that the property's income covers debt service with an adequate cushion, that your DSCR meets lender requirements, and that your cash-on-cash return meets your investment criteria. If the numbers work, locking eliminates the risk that a rate increase could break the deal. Waiting for a slightly better rate introduces the risk of a worse one.

Align Lock Period with Closing Timeline

Work backward from your expected closing date to determine the appropriate lock period. Add at least 10 to 15 days of buffer to account for common delays: appraisal turnaround times, lender review periods, title and survey issues, and legal document negotiation. If your expected closing is 50 days away, a 60-day lock provides a reasonable cushion. If closing is 70 days away, a 90-day lock is more appropriate, even if it costs a few extra basis points.

Coordinate with Your Lender

Before locking, have a direct conversation with your lender about the expected closing timeline. Ask whether there are any known bottlenecks, such as a backlog in the appraisal queue or a legal team that is stretched thin. If the lender indicates that closing within 45 days is unlikely, there is no point in taking a 45-day lock. Coordinating expectations upfront prevents expensive extensions later.

Consider Splitting the Risk

If you are torn between locking now and waiting, some borrowers take a middle approach. They lock a portion of their rate exposure and float the remainder. For example, on a deal with two tranches of debt, you might lock the senior mortgage while floating the mezzanine piece. This is not available in all deal structures, but where it is possible, it provides partial protection while preserving some upside if rates decline.

Use Rate Lock as a Negotiation Tool

If you are evaluating multiple lenders, the rate lock terms can be a powerful point of comparison. One lender might offer a slightly higher rate but include a free float-down provision and a more flexible extension policy. Another might offer a lower rate but with a larger at-risk deposit and no float-down. Evaluating the full package of rate lock terms, not just the headline rate, gives you a more accurate picture of the true cost and risk profile of each loan offer.

Frequently Asked Questions

A rate lock is a lender's written commitment to hold a specific interest rate for a defined period while the loan moves through underwriting and closing. Once locked, your rate will not increase even if market rates rise before you close. Rate locks on commercial mortgages typically range from 30 to 90 days, and most lenders require a deposit or fee to secure the lock.

The lock protects both the borrower and the lender. The borrower gains certainty about their future debt service payments, and the lender can hedge their rate exposure in the capital markets. Without a lock, the borrower bears full market risk during a period when they have limited control over the closing timeline.

Rate lock deposits on commercial loans typically range from 0.5% to 2.0% of the loan amount, depending on the lender, loan type, and lock period. For a $3,000,000 loan, that means a deposit between $15,000 and $60,000. In most cases, this deposit is credited toward closing costs or refunded at closing, so it is not an additional expense if the deal closes successfully.

Beyond the deposit, longer lock periods may carry a small rate premium, typically 5 to 25 basis points depending on the length. Some lenders also charge a separate rate lock fee that is non-refundable regardless of whether the loan closes. Always clarify with your lender whether the lock cost is a refundable deposit, a rate adjustment, a flat fee, or some combination of these.

Most standard rate locks do not allow renegotiation if rates fall after you lock. However, some lenders offer a float-down option, which lets you capture some or all of a rate decrease that occurs during your lock period. Float-down provisions typically require the market rate to drop by at least 25 basis points before they can be exercised, and they may only be available within a specific window before closing.

Float-down options are most common on agency loans (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac multifamily programs) and some bank loans. They are much less common on CMBS and life company loans. If having the ability to capture a lower rate is important to you, ask about float-down availability before you sign the rate lock agreement, and understand any additional fees or conditions that apply.

If your lock expires before closing, you generally have two options. First, you can request a lock extension from your lender, which typically costs 0.125% to 0.375% of the loan amount per additional period (usually 15 to 30 days). Second, you can re-lock at current market rates, which may be higher or lower than your original locked rate. Either way, an expired lock adds cost and complexity to your transaction.

To avoid this situation, choose a lock period that includes at least 10 to 15 days of buffer beyond your expected closing date. Communicate proactively with your lender if you anticipate delays, and begin the extension process before the lock actually expires. Most lenders are more accommodating when borrowers address potential problems early rather than waiting until the last day.

A rate lock and a rate cap serve very different purposes at different stages of a loan. A rate lock guarantees a fixed interest rate during the period between loan commitment and closing. It protects you from rate increases while your loan is being processed, and it expires once the loan closes. Rate locks apply to the origination of both fixed-rate and floating-rate loans.

A rate cap, by contrast, is an interest rate derivative purchased on floating-rate loans that limits how high your rate can go during the life of the loan. Rate caps are bought at or after closing and typically last 1 to 5 years. They are common on bridge loans and other adjustable-rate commercial products. The cost of a rate cap depends on the notional loan amount, the cap term, and the strike rate. Both tools manage interest rate risk, but they operate in different timeframes and apply to different phases of the lending process.
