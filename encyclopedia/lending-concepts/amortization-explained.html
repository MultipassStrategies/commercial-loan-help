Commercial Mortgage Amortization: Schedules, Terms, and What Borrowers Should Know

Amortization determines how your commercial loan is repaid over time, how much you pay each month, and how large your balloon payment will be at maturity. Understanding how it works is essential before signing any loan agreement.

Key Facts at a Glance

Definition Repayment of loan principal over time through scheduled payments
Common Periods 20 to 30 years
Difference from Loan Term Amortization period usually longer than loan term
Balloon Payment Remaining balance due when term ends before full amortization
Impact on Payment Longer amortization = lower monthly payment
Interest-Only Option Delays principal repayment; payments are interest only

What Amortization Means in Commercial Lending

Amortization is the process of repaying a loan's principal balance through regular, scheduled payments over time. Each payment includes two components: a portion that goes toward interest on the outstanding balance, and a portion that reduces the principal itself. As the principal decreases with each payment, the interest portion shrinks and the principal portion grows. By the end of the amortization period, the entire loan balance would be repaid to zero.

In commercial real estate, amortization is one of the most important structural elements of any loan. It directly determines how much you pay each month, how quickly you build equity in the property, and whether you will face a large balloon payment at the end of the loan term. Two loans with identical interest rates and loan amounts can have dramatically different monthly payments and payoff structures depending on their amortization schedules.

Unlike residential mortgages, where 30-year fully amortizing loans are standard, commercial loans frequently use amortization periods that differ from the actual loan term. This mismatch is one of the defining features of commercial lending, and understanding it is critical for every borrower.

Amortization Period vs. Loan Term: The Mismatch

This is perhaps the most important concept in commercial loan structure, and the one that catches many first-time borrowers off guard. The amortization period and the loan term are two different things.

The amortization period is the theoretical timeframe used to calculate your monthly payments. If your loan has a 25-year amortization, your monthly payment is calculated as if you will be making payments for 25 years until the loan is fully repaid.

The loan term is the actual length of time you have the loan before the entire remaining balance becomes due. Commercial loan terms typically range from 5 to 10 years, though some programs offer longer terms.

Here is how the mismatch works in practice. Suppose you take out a $1,000,000 loan at 6.5% interest with a 10-year term and 25-year amortization. Your monthly payments are calculated based on 25 years, which gives you a payment of $6,752 per month. You make those payments for 10 years (120 payments), steadily paying down principal along the way. But after 10 years, you have only made 120 of the 300 payments that would be needed to fully repay the loan. The remaining balance at that point, approximately $775,114, is due in full as a balloon payment.

This structure exists because lenders want the lower risk that comes with shorter commitment periods (they can reassess the loan at maturity), while borrowers benefit from the lower monthly payments that come with longer amortization schedules. It is a compromise that works well for both parties, as long as the borrower understands and plans for the balloon payment at maturity.

How an Amortization Schedule Works

An amortization schedule is a table that shows every payment over the life of the loan, broken down into principal and interest components. Understanding how this schedule works reveals an important pattern that affects your equity position throughout the loan.

In the early years of a loan, the majority of each payment goes toward interest rather than principal. This happens because interest is calculated on the outstanding balance, which is at its highest point at the beginning of the loan. As you make payments and the balance decreases, less interest accrues each month, and more of your payment goes toward reducing principal.

Consider that same $1,000,000 loan at 6.5% with 25-year amortization. Your first monthly payment of $6,752 breaks down as follows: $5,417 goes to interest (the outstanding $1,000,000 multiplied by 6.5%, divided by 12 months), and only $1,335 goes toward principal. That means roughly 80% of your first payment is interest.

Five years into the loan, the balance has decreased to approximately $898,000. Now your monthly payment of $6,752 splits into $4,864 of interest and $1,888 of principal. The principal portion has grown by over 40% compared to the first payment.

By year 15, the balance is approximately $680,000, and your payment splits into $3,683 of interest and $3,069 of principal. The ratio has shifted significantly, with nearly half of each payment now reducing the loan balance.

This front-loaded interest structure has practical implications. In the early years of a commercial loan, you are building equity slowly through principal payments while paying substantially more in interest. This is one reason why some borrowers opt for interest-only periods at the start. If you plan to sell or refinance within a few years, the additional principal reduction from an amortizing payment may not justify the higher monthly cost.

Typical Amortization Periods by Loan Type

Different commercial loan products use different amortization schedules. The table below shows what to expect from each major loan type.

Loan Type Typical Amortization Notes
SBA 504 Up to 25 years Can be fully amortizing; longest SBA amortization for real estate
SBA 7(a) Up to 25 years 25 years for real estate; 10 years for equipment; 7 years for working capital
Bank Conventional 20 to 25 years Terms usually 5 to 10 years; amortization negotiable
CMBS 25 to 30 years Fixed structure; 10-year term with 30-year amortization is common
Agency (Fannie/Freddie) 30 to 35 years Multifamily only; longest amortization periods in the market
Bridge Interest-only Typically no amortization; 1 to 3 year terms
Life Company 25 to 30 years Conservative underwriting; low leverage with long amortization

The loan type you qualify for significantly affects your amortization options. Agency loans offer the longest amortization periods, which produce the lowest monthly payments relative to loan amount. Bridge loans sit at the opposite end of the spectrum, offering no amortization at all in exchange for short-term flexibility.

Interest-Only Periods and the Transition to Amortizing Payments

Many commercial loans include an interest-only (IO) period at the beginning of the loan term. During this phase, monthly payments consist entirely of interest, and the principal balance does not decrease. Interest-only periods typically last 1 to 5 years, depending on the loan type and lender.

Interest-only payments are significantly lower than amortizing payments. On a $1,000,000 loan at 6.5%, the interest-only payment is $5,417 per month. Compare that to $6,752 per month with 25-year amortization, a difference of $1,335 per month or $16,020 per year. For borrowers with properties that are stabilizing, renovating, or in a lease-up phase, this cash flow relief can be critical.

The transition from interest-only to amortizing payments requires careful planning. When the IO period ends, your monthly payment increases, sometimes by 25% or more. If the loan originally called for 25-year amortization and you had 3 years of interest-only, the remaining principal must now amortize over the remaining 22 years (not 25), which means even higher payments than the original amortizing schedule would have required.

Some loan programs structure IO periods differently. CMBS loans may offer 2 to 5 years of interest-only followed by amortization for the remainder of the term. Agency loans sometimes offer IO for the first 1 to 3 years. Bridge loans are typically interest-only for their entire term, with the assumption that the borrower will refinance into a permanent loan or sell the property before maturity.

Balloon Payments at Maturity

A balloon payment is the remaining loan balance that becomes due when the loan term expires before the amortization period is complete. Because most commercial loans have terms shorter than their amortization periods, balloon payments are the norm rather than the exception in commercial lending.

The size of your balloon payment depends on two factors: how long you have been making payments (the loan term) and how those payments were structured (the amortization period). A shorter loan term means you have had less time to pay down principal, resulting in a larger balloon. A longer amortization period means each payment contains less principal, also resulting in a larger balloon.

When the balloon payment comes due, borrowers typically have three options. The most common path is refinancing: taking out a new loan to pay off the remaining balance of the maturing loan. The second option is selling the property and using the proceeds to pay off the balance. The third option, which is less common, is negotiating a loan extension or modification with the existing lender.

Balloon maturity risk is real and should not be underestimated. If market conditions have changed, interest rates have risen, or the property has declined in value or performance, refinancing may be difficult or expensive. Borrowers who purchased or refinanced during periods of low interest rates may face significant payment increases when they refinance at maturity. Planning for balloon maturity should begin 12 to 18 months before the loan matures, giving you adequate time to explore options and secure the best available terms.

Self-Amortizing vs. Partially Amortizing Loans

Understanding the distinction between these two structures is essential for evaluating commercial loan offers.

A self-amortizing (or fully amortizing) loan is one where the loan term equals the amortization period. If you have a 25-year term with 25-year amortization, every scheduled payment reduces the balance, and the final payment brings the balance to zero. There is no balloon payment. These loans are relatively uncommon in commercial lending but are available through SBA 504 programs, some community banks, and HUD/FHA multifamily programs. The main advantage is the elimination of balloon payment risk. The tradeoff is that monthly payments are higher than they would be on a partially amortizing loan with the same amortization period but a shorter term.

A partially amortizing loan is the standard structure in commercial real estate. The loan term is shorter than the amortization period, so only a portion of the principal is repaid during the term, and the rest is due as a balloon payment at maturity. A 10-year term with 25-year amortization is a classic example. You make amortizing payments for 10 years, building some equity along the way, but a substantial balance remains when the term expires.

A third variation worth noting is the interest-only loan, where no principal is repaid during the term. The entire original loan amount is due as a balloon payment at maturity. This is typical of bridge loans and some short-term financing structures.

How Amortization Period Affects Monthly Payment and Total Interest

The amortization period you choose has a direct and significant impact on three things: your monthly payment, the total interest you pay, and the size of your balloon payment. The following table illustrates these differences using a $1,000,000 loan at 6.5% interest with a 10-year term.

Amortization Period Monthly Payment Total Interest (10 Years) Balloon Balance at Year 10
15 years $8,711 $490,540 $445,211
20 years $7,456 $551,303 $656,615
25 years $6,752 $585,362 $775,114
30 years $6,321 $606,243 $847,761

The numbers reveal clear tradeoffs. Moving from 15-year to 30-year amortization reduces your monthly payment by $2,390 (a 27% decrease), which improves monthly cash flow and boosts your Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR). However, total interest paid over 10 years increases by $115,703, and your balloon payment grows from $445,211 to $847,761.

In practical terms, the longer amortization means you are paying less each month but retaining more debt. The lower payment may allow you to qualify for a larger loan or meet DSCR requirements, but the larger balloon payment increases your refinancing risk at maturity. Conversely, a shorter amortization builds equity faster, giving you more flexibility and a smaller balance to refinance, but at the cost of higher monthly payments that may strain cash flow.

For most commercial borrowers, 25-year amortization represents the sweet spot. It offers a manageable monthly payment without extending the schedule so far that principal reduction becomes negligible. That said, the right choice depends on your specific circumstances, including your cash flow needs, how long you plan to hold the property, and your risk tolerance for balloon payments.

Choosing the Right Amortization Schedule

Selecting the right amortization period requires balancing several competing priorities. Here are the key factors to consider.

Cash flow needs: If the property generates tight cash flow relative to debt service, a longer amortization period reduces the monthly payment and gives you more breathing room. This is especially important for properties that are still stabilizing or that have significant operating expenses.

DSCR requirements: Because DSCR is calculated using annual debt service, a longer amortization period (which lowers the payment) directly improves your DSCR. If you are close to a lender's minimum DSCR threshold, extending the amortization by 5 years may make the difference between approval and rejection.

Equity building goals: If you plan to hold the property long-term and want to build equity through principal paydown, a shorter amortization period accelerates that process. Over a 10-year hold period, the difference in principal reduction between 15-year and 30-year amortization is over $400,000 on a $1,000,000 loan.

Refinancing strategy: If you plan to refinance or sell within a few years, the amortization period matters less because you will not hold the loan long enough for the differences to compound significantly. In this case, optimizing for the lowest monthly payment (longest amortization) may make the most sense.

Interest rate environment: In a high-rate environment, longer amortization helps offset the impact of elevated rates on monthly payments. When rates are low, shorter amortization allows you to pay down principal aggressively at a low cost.

Loan program constraints: Some loan programs fix the amortization period. CMBS loans typically use 30-year amortization with no flexibility. SBA loans cap at 25 years for real estate. Agency loans may offer 30 to 35 years. Understand what each program allows before you commit to a strategy.

The best approach for most borrowers is to model multiple scenarios, comparing monthly payments, total interest costs, and balloon balances at different amortization periods. This analysis, combined with your specific investment strategy and cash flow projections, will point you toward the right structure.

Most commercial loans use 25-year amortization, though this varies by loan type. SBA loans go up to 25 years for real estate, CMBS and life company loans typically use 25 to 30 years, and agency multifamily loans (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac) can extend to 30 or even 35 years. Bridge loans usually do not amortize at all, using interest-only structures instead. The amortization period is one of many terms you can negotiate with your lender.

The amortization period is the theoretical timeframe over which the loan would be fully repaid. The loan term is the actual duration of the loan before the remaining balance becomes due. In commercial lending, the amortization period is almost always longer than the loan term. For example, a loan with a 10-year term and 25-year amortization calculates payments as if the loan will be repaid over 25 years, but the entire remaining balance (the balloon payment) is due at the end of year 10.

When a commercial loan reaches maturity and there is a remaining balance (balloon payment), the borrower must either refinance with a new loan, sell the property to pay off the balance, or negotiate a loan extension or modification with the current lender. Most borrowers refinance, which is why it is important to start the refinancing process 12 to 18 months before maturity. If you cannot refinance or sell, the loan goes into default, and the lender can pursue foreclosure.

Fully amortizing commercial loans do exist but are relatively uncommon. SBA 504 loans can be fully amortizing with 25-year terms, and some community bank loans are structured this way. HUD/FHA multifamily loans are fully amortizing with terms up to 35 years. The advantage is no balloon payment risk; the disadvantage is that monthly payments are higher because you are repaying all principal during the term rather than deferring a portion.

An interest-only period reduces monthly payments during that phase because you are not repaying any principal. However, it increases total interest paid over the life of the loan because the principal balance does not decrease during the interest-only period. When the interest-only period ends and the loan begins to amortize, payments increase (sometimes significantly) because the full principal must now be repaid over the remaining amortization period. Interest-only periods are most beneficial for borrowers who plan to sell or refinance before the amortization phase begins.
