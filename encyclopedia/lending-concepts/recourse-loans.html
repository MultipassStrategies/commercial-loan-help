Recourse Loans in Commercial Real Estate: What Borrowers Need to Know

Most commercial real estate loans require personal liability from the borrower. Understanding how recourse works, which loan types require it, and how to negotiate better terms can save you significant risk exposure on every deal.

Key Facts at a Glance

Most Common In Bank loans, SBA loans, bridge loans
Key Advantage Lower rates and easier qualification

What Recourse Means in Commercial Lending

A recourse loan gives the lender the right to pursue the borrower's personal assets if the collateral property does not fully satisfy the outstanding debt. In practical terms, this means the borrower (or a designated guarantor) is personally on the hook for the loan. If the property is foreclosed and sold for less than the remaining balance, the lender can seek a deficiency judgment to recover the difference from the guarantor's personal wealth.

This stands in contrast to non-recourse loans, where the lender's recovery is limited to the collateral property itself. In a non-recourse structure, if the property sells for less than the loan balance at foreclosure, the lender absorbs the loss. With recourse, that loss transfers to the borrower personally.

The majority of commercial real estate loans in the United States are structured as recourse. Banks, credit unions, SBA lenders, and most bridge lenders require personal guaranties as a standard condition of approval. Non-recourse is the exception rather than the rule, typically available only through CMBS conduits, agency programs (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac for multifamily), and life insurance company lenders.

How Personal Liability Works

Personal liability in a recourse loan is established through a guaranty agreement, which is a separate legal document from the mortgage and promissory note. The guaranty creates a direct obligation between the guarantor (usually the borrower or a principal of the borrowing entity) and the lender.

When a borrower forms an LLC or corporation to hold real estate (which is standard practice), the entity signs the loan documents and holds title to the property. Without a personal guaranty, the lender's recourse would be limited to the assets of that entity. The guaranty bridges this gap by making one or more individuals personally responsible for the entity's debt obligations.

What Lenders Can Pursue

Under a full recourse guaranty, the lender can pursue virtually all non-exempt personal assets of the guarantor. This includes:

  • Personal bank accounts and investment portfolios
  • Other real estate holdings (including the guarantor's personal residence in many states)
  • Business interests and ownership stakes in other entities
  • Vehicles, equipment, and other tangible personal property
  • Future income through wage garnishment (where permitted by state law)

Certain assets are protected from creditors under federal and state law. Qualified retirement accounts (401k, IRA) are generally exempt under federal law. Homestead exemptions vary dramatically by state: Texas and Florida offer unlimited homestead protection, while other states cap the exemption at much lower amounts. These protections apply regardless of the guaranty language, but they vary significantly by jurisdiction.

Which Loan Types Are Typically Recourse

The recourse structure of a loan depends primarily on the lender type and loan program. The following table summarizes typical recourse requirements across major commercial loan categories:

Loan Type Recourse Structure Typical LTV Rate Range
Bank / Credit Union Full recourse (standard) 70% to 80% 6.0% to 8.0%
SBA 504 Full recourse with SBA guaranty Up to 90% 6.5% to 8.5%
SBA 7(a) Full recourse; all 20%+ owners must guaranty 85% to 90% 7.0% to 9.5%
Bridge Loans Full or partial recourse 65% to 80% 8.0% to 12.0%
CMBS Non-recourse (with carve-outs) 65% to 75% 6.5% to 8.0%

Life Company Non-recourse (with carve-outs) 60% to 65% 5.0% to 7.0%

As the table shows, recourse loans generally offer higher leverage and more competitive rates. The lender accepts the personal guaranty as additional security, which allows them to take on more risk at the property level. Non-recourse lenders compensate for the lack of personal liability by requiring lower LTV, higher debt service coverage, and stricter underwriting standards.

Advantages of Recourse Loans

While the idea of personal liability may sound unappealing, recourse loans offer several meaningful advantages that make them the right choice for many borrowers:

Lower Interest Rates

Recourse loans consistently price 25 to 75 basis points below comparable non-recourse options. The personal guaranty reduces the lender's risk, and they pass part of that benefit to the borrower through lower rates. On a $3,000,000 loan, a 50 basis point rate reduction saves $15,000 per year in interest expense.

Higher Leverage

Bank lenders offering recourse loans typically go to 75% or 80% LTV, while non-recourse CMBS programs cap at 65% to 75%. SBA programs with full recourse reach 90% LTV. This higher leverage means less cash equity required at closing, preserving capital for renovations, operating reserves, or additional investments.

Easier Qualification

Non-recourse lenders focus almost exclusively on the property's income and condition. Recourse lenders also consider the borrower's overall financial strength, which means a strong personal balance sheet can offset weakness in the property's financials. First-time investors, borrowers with transitional properties, and those with smaller deals often find recourse lenders more accessible.

Faster Processing

Bank loans with recourse typically close in 30 to 60 days. CMBS and agency non-recourse loans can take 60 to 120 days. For borrowers working on tight acquisition timelines, the speed advantage of recourse financing can be decisive.

More Flexible Terms

Recourse lenders (especially community banks) offer more flexibility on prepayment penalties, interest-only periods, loan modifications, and workout arrangements. Non-recourse CMBS loans are securitized and serviced by third parties, making modifications extremely difficult.

Guarantor Requirements

Lenders evaluate potential guarantors based on several criteria. Meeting these requirements is essential for loan approval when recourse is required.

Net Worth

Most lenders require the guarantor's net worth (total assets minus total liabilities) to equal or exceed the loan amount. For a $2,000,000 loan, the guarantor should demonstrate at least $2,000,000 in net worth. Some lenders accept combined net worth from multiple guarantors.

Liquidity

Beyond total net worth, lenders want to see liquid assets (cash, publicly traded securities, and readily accessible funds) equal to 10% to 20% of the loan amount. On a $2,000,000 loan, that means $200,000 to $400,000 in liquid reserves post-closing. This ensures the guarantor can cover unexpected shortfalls without being forced to sell illiquid assets.

Credit Score

Most commercial lenders require guarantors to have a minimum FICO score of 660 to 680, though some programs require 700 or higher. SBA loans typically require a minimum of 680. Credit score affects not just approval but also pricing, with higher scores unlocking better rates.

Experience

While not strictly a guarantor requirement, the borrower's experience in commercial real estate directly affects the lender's willingness to offer favorable recourse terms. Experienced borrowers with a track record of successful projects may qualify for limited or partial recourse, while first-time borrowers almost always face full recourse requirements.

Spousal Guarantees

One of the most frequently misunderstood aspects of recourse lending is the spousal guaranty requirement. In community property states, lenders routinely require the guarantor's spouse to co-sign the guaranty agreement, even if the spouse has no involvement in the property or business.

Community Property States

The following states follow community property law: Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin. In these states, most assets and income acquired during the marriage are considered jointly owned. A guaranty signed by only one spouse may not be enforceable against community property assets, so lenders require both signatures to protect their claim.

Practical Implications

Spousal guaranty requirements can create friction in the loan process, particularly when spouses are uncomfortable with the liability exposure. Some strategies to address this include:

  • Discussing guaranty requirements early in the loan process so there are no surprises at closing
  • Working with an attorney to understand the specific protections and exposures under your state's community property laws
  • Considering prenuptial or postnuptial agreements that may affect how community property is treated (consult a family law attorney)
  • Exploring non-recourse loan options if spousal guaranty is a dealbreaker

Default Consequences Under Recourse

Understanding what happens when a recourse loan goes into default is critical for any borrower signing a personal guaranty. The process typically follows several stages.

Stage 1: Default and Acceleration

When the borrower misses payments or violates loan covenants, the lender issues a notice of default. After a cure period (typically 30 to 60 days), the lender can accelerate the loan, demanding full repayment of the outstanding balance immediately.

Stage 2: Foreclosure

If the borrower cannot repay or negotiate a workout, the lender initiates foreclosure proceedings. This process varies by state (judicial vs. non-judicial foreclosure) and can take anywhere from 60 days to over a year. The property is sold at auction or through a negotiated sale.

Stage 3: Deficiency Judgment

If the foreclosure sale proceeds do not fully cover the outstanding loan balance, interest, penalties, and costs, the lender can pursue a deficiency judgment against the guarantor. This is the stage where the recourse guaranty becomes directly relevant. The lender files a lawsuit against the guarantor personally, seeking to recover the shortfall from their personal assets.

Stage 4: Collection

With a deficiency judgment in hand, the lender (or a debt buyer who purchases the judgment) can use various collection methods including bank account levies, property liens, and wage garnishment. Deficiency judgments are typically enforceable for 10 to 20 years depending on state law, and they can often be renewed.

Negotiating Recourse Terms

While most commercial lenders require recourse, the specific terms of the guaranty are often negotiable. Experienced borrowers regularly negotiate modifications that limit their personal exposure.

Guaranty Burnoff

A burnoff provision reduces the guaranty amount over time, typically tied to the loan balance or a predetermined schedule. For example, a guaranty might start at 100% of the loan amount and burn off by 25% per year, reaching zero after four years. Some burnoff provisions are tied to performance milestones such as achieving a target DSCR or occupancy level.

Limited or Partial Guaranty

Instead of guaranteeing the full loan amount, the guarantor might be liable for only a portion. A 50% guaranty on a $2,000,000 loan limits personal exposure to $1,000,000. Some lenders offer a declining balance guaranty where the guaranteed amount decreases as the principal is paid down.

Carve-Out Only Guaranty

This is technically a non-recourse structure with bad boy carve-outs. The guarantor is not liable for standard loan default but becomes personally liable if specific "bad acts" occur, such as fraud, voluntary bankruptcy, or misappropriation of funds. This structure is standard in CMBS and agency lending but can sometimes be negotiated with bank lenders as well.

Tips for Negotiation

  • Start the conversation early. Raise recourse terms during the term sheet stage, not at closing.
  • Demonstrate strong property fundamentals. A well-performing property with stable income reduces the lender's perceived risk.
  • Show a strong personal balance sheet. Guarantors with substantial net worth and liquidity have more negotiating leverage.
  • Offer relationship value. Borrowers with multiple loans or deposits at the same bank can use that relationship as leverage.
  • Get competing term sheets. Nothing motivates a lender to improve terms like a competitive offer from another institution.

Recourse vs Non-Recourse: Choosing the Right Structure

The decision between recourse and non-recourse financing depends on several factors including deal size, borrower risk tolerance, property quality, and available capital.

Recourse financing is typically the better choice when you need higher leverage, want lower rates, are working with a smaller loan (under $3,000,000), need fast closing, or have a strong personal balance sheet that makes the guaranty a manageable risk. Many experienced investors willingly accept recourse on value-add projects where they plan to refinance into non-recourse permanent financing once the property is stabilized.

Non-recourse financing makes more sense for larger, stabilized assets where protecting personal wealth is the priority. Investors building a large portfolio of leveraged properties often prefer non-recourse to limit the cascading risk of one default triggering financial problems across their entire portfolio.
