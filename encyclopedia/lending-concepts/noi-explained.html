Net Operating Income (NOI): How Commercial Properties Are Valued and Underwritten

Net Operating Income is the single most important number in commercial real estate. It drives property valuations, determines loan sizing, and tells lenders whether your deal works.

Key Facts at a Glance

Definition Revenue minus operating expenses
Formula Effective Gross Income minus Operating Expenses
Excludes Debt service, depreciation, capex, income taxes
Primary Use Property valuation and loan underwriting
Key Calculations Used in DSCR, cap rate, and debt yield
Trailing vs Pro Forma Lenders prefer actual T12 data

What NOI Means

Net Operating Income (NOI) is the income a commercial property generates after paying all operating expenses but before paying debt service (mortgage payments), capital expenditures, depreciation, or income taxes. It is the single most important number in commercial real estate because it drives three critical metrics that lenders and investors rely on: Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR), capitalization rate (cap rate), and debt yield.

Every property valuation, every loan sizing decision, and every investment analysis begins with NOI. When a lender says a property is "worth" a certain amount, they arrived at that figure by dividing NOI by a market cap rate. When they determine how large a loan to offer, they calculate how much debt service the NOI can support at their minimum DSCR threshold. NOI is the foundation upon which the entire commercial real estate finance system is built.

A critical distinction: NOI is a property-level metric, not an owner-level metric. It measures how the property itself performs operationally, regardless of who owns it or how it is financed. Two investors can own identical properties with the same NOI but have completely different mortgage payments, tax situations, and cash flow. NOI strips away all of those owner-specific variables to isolate the property's raw earning power. This makes NOI the universal language for comparing commercial properties across markets, property types, and ownership structures.

How to Calculate NOI Step by Step

The NOI calculation follows a logical sequence. Each step builds on the previous one, starting with the maximum possible income and working down to the net figure.

Step 1: Gross Potential Rental Income (GPI)

Start with the total rental income the property would generate if every unit or space were leased at current market rents with 100% occupancy. This represents the theoretical maximum. For an apartment building, multiply the number of units by their respective market rents. For a commercial property, multiply the leasable square footage by the market rent per square foot.

Step 2: Minus Vacancy and Credit Loss

No property maintains 100% occupancy at all times. Subtract an allowance for vacancy (periods when units are empty between tenants) and credit loss (tenants who fail to pay rent). This deduction typically ranges from 5% to 10% of gross potential income, depending on the market, property type, and historical performance. Lenders apply this deduction even when a property is currently fully occupied because future vacancy is inevitable.

Step 3: Plus Other Income

Add all non-rental income the property generates. Common sources include parking fees, laundry room revenue, pet fees and pet rent, storage unit rentals, late fees, application fees, vending machines, and billboard or antenna lease income. While individually small, these income streams can add meaningful dollars to the bottom line.

Step 4: Equals Effective Gross Income (EGI)

The result of the first three steps is Effective Gross Income, the realistic top-line revenue the property produces. EGI is the starting point for calculating NOI and is itself an important metric that lenders scrutinize closely.

Step 5: Minus Operating Expenses

Subtract all costs required to operate and maintain the property on a day-to-day basis. This includes property management fees, real estate taxes, property insurance, utilities (when paid by the landlord), maintenance and repairs, landscaping, snow removal, general and administrative costs, marketing and leasing expenses, and replacement reserves. Importantly, operating expenses do not include mortgage payments, capital expenditures, depreciation, or income taxes.

Step 6: Equals NOI

The final result is Net Operating Income: the property's annual earnings from operations before any financing costs or capital spending.

NOI Formula

NOI = Effective Gross Income (EGI) - Operating Expenses

Worked Example: 40-Unit Apartment Building

Consider a 40-unit apartment building where the average market rent is $1,250 per unit per month:

  • Gross Potential Rental Income: 40 units x $1,250/month x 12 months = $600,000
  • Vacancy and Credit Loss (7%): minus $42,000
  • Other Income (laundry, parking, pet fees, storage): plus $28,000
  • Effective Gross Income (EGI): $586,000
  • Operating Expenses (42% of EGI): minus $246,120
NOI Calculation

NOI = $586,000 - $246,120 = $339,880

This property generates $339,880 in annual NOI. At a 6.5% cap rate, the implied property value would be approximately $5,229,000. If a lender requires a 1.25x DSCR, the maximum annual debt service would be $271,904, which supports a loan of roughly $3,400,000 at 6.5% interest with a 30-year amortization.

What Is Included and Excluded from NOI

Understanding exactly which items belong in the NOI calculation is essential. Misclassifying even one line item can significantly change the result and lead to incorrect valuations or loan sizing.

Included in Operating Expenses (Deducted from Income)

  • Property management fees: Typically 4% to 8% of gross income. Lenders include this even if the owner self-manages.
  • Real estate taxes: Annual property taxes assessed by the local taxing authority.
  • Property insurance: Hazard, liability, and flood insurance premiums.
  • Utilities (if landlord-paid): Water, sewer, electric, gas, and trash removal when paid by the property owner rather than tenants.
  • Maintenance and repairs: Routine upkeep including plumbing, electrical, appliance repair, painting, and general building maintenance.
  • Landscaping and snow removal: Grounds maintenance and seasonal services.
  • General and administrative: Accounting, legal, office supplies, and software for property management.
  • Marketing and leasing costs: Advertising, signage, listing fees, and leasing agent commissions for renewals.
  • Replacement reserves: An annual set-aside (typically $250 to $500 per unit for multifamily) to fund future capital replacements.

Excluded from NOI

  • Mortgage payments (principal and interest): Debt service is a financing cost, not an operating cost.
  • Capital expenditures: Major replacements such as a new roof, HVAC system replacement, parking lot repaving, or elevator modernization.
  • Depreciation and amortization: Non-cash accounting entries that do not represent actual operating expenses.
  • Income taxes: The property owner's federal and state income tax obligations.
  • Owner draws or distributions: Payments to the property owner or investors.
  • Tenant improvement allowances (TIs): One-time buildout costs for new tenants in commercial spaces.
  • Leasing commissions: Fees paid to brokers for securing new tenants (distinct from routine marketing costs).

Effective Gross Income (EGI) Calculation

Effective Gross Income is the "top line" that feeds into the NOI calculation, and lenders pay close attention to how it is derived. EGI represents the realistic revenue a property will produce after accounting for the fact that not every unit will be occupied every month and not every tenant will pay on time.

The vacancy assumption is one of the most important judgment calls in the entire underwriting process. Even when a property is 100% occupied at the time of loan application, lenders will apply a vacancy factor because tenants move out, leases expire, and economic conditions change. The vacancy assumption varies by property type and market conditions:

  • Multifamily (strong market): 5% to 7%
  • Multifamily (average market): 7% to 10%
  • Office: 10% to 15% (higher in markets with elevated remote work adoption)
  • Retail: 5% to 10% (depending on tenant credit quality and lease terms)
  • Industrial: 5% to 7% (strong demand keeps vacancy low)
  • Hospitality: Occupancy varies seasonally; lenders use average annual occupancy rates rather than a standard vacancy percentage

Lenders will also scrutinize the "other income" component of EGI. They want to see that ancillary income sources are stable, documented, and likely to continue. One-time income (such as a large insurance settlement or an unusual late fee windfall) will be stripped out. Recurring income from parking, laundry, and storage is given full credit.

Typical Operating Expense Categories and Ratios

Operating expense ratios, expressed as a percentage of Effective Gross Income, vary significantly by property type. Understanding the typical range helps borrowers assess whether their property is operating efficiently and helps flag anomalies that lenders will question.

Property Type Typical Expense Ratio Key Driver
Multifamily 35% to 50% of EGI Management intensity, utilities, unit turnover
Office (full service) 40% to 55% Landlord pays most utilities and common area maintenance
Retail (NNN) 10% to 20% Most expenses passed through to tenants
Industrial 15% to 25% Low maintenance, tenants handle most costs
Hospitality 60% to 75% Labor-intensive operations, daily housekeeping, food service

Triple net (NNN) leases produce the lowest expense ratios because tenants are contractually responsible for paying real estate taxes, property insurance, and common area maintenance in addition to base rent. The landlord's remaining expenses are minimal, often limited to management fees and structural reserves. This is why NNN-leased properties are popular with investors who want predictable, low-maintenance income streams.

At the other end of the spectrum, hospitality properties carry the highest expense ratios because they require daily staffing, housekeeping, food and beverage operations, and constant guest-facing services. A hotel generating $2 million in revenue may only produce $500,000 to $800,000 in NOI after all operating costs.

Trailing 12-Month (T12) vs Pro Forma NOI

The distinction between trailing and projected NOI is one of the most consequential topics in commercial real estate underwriting. The type of NOI a lender uses directly affects the loan amount, interest rate, and loan structure you will receive.

Trailing 12-Month (T12) NOI

T12 NOI is based on actual income and expenses from the prior 12 months. It reflects real performance, supported by bank statements, rent rolls, and operating statements. Lenders strongly prefer T12 data because it eliminates guesswork. When a property has a documented track record of generating $350,000 in NOI, the lender has high confidence that it will continue to perform at a similar level. Most conventional lenders (banks, CMBS, agency, and life companies) underwrite primarily to T12 figures.

Pro Forma NOI

Pro forma NOI is a projection of what the property will earn in the future, based on assumptions about rent increases, occupancy improvements, and expense reductions. Bridge lenders and value-add investors rely on pro forma NOI because the property has not yet reached its stabilized potential. For example, an investor purchasing a 60%-occupied apartment building plans to renovate units and raise rents. The current T12 NOI might be $150,000, but the stabilized pro forma NOI after renovation could be $350,000. A bridge lender will underwrite to the stabilized figure (with significant haircuts) to fund the business plan.

Other Methods Lenders Use

Beyond T12 and pro forma, lenders employ additional methods to analyze income:

  • Annualized NOI: Takes the most recent three to six months and annualizes the result. Useful when the property has undergone recent changes that make the full T12 less representative.
  • T3 (Trailing Three-Month): Examines only the most recent quarter, then annualizes. Lenders use this alongside T12 to spot trends, both positive and negative.
  • Rent roll analysis: Instead of relying solely on historical financials, the lender builds NOI from the current rent roll (the list of all tenants, their lease terms, and their rents). This catches recent lease expirations or new leases that may not be fully reflected in the T12.

In practice, most lenders use a combination of these methods. They will start with the T12, cross-reference it against the current rent roll, check for trends in the T3, and then apply their own adjustments to arrive at an underwritten NOI figure.

How Lenders Analyze NOI for Underwriting

Lenders do not simply accept the borrower's stated NOI at face value. They perform their own analysis and make adjustments to arrive at what is known as "underwritten NOI." This figure is almost always lower than the borrower's reported NOI because lenders build in conservative assumptions.

Common lender adjustments include:

  • Adding management fees: If the property is self-managed, the lender will add a management fee (typically 4% to 8% of gross income) to operating expenses. The logic is simple: if the owner stops managing the property, professional management will be needed, and the property must be able to support that cost.
  • Applying market vacancy: Even if the property is 100% occupied, the lender applies a vacancy factor based on the market and property type. This protects against future income loss.
  • Normalizing one-time expenses: If the T12 includes an unusually large expense (such as a one-time legal settlement or emergency repair), the lender may remove it to get a more representative expense figure. Conversely, if the T12 is missing a normal expense category, the lender will add it back.
  • Adjusting above-market rents: If a tenant is paying significantly above market rent on a lease that expires within the loan term, the lender may underwrite that unit at market rent rather than the current inflated figure.
  • Including replacement reserves: Even if the borrower's operating statement does not include a line item for reserves, the lender will deduct $250 to $500 per unit (for multifamily) or a percentage of income for other property types.

The difference between "in-place NOI" (what the property currently earns based on the rent roll and actual expenses) and "underwritten NOI" (the lender's adjusted figure) can be significant. Borrowers should expect the lender's underwritten NOI to be 5% to 15% lower than their own calculation. Understanding this gap before you apply helps set realistic expectations for loan sizing.

NOI Growth Strategies

Because NOI directly drives property value and loan capacity, even modest increases in NOI can have an outsized impact. At a 6% cap rate, every additional $10,000 of NOI adds approximately $167,000 to the property's value. Here are proven strategies to grow NOI:

  1. Raise rents to market: Conduct a market survey to identify units or spaces rented below prevailing rates. Implement increases at lease renewal. Even $50 per month per unit across 40 units adds $24,000 to annual NOI.
  2. Add ancillary income streams: Introduce or expand revenue from parking, storage units, laundry facilities, pet rent, covered parking premiums, and vending machines. These income sources typically carry zero additional operating expense.
  3. Reduce vacancy through better marketing and tenant retention: Lower turnover reduces lost rent during vacant periods and saves on make-ready costs. Invest in tenant satisfaction, responsive maintenance, and competitive renewal offers.
  4. Implement utility submetering (RUBS): A Ratio Utility Billing System allocates water, sewer, and trash costs to individual tenants based on unit size or occupancy. This shifts utility expenses from the landlord to tenants, directly reducing operating expenses and increasing NOI.
  5. Challenge property tax assessments: Property taxes are often the single largest operating expense. If your assessed value exceeds the property's actual market value, file an appeal. Successful appeals can reduce annual tax bills by thousands of dollars.
  6. Competitively bid insurance and vendor contracts: Obtain multiple quotes for property insurance, landscaping, snow removal, and maintenance contracts every two to three years. Market pricing shifts, and loyal customers often overpay.
  7. Convert units to higher use: Combine small units into larger ones that command premium rents, convert standard units to furnished or corporate housing, or explore short-term rental opportunities where regulations permit.
  8. Add billback programs for utilities: Beyond RUBS, install individual meters for electric, gas, or water and bill tenants directly for their consumption. This is particularly effective in properties where the landlord currently pays all utilities.
  9. Upgrade amenities to justify rent premiums: Adding in-unit washers and dryers, modern appliances, smart home features, or community amenities (fitness center, package lockers, coworking space) can support rent increases that exceed the cost of the improvements.

Common Mistakes Borrowers Make with NOI

Submitting an inaccurate or poorly supported NOI calculation to a lender creates problems that range from loan denial to mid-process surprises that delay closing. Avoid these common errors:

  • Forgetting to include management fees (even if self-managed): This is the single most common mistake. Lenders will add management fees regardless, so leaving them out of your presentation makes it appear that you do not understand underwriting standards. Always include a management fee of 4% to 8% of gross income.
  • Using pro forma when the lender wants T12: Submitting projected income and expenses to a conventional lender who requires trailing actuals wastes everyone's time. Before preparing your loan package, confirm which type of NOI the lender requires.
  • Excluding replacement reserves: Replacement reserves ($250 to $500 per unit for multifamily) are a standard lender deduction. If your NOI calculation omits them, the lender will add them back, reducing your NOI and potentially disqualifying your deal.
  • Not normalizing one-time expenses: Including a large one-time repair cost inflates expenses and depresses NOI. Conversely, if you had unusually low expenses in a given year (for example, you deferred maintenance), the lender will add back normal expense levels. Present a normalized expense picture.
  • Using gross rent instead of effective gross income: Gross rent ignores vacancy, credit loss, and other income. Always calculate EGI properly before deducting operating expenses. Presenting gross rent as if it were EGI overstates the property's income.
  • Double-counting income sources: Including laundry income in both the rent roll and the "other income" category is a common bookkeeping error that lenders will catch immediately. Audit your income categories to ensure each source appears exactly once.
  • Underestimating vacancy in a softening market: If your market is experiencing rising vacancy or new supply coming online, using a 5% vacancy factor based on historical performance may not be realistic. Lenders track local market conditions and will apply higher vacancy assumptions if the data supports it.
