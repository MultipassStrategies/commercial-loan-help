Defeasance in Commercial Real Estate: Process, Costs, and When It Makes Sense

Defeasance is the most common way to exit a CMBS loan before maturity. It is also one of the most misunderstood processes in commercial real estate finance. Here is how it works, what it costs, and when it makes financial sense.

Key Facts at a Glance

Definition Replacing property collateral with government securities to release a mortgage
Process Purchase Treasury securities that replicate the loan's remaining payment stream
Common In CMBS loans, some conduit and securitized products
Cost Components Securities purchase, legal fees, accountant, servicer fees, consultant
Timeline 30 to 60 days from start to completion
Requires A specialized defeasance consultant to manage the process

What Is Defeasance and How Does It Work?

Defeasance is a process that allows a borrower to release commercial property from a mortgage lien without paying off the loan early. Instead of making a lump-sum prepayment, the borrower purchases a portfolio of U.S. Treasury securities whose cash flows exactly match the remaining scheduled loan payments, including both principal and interest. These securities replace the property as collateral for the loan.

After the substitution, the loan continues to exist on paper. A successor borrower (typically a special-purpose entity) takes over as the nominal borrower, and the Treasury securities automatically generate the payments needed to service the debt through the original maturity date. The property is released from the mortgage lien, freeing the owner to sell, refinance, or otherwise use the property without restriction.

The lender (or, more accurately, the bondholders in a CMBS securitization) does not care whether payments come from rental income or government securities. As long as the payment stream remains intact and the credit quality of the collateral is maintained, the trust is satisfied. U.S. Treasury securities actually represent a credit upgrade over any commercial property, which is why defeasance is permitted under CMBS pooling and servicing agreements.

Defeasance exists because CMBS loans are packaged into securities and sold to investors who expect a specific, predictable cash flow. Simple prepayment would disrupt that cash flow and potentially harm bondholders. Defeasance solves this by maintaining the payment stream while giving the borrower a way to exit the loan and free the property.

The Defeasance Process Step by Step

Defeasance involves multiple parties and a carefully sequenced series of steps:

  1. Notify the loan servicer of intent to defease. Review your loan documents for the required notice period, which is typically 30 days. The servicer will confirm that defeasance is permitted and provide the procedural requirements.
  2. Engage a defeasance consultant. The consultant manages the entire process, coordinating between all parties. They review your loan documents, identify the specific defeasance requirements, and create a timeline for completion.
  3. Calculate the Treasury securities portfolio. The consultant's team determines the exact combination of U.S. Treasury securities needed to replicate every remaining loan payment (principal and interest) through maturity. This calculation is precise to the penny and accounts for payment dates, day-count conventions, and any balloon payment.
  4. Prepare defeasance documents. Legal counsel for both the borrower and the lender drafts the defeasance agreement, successor borrower documents, and security assignment agreements. The servicer's counsel also reviews all documents for compliance with the pooling and servicing agreement.
  5. Independent accountant verification. A certified public accountant independently verifies that the proposed Treasury securities portfolio will generate cash flows sufficient to cover all remaining loan payments.
  6. Close the defeasance transaction. On the closing date, the borrower purchases the Treasury securities through a securities broker, deposits them with the successor borrower (a special-purpose entity), and executes all closing documents. The securities are pledged as collateral, replacing the property.
  7. Release the mortgage lien. Once the servicer confirms that all defeasance conditions have been satisfied, it releases the mortgage lien. The borrower receives a recorded satisfaction or release of mortgage, and the property is free and clear.
  8. Securities make remaining payments automatically. From closing day forward, the Treasury securities generate the cash flows used to make remaining loan payments. The successor borrower holds nominal title, and the securities pay down the debt through the original maturity date.

Cost Components of Defeasance

Defeasance costs fall into two categories: fixed professional fees and the variable securities purchase price. The fixed fees remain relatively consistent regardless of loan size, while the securities cost depends on the loan balance, remaining term, and current interest rate environment.

Cost Component Typical Range Description
Securities Purchase Varies (largest cost) Present value of remaining loan payments; depends on rate environment
Legal Fees $15,000 to $30,000 Borrower's counsel and lender's counsel
Defeasance Consultant Fee $10,000 to $15,000 Project management, securities portfolio calculation, closing coordination
Accountant Verification $5,000 to $10,000 Independent CPA verification of securities cash flow sufficiency
Servicer Processing Fee $5,000 to $25,000 Master servicer and special servicer review and processing
Rating Agency Fee (if applicable) $5,000 to $15,000 Required for some CMBS transactions; not always applicable
Total Fixed Costs (excluding securities) $40,000 to $95,000 Professional fees regardless of loan size

The securities purchase price is by far the largest component. It is determined by the relationship between your loan's interest rate and current Treasury yields. If your loan carries a 5.50% coupon and the matching Treasury yields 4.00%, the borrower must invest a larger principal amount in lower-yielding securities to replicate the higher-coupon loan payments. Conversely, if Treasury yields have risen above your loan rate, the securities cost may be less than your outstanding balance.

When Is Defeasance Required vs. Optional?

The type of loan you have determines whether defeasance is your only exit option or one of several choices:

  • CMBS loans: Almost always require defeasance as the sole method for early loan exit. CMBS pooling and servicing agreements typically do not permit standard prepayment (except during the open window near maturity). If you want to sell or refinance a property with a CMBS loan before the open window, defeasance is your only path.
  • Agency loans (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac): Some agency loan programs offer borrowers a choice between defeasance and yield maintenance. The loan documents specify which options are available. Fannie Mae DUS loans, for example, commonly allow either defeasance or yield maintenance, giving borrowers the flexibility to choose the less expensive option based on market conditions at the time.
  • Life company loans: Rarely use defeasance. Life insurance company lenders typically require yield maintenance or a fixed prepayment penalty as the early exit mechanism. Their loan structures are simpler because these loans are generally held on balance sheet rather than securitized.
  • Bank loans: Almost never involve defeasance. Bank loans typically include yield maintenance, declining prepayment penalties (such as 5-4-3-2-1 step-down schedules), or a fixed penalty period followed by an open prepayment window. Defeasance is impractical for portfolio loans because the operational complexity is not warranted.

Defeasance Cost Estimation: A Worked Example

Understanding defeasance cost requires working through the numbers. Consider this scenario:

  • Loan balance: $5,000,000
  • Loan interest rate: 4.75%
  • Remaining term: 4 years
  • Current 4-year Treasury yield: 4.25%
  • Monthly payment (principal and interest): approximately $32,500

Because the loan rate (4.75%) is higher than the current Treasury yield (4.25%), the borrower must purchase Treasury securities with a face value greater than the remaining loan balance. The lower-yielding securities need a larger principal base to generate the same dollar amount of interest payments.

In simplified terms, the securities purchase cost works like this: each remaining loan payment (approximately $32,500 per month for 48 months, plus any balloon payment) must be matched by a Treasury security maturing on or before that payment date. Since each Treasury pays a lower coupon than the loan rate, the borrower pays a premium to acquire enough securities to cover the shortfall.

For this example, the estimated costs would be approximately:

  • Securities purchase premium over loan balance: approximately $50,000 to $75,000 (the difference driven by the 0.50% rate gap over 4 years)
  • Legal fees: $20,000
  • Defeasance consultant: $12,000
  • Accountant verification: $7,500
  • Servicer fee: $10,000
  • Total estimated defeasance cost (above loan balance): $99,500 to $124,500

If the rate gap were larger (for example, the loan rate at 5.50% and Treasuries at 3.50%), the securities premium would increase significantly. Conversely, if Treasury rates had risen to 5.00% or above the loan rate, the securities purchase cost could actually be less than the outstanding loan balance, making defeasance relatively inexpensive aside from the fixed fees.

Defeasance vs. Yield Maintenance: A Detailed Comparison

Defeasance and yield maintenance both serve the same purpose (compensating the lender for early loan termination), but they work very differently. Understanding the differences helps borrowers evaluate their options and anticipate costs.

Factor Defeasance Yield Maintenance
Process Replace collateral with Treasury securities Cash penalty payment to lender
Speed 30 to 60 days 10 to 30 days
Fixed Costs $40,000 to $95,000 Minimal (legal review only)
Variable Cost Securities purchase price Present value of rate differential
Common In CMBS loans Bank, life company loans
Complexity High (multiple parties involved) Low (calculation plus payment)
Loan After Completion Continues with securities as collateral Fully paid off and terminated

The most important practical difference is the fixed cost floor. Defeasance carries $40,000 to $95,000 in professional fees regardless of the rate environment, while yield maintenance can cost as little as a few hundred dollars in legal fees when rates have risen significantly. This means defeasance is almost always more expensive in a rising rate environment. In a falling rate environment, the variable cost component is similar for both, but defeasance still adds the fixed fee layer on top.

For borrowers who have a choice between the two (common with some agency loans), the decision often comes down to the rate environment. When rates have risen since origination, yield maintenance is typically the better choice. When rates have fallen, total cost may be comparable, but yield maintenance is still faster and simpler.

When Does Defeasance Make Financial Sense?

Defeasance is rarely optional for CMBS borrowers who need to sell or refinance. The real question is whether the transaction that triggers defeasance generates enough value to justify the cost. Here are the key considerations:

  • Property sale: The sale price must be high enough to cover the outstanding loan balance, total defeasance costs, transaction costs (broker commissions, transfer taxes), and still deliver an acceptable return to the seller. If the property has appreciated significantly, the defeasance cost is typically a manageable line item in the transaction.
  • Refinancing: The benefit of the new loan (lower rate, cash-out proceeds, better terms) must exceed the defeasance cost. Borrowers refinancing to a lower interest rate may find that the present value of interest savings over the new loan term exceeds the one-time defeasance expense.
  • Opportunity cost: Consider what happens if you wait. If the loan has an open prepayment window approaching (typically the last 3 to 6 months before maturity), waiting may eliminate the defeasance cost entirely. However, waiting also means potentially missing a favorable sale or refinance opportunity.
  • Rate environment: In a rising rate environment, defeasance becomes less expensive on the securities side (because higher-yielding Treasuries require less principal to replicate your loan payments). Monitor Treasury rates relative to your loan rate when timing the transaction.

A useful rule of thumb: if the defeasance cost represents less than 2% to 3% of the property value, it is usually manageable within a sale or refinance transaction. When the cost exceeds 5% of property value, borrowers should carefully evaluate whether waiting for the open window or finding alternative strategies makes more sense.

Common CMBS Defeasance Provisions

Every CMBS loan has specific defeasance provisions outlined in the loan agreement and the pooling and servicing agreement. While the details vary, most CMBS loans include these standard features:

  • Lockout period: Most CMBS loans prohibit defeasance during the first 2 years of the loan term (sometimes longer). During this period, the borrower cannot exit the loan by any means. The lockout protects bondholders by ensuring a minimum period of predictable cash flow from the actual property collateral.
  • Defeasance window: After the lockout period expires and before the open window begins, defeasance is the permitted exit mechanism. This window typically spans the middle years of the loan term.
  • Open prepayment window: Most CMBS loans allow penalty-free prepayment during the last 3 to 6 months before maturity. During this window, the borrower can simply pay off the loan without defeasance or any prepayment penalty. If your disposition or refinance timeline allows, waiting for this window can save significant money.
  • Partial defeasance: For CMBS loans secured by multiple properties (portfolio loans or cross-collateralized structures), partial defeasance allows the borrower to release individual properties by defeasing the allocated portion of the debt. This is more complex than full defeasance and typically requires a release price premium of 110% to 125% of the allocated loan balance. The remaining portfolio must still meet debt service coverage and loan-to-value tests after the release.
  • Successor borrower requirements: The loan documents specify what type of entity can serve as the successor borrower. Typically, this must be a newly created single-purpose, bankruptcy-remote entity that has no assets other than the defeased loan and the pledged securities.

Timing Considerations for Defeasance

Timing can significantly impact both the cost and the smoothness of the defeasance process. Here are the factors that borrowers should plan around:

  • Interest rate environment: The cost of the Treasury securities portfolio fluctuates daily with Treasury yields. A borrower who locks in securities pricing on a day when Treasury yields spike could save tens of thousands of dollars compared to a day when yields drop. Some defeasance consultants offer rate-lock services that allow borrowers to fix the securities cost in advance of closing.
  • Holiday and market closures: Treasury markets are closed on federal holidays and weekends. If your target closing date falls near a holiday period (Thanksgiving, Christmas, New Year's), build in extra buffer time. Securities cannot be purchased or settled when markets are closed, which can delay the entire transaction.
  • Servicer processing times: Loan servicers have their own review timelines and may be processing multiple defeasance requests simultaneously. Large special servicers may take longer during busy periods. Factor in at least 2 to 3 weeks for servicer review of the defeasance package, beyond the initial 30-day notice period.
  • Coordination with property sale: If defeasance is triggered by a property sale, the defeasance closing date must align with the buyer's closing date. This requires careful coordination between the defeasance consultant, the seller's attorney, the buyer's attorney, and the title company. Build a timeline that works backward from the buyer's desired closing date, adding buffer for potential delays.
  • Month-end timing: Closing defeasance on or near a loan payment date simplifies the securities portfolio calculation because there is no need to account for accrued interest on a mid-period closing. Many defeasance consultants recommend targeting a closing date that coincides with a scheduled payment date when possible.
