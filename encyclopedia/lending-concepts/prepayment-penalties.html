Commercial Loan Prepayment Penalties: Types, Costs, and How to Negotiate

Prepayment penalties are one of the most misunderstood and costly provisions in commercial real estate loans. Understanding the four main types, and knowing when and how to negotiate them, can save you hundreds of thousands of dollars over the life of your loan.

Key Facts at a Glance

Main Types Yield Maintenance, Defeasance, Step-Down, Lockout
Why They Exist Protect lenders from lost interest income when borrowers prepay
Cost Range 1% to 30%+ of loan balance (varies dramatically by type)
Negotiability Often negotiable at loan origination
Impact Can make refinancing prohibitively expensive if not planned for

Why Commercial Loans Have Prepayment Penalties

Unlike most residential mortgages, nearly every commercial real estate loan carries some form of prepayment penalty. This is not an arbitrary fee. It exists because commercial lenders structure their business around earning a specific yield on their capital over a defined period of time.

When a lender originates a commercial loan at 6.5% for a 10-year term, they have committed capital at that rate and built their financial projections around receiving those interest payments for the full decade. If the borrower pays off the loan early, the lender must reinvest that capital, often at a lower rate, especially if market interest rates have declined since the loan was originated. This is known as reinvestment risk.

The problem is magnified in the secondary market. Many commercial loans, particularly CMBS (Commercial Mortgage-Backed Securities) loans, are packaged and sold to investors who have purchased bonds backed by a pool of mortgages. These investors paid a price based on the expected cash flows from those loans. When borrowers prepay, it disrupts those cash flows and reduces investor returns. Prepayment penalties exist to compensate for this disruption, either by making the lender whole financially or by discouraging prepayment entirely.

For borrowers, the practical implication is significant. You cannot simply pay off a commercial loan whenever you want without financial consequences. Whether you are selling the property, refinancing for a better rate, or pulling out equity, the prepayment penalty must factor into your decision. In some cases, the penalty cost exceeds the benefit of the transaction, making it better to wait.

Yield Maintenance: How It Works and What It Costs

Yield maintenance is the most common prepayment penalty in conventional commercial loans and agency (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac) financing. The concept is straightforward: the borrower must pay the lender enough money to make them whole, as if the loan had continued to maturity.

The calculation works by determining the present value of the remaining loan payments, discounted at the current Treasury rate for a comparable maturity. The key variable is the difference between your loan's interest rate and the current Treasury rate at the time of prepayment.

Simplified Yield Maintenance Formula

Yield Maintenance = Present Value of (Loan Rate - Treasury Rate) x Remaining Payments

Here is why this matters in practice. When interest rates have fallen since you originated your loan, yield maintenance becomes very expensive. If you locked in a 6.5% rate and the comparable Treasury is now at 3.5%, the lender is losing 3% per year on every dollar for each remaining year. On a $5 million loan with 7 years remaining, the penalty could easily exceed $750,000, or roughly 15% of the loan balance.

Conversely, when rates have risen above your loan rate, yield maintenance can be surprisingly cheap. If current Treasury rates exceed your loan rate, the lender can reinvest at a higher return, so the formula may produce a minimal penalty (often just 1% of the balance or a nominal fee, depending on the loan documents).

This rate-dependent nature makes yield maintenance unpredictable. Borrowers who originated loans during low-rate periods and then saw rates rise may find their yield maintenance penalty is negligible. Those who locked in during higher-rate periods and watch rates fall may face enormous penalties. The timing of your exit is as important as the terms you negotiated at origination.

Defeasance: Process, Costs, and Securities Matching

Defeasance is the most complex and often the most expensive form of prepayment penalty. Rather than simply paying a fee to the lender, defeasance requires you to replace the property as collateral with a portfolio of U.S. government securities (typically Treasury bonds) that replicate the exact cash flows of your remaining loan payments.

The process works as follows. When you want to prepay a loan with a defeasance requirement, you do not actually pay off the loan. Instead, you purchase a carefully selected portfolio of Treasury securities whose interest and principal payments match your remaining loan payments dollar-for-dollar, month-by-month, through the maturity date. These securities are placed in a trust, and the trust makes the loan payments going forward. Your property is released from the mortgage, allowing you to sell or refinance freely.

Defeasance is most common in CMBS loans because securitized loan pools require uninterrupted cash flows to the bondholders. Paying off the loan would disrupt these flows, but defeasance maintains them perfectly by substituting one source of payments (your property's income) with another (Treasury securities).

The cost of defeasance includes several components:

  • Securities purchase: This is the largest cost. You must buy enough Treasury bonds to cover all remaining payments. In a low-rate environment, these bonds cost more because their yields are lower, so you need to purchase more bonds to generate the same cash flow. This component can range from 5% to 30% or more of the loan balance.
  • Defeasance consultant: A specialized firm manages the process, selects the securities, and coordinates with all parties. Fees typically range from $25,000 to $75,000.
  • Legal counsel: Both borrower and lender need attorneys to review and approve the transaction. Legal fees add $10,000 to $30,000.
  • Accountant: A CPA must verify the securities portfolio and provide a tax opinion. This adds $5,000 to $15,000.
  • Successor borrower: Some defeasance structures require a successor borrower entity to take over the defeased loan. This entity charges a fee, typically $5,000 to $10,000.

The total cost of defeasance, including all professional fees and the securities premium, commonly runs between 8% and 35% of the loan balance. The process itself takes 30 to 45 days from start to completion, which must be factored into any transaction timeline.

Step-Down Penalties: Predictable and Borrower-Friendly

Step-down penalties (also called declining prepayment penalties or graduated penalties) are the simplest and most predictable form of prepayment protection. The penalty is a fixed percentage of the outstanding loan balance that decreases, or "steps down," each year of the loan term.

The most common step-down schedule is 5-4-3-2-1, meaning you pay 5% of the remaining balance if you prepay in year one, 4% in year two, 3% in year three, 2% in year four, and 1% in year five. After year five, there is no penalty.

Loan Year 5-4-3-2-1 Schedule 3-2-1 Schedule
Year 1 5% of balance 3% of balance
Year 2 4% of balance 2% of balance
Year 3 3% of balance 1% of balance
Year 4 2% of balance No penalty
Year 5 1% of balance No penalty
Year 6+ No penalty No penalty

The primary advantage of step-down penalties is predictability. Unlike yield maintenance, which fluctuates with interest rates, a step-down penalty gives you a clear, calculable number at any point during the loan. If you have a $3 million loan balance and are in year three of a 5-4-3-2-1 schedule, your penalty is exactly $90,000 (3% of $3,000,000). There is no guesswork, no dependence on Treasury rates, and no complex financial modeling required.

Step-down penalties are most common in bank loans, credit union loans, and some life company loans. They are the preferred structure for borrowers who anticipate the possibility of an early exit, because the costs are transparent from day one. When comparing loan offers, a step-down penalty is almost always more favorable to the borrower than yield maintenance or defeasance, particularly in a falling-rate environment.

Lockout Periods: When Prepayment Is Not an Option

A lockout period is the most restrictive form of prepayment protection. During the lockout, the borrower is completely prohibited from prepaying the loan. There is no penalty amount you can pay to get out early; prepayment is simply not permitted at any cost.

Lockout periods are most common in CMBS loans, where they typically last for the first 2 to 3 years of the loan term. After the lockout expires, the loan transitions to a different form of prepayment protection (usually defeasance or yield maintenance) for the remainder of the term.

For example, a typical 10-year CMBS loan might have the following structure: a 2-year lockout (no prepayment allowed), followed by defeasance from years 3 through 9, followed by a penalty-free window in the final 3 to 6 months before maturity. The lockout ensures that investors receive cash flows for at least the first two years, providing stability for the securitized bond pool.

Bridge loans also frequently include lockout periods, though they are typically shorter (6 to 18 months). The lockout in bridge loans exists because the lender has structured the loan with specific return expectations, and a very early payoff would prevent them from earning a reasonable return on the origination costs and capital deployment.

The practical impact of a lockout is straightforward: if you are in a lockout period and need to sell the property or refinance, you cannot do so unless the new buyer assumes the existing loan (which is uncommon) or you wait until the lockout expires. This makes lockout provisions especially important to understand before signing the loan documents.

Penalty-Free Windows at Maturity

Nearly all commercial loans include a penalty-free window, sometimes called an "open period," during the final months before the loan matures. This window typically lasts 3 to 6 months and allows the borrower to pay off the loan without any prepayment penalty.

The penalty-free window exists for a practical reason. As the loan approaches maturity, the lender expects to be repaid. Allowing penalty-free prepayment in the final months gives the borrower time to arrange refinancing or close a sale without being penalized for paying off a few months before the exact maturity date.

For borrowers who know they will hold a property through most of the loan term, the penalty-free window is an important planning tool. If your 10-year loan matures in January 2030 and has a 6-month open period, you can begin the refinancing process in mid-2029 and close the new loan anytime after July 2029 without paying a penalty. This flexibility is valuable because refinancing closings rarely align perfectly with maturity dates.

When negotiating loan terms, always confirm the length of the penalty-free window. Some loans offer only 90 days, which can be tight for arranging a refinance, while others offer 6 months or even a full year. A wider penalty-free window gives you more flexibility and reduces the risk of being forced to accept unfavorable refinancing terms because of a tight deadline.

How Prepayment Penalties Affect Refinancing Decisions

Every commercial property owner will eventually face a decision about whether to pay a prepayment penalty to refinance or wait until the penalty-free window opens. This decision requires careful financial analysis.

The core question is simple: does the financial benefit of the new loan exceed the cost of the prepayment penalty? Consider this example. You have a $4 million loan at 7.25% with 3 years remaining and a step-down penalty of 2% ($80,000). You can refinance at 5.75%, saving approximately $60,000 per year in interest. Over the remaining 3 years, that adds up to $180,000 in savings, minus the $80,000 penalty, for a net benefit of $100,000. In this case, paying the penalty makes clear financial sense.

However, the analysis becomes more complex with yield maintenance or defeasance. If the same loan carried yield maintenance, the penalty might be $400,000 instead of $80,000, completely erasing the benefit. Each situation requires its own calculation.

Beyond the simple rate comparison, consider these additional factors:

  • Cash-out proceeds: If refinancing allows you to extract equity for a profitable investment, factor in the expected returns on that capital.
  • Loan structure improvements: A new loan might offer a longer amortization, interest-only period, or higher LTV that improves cash flow even if the rate savings alone do not justify the penalty.
  • Property disposition timeline: If you plan to sell the property before the current loan matures, refinancing now into a loan with a more favorable prepayment structure may be worth the current penalty cost.
  • Rate environment outlook: If rates are expected to rise further, locking in a lower rate now (even with a penalty) may prove wise over the remaining hold period.

Negotiating Prepayment Terms at Loan Origination

Loan origination is the single best opportunity to negotiate favorable prepayment terms. Once the loan is closed, your prepayment provisions are locked in for the entire term. Here are the most effective negotiation strategies:

  1. Request step-down instead of yield maintenance. If a lender initially offers yield maintenance, ask whether they will accept a step-down schedule instead. Many bank lenders will agree, especially if you are a strong borrower with multiple lending relationships.
  2. Negotiate a shorter lockout period. If the lender requires a lockout, push for the shortest period possible. Reducing a lockout from 3 years to 2 years gives you an extra year of exit flexibility.
  3. Expand the penalty-free window. Ask for a 6-month or 12-month penalty-free window instead of the standard 90 days. This small concession from the lender can make a major difference in your refinancing timeline.
  4. Cap the maximum penalty. For yield maintenance loans, negotiate a cap (for example, the greater of yield maintenance or 1%, but not to exceed 3% of the balance). This protects you from catastrophically high penalties in a sharply declining rate environment.
  5. Leverage competition. When you have multiple lenders competing for your deal, use competing term sheets to negotiate better prepayment terms. Lenders are most flexible when they know they could lose the deal.
  6. Consider the full picture. Sometimes a slightly higher interest rate from a lender offering step-down penalties is a better deal overall than a lower rate from a lender requiring defeasance, especially if you anticipate an early exit.

The key principle is that prepayment penalties are not fixed or non-negotiable. They are a business term that can be adjusted based on the borrower's negotiating position, the lender's appetite for the deal, and market conditions. Borrowers who accept prepayment terms without negotiation often leave significant value on the table.

Which Loan Types Have Which Penalties

Different commercial loan products come with different standard prepayment structures. Understanding the typical penalty for each loan type helps you set expectations before you start shopping for financing.

Loan Type Typical Prepayment Penalty Details
SBA 7(a) Step-down (5-3-1) Only on loans with 15+ year maturity; 5% year 1, 3% year 2, 1% year 3, then no penalty
SBA 504 Declining over 10 years Applies to CDC portion only; starts at approximately 5%, declines annually; bank first mortgage follows bank terms
Bank (Conventional) Negotiable; often step-down Most flexible; commonly 5-4-3-2-1 or 3-2-1; some banks offer yield maintenance; small banks are often the most negotiable
CMBS Defeasance or yield maintenance Typically 2-year lockout followed by defeasance or yield maintenance; penalty-free window of 3 to 6 months before maturity
Agency (Fannie Mae / Freddie Mac) Yield maintenance or declining Standard is yield maintenance; some programs offer declining balance penalties; penalty-free window of 3 to 6 months
Bridge Lockout then none Usually 6 to 24 month lockout or minimum interest guarantee; no penalty after lockout expires; some charge exit fees of 0.25% to 1%
Life Company Yield maintenance Almost always yield maintenance; very conservative terms; some offer defeasance on larger loans; penalty-free window of 3 to 6 months

As the table illustrates, borrowers who prioritize exit flexibility should focus on bank loans and SBA loans, which offer the most favorable and negotiable prepayment terms. CMBS and life company borrowers trade prepayment flexibility for lower rates and higher leverage. Bridge loan borrowers typically face short lockouts but enjoy the most flexibility after the lockout period expires.

The choice of loan product should always consider the borrower's anticipated hold period and exit strategy. A borrower planning to hold a property for 15 years may comfortably accept defeasance on a CMBS loan to capture the lowest possible rate. A borrower who might sell or refinance within 3 to 5 years should strongly prefer a step-down structure, even if it means accepting a slightly higher interest rate.
