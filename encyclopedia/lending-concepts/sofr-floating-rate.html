SOFR and Floating Rate Commercial Loans: How Variable Rate Lending Works

Floating rate loans tie your interest rate to a benchmark index that moves with the market. Since the transition from LIBOR to SOFR, understanding how these rates are structured, how they reset, and how to manage the risk has become essential for commercial real estate borrowers.

Key Facts at a Glance

SOFR Definition Secured Overnight Financing Rate, based on Treasury repo transactions
Rate Structure SOFR index + fixed spread (margin)
Typical Spread 1.50% to 3.50% over SOFR

What SOFR Is and Why It Replaced LIBOR

The Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR) is the primary benchmark interest rate for U.S. dollar-denominated floating rate loans. Published daily by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, SOFR reflects the cost of borrowing cash overnight using U.S. Treasury securities as collateral. It is derived from actual transactions in the Treasury repurchase agreement (repo) market, which typically sees over $1 trillion in daily volume.

SOFR replaced the London Interbank Offered Rate (LIBOR), which served as the dominant benchmark for decades. LIBOR was based on estimates submitted by a panel of banks about their expected borrowing costs. The problem was that these estimates were not grounded in real transactions. In 2012, a series of scandals revealed that banks had been manipulating their LIBOR submissions to benefit trading positions. Regulators around the world concluded that a benchmark built on hypothetical estimates was fundamentally flawed and began the process of transitioning to transaction-based alternatives.

The Alternative Reference Rates Committee (ARRC), convened by the Federal Reserve, selected SOFR as the preferred replacement for U.S. dollar LIBOR. The formal transition took several years. LIBOR publication for most U.S. dollar tenors officially ceased on June 30, 2023. Since then, virtually all new floating rate commercial real estate loans have been indexed to SOFR.

For borrowers, the shift from LIBOR to SOFR brought several changes. SOFR tends to be slightly lower than LIBOR because it is a secured (collateralized) rate, while LIBOR reflected unsecured lending. To account for this difference, many lenders added a credit spread adjustment of approximately 10 to 26 basis points during the transition period. Today, with the transition complete, loan spreads have been recalibrated so that all-in rates are quoted directly over SOFR without any legacy adjustments.

How Floating Rates Are Structured

Every floating rate commercial loan has two components that combine to form the borrower's interest rate: the index and the spread.

Floating Rate Formula

All-In Rate = SOFR Index Rate + Fixed Spread (Margin)

The SOFR index rate is the variable component. It moves up and down with market conditions, monetary policy, and economic factors. The spread (also called the margin) is fixed at loan origination and remains constant throughout the life of the loan. The spread reflects the lender's assessment of credit risk, property risk, and their required profit margin.

Worked Example

Consider a borrower closing on a floating rate bridge loan in early 2026:

  • 30-Day Term SOFR at closing: 4.30%
  • Lender spread: 2.50%
  • All-in rate: 6.80%
  • Loan amount: $5,000,000
Monthly Interest Calculation

Monthly Interest = $5,000,000 x 6.80% / 12 = $28,333

If SOFR drops to 3.80% at the next reset date, the all-in rate falls to 6.30%, and the monthly interest payment drops to $26,250. Conversely, if SOFR rises to 4.80%, the all-in rate increases to 7.30%, and the monthly payment climbs to $30,417. This variability is the defining characteristic of floating rate debt.

What Determines the Spread

Spreads on floating rate commercial loans vary based on several factors:

  • Property type and quality: Class A multifamily commands tighter spreads than a suburban office building
  • Loan-to-value ratio: Lower leverage means lower risk and tighter spreads
  • Borrower strength: Experienced sponsors with strong balance sheets earn better pricing
  • Loan purpose: Stabilized acquisitions receive tighter spreads than heavy value-add or construction
  • Market conditions: In periods of credit tightening, spreads widen across the board

Typical spreads on floating rate commercial real estate loans range from 1.50% for the strongest deals (agency multifamily, low leverage) to 3.50% or more for higher-risk bridge and transitional loans.

Rate Caps and Why They Are Required

A rate cap is a financial derivative that sets a ceiling on the SOFR component of a floating rate loan. If SOFR exceeds the cap's strike rate, the cap provider (typically a major bank) pays the borrower the difference. The borrower purchases the cap upfront for a one-time premium, and the cap remains in effect for a specified period, usually one to three years.

Rate Cap Mechanics

If SOFR > Strike Rate: Cap Provider Pays Borrower (SOFR - Strike Rate) x Notional Amount

If SOFR < Strike Rate: No Payment (cap expires unused for that period)

Almost every lender in the floating rate commercial real estate market requires borrowers to purchase an interest rate cap at or before loan closing. The reason is straightforward: without a cap, there is no limit to how high the borrower's interest expense could climb. A sudden rate spike could push debt service above the property's net operating income, creating a cash flow shortfall and increasing default risk.

Rate cap costs vary significantly based on three factors: the strike rate (lower strikes cost more because they provide more protection), the term (longer caps cost more), and the notional amount (larger loan balances mean higher premiums). In the elevated rate environment of 2024 and 2025, cap costs surged dramatically. A two-year cap on a $10,000,000 loan with a 3.00% SOFR strike might have cost $300,000 or more when SOFR was above 5.00%. As rates have moderated in early 2026, cap premiums have come down, though they remain a meaningful closing cost for floating rate borrowers.

Lenders typically specify the maximum allowable strike rate for the cap. For example, a lender might require a cap with a SOFR strike no higher than 5.00%, ensuring the borrower's maximum all-in rate (strike plus spread) stays within an acceptable range for debt service coverage.

Term SOFR vs. Daily SOFR

Not all SOFR is created equal. There are two primary ways SOFR is applied to commercial loans, and the distinction matters for payment predictability and rate calculation.

Feature Term SOFR Daily SOFR (Compounded)
How It Is Set Forward-looking; set at start of interest period Backward-looking; compounds daily over the period
Rate Known in Advance Yes, known at the beginning of each period No, final rate determined at end of the period
Published By CME Group Federal Reserve Bank of New York
Common Tenors 1-month, 3-month, 6-month Overnight rate compounded over custom periods
CRE Usage Most common for commercial real estate loans Less common in CRE; more prevalent in corporate lending
Payment Predictability Higher: borrower knows rate at period start Lower: exact rate not final until period end
Licensing Requires CME license; involves usage fees Freely available; no licensing fees

In commercial real estate, 1-month Term SOFR is the most widely used benchmark. Borrowers prefer it because the rate is known at the start of each monthly interest period, which allows for accurate budgeting and cash flow projections. Lenders favor it for the same reason: both parties know exactly what the payment will be on day one of the period.

Daily SOFR (also called SOFR compounded in arrears) is based on the actual overnight SOFR rate observed each day during the interest period. These daily rates are compounded together to produce the final rate for the period. Because the rate is not finalized until the end of the period (or a few days before, using a lookback convention), borrowers do not know the exact payment amount until the calculation is complete. This is more common in large syndicated corporate loans and is used by some banks for their commercial real estate floating rate products.

The practical difference between Term SOFR and compounded daily SOFR is usually small, often within a few basis points. The primary consideration is operational: how comfortable is the borrower with not knowing the exact rate in advance?

How Rate Resets Work

On a floating rate loan, the interest rate does not change continuously. Instead, it resets at defined intervals specified in the loan documents. The most common reset frequency for commercial real estate loans is monthly, though some loans reset quarterly.

Here is how a typical monthly reset works with 1-month Term SOFR:

  1. Determination date: Two business days before the start of each interest period, the lender observes the published 1-month Term SOFR rate
  2. Rate calculation: The lender adds the contractual spread to the observed SOFR rate to determine the all-in interest rate for the upcoming period
  3. Notification: The borrower receives notice of the new rate and the corresponding payment amount
  4. Payment: The borrower makes the interest payment based on the new rate for that month
  5. Repeat: The process repeats at the start of the next interest period

Most loan documents also include a SOFR floor, which is a minimum level for the index rate regardless of where SOFR actually trades. Common floor levels are 0.00% to 1.00%. If SOFR drops below the floor, the borrower's rate is calculated using the floor instead. This protects the lender's minimum return. For example, with a 0.50% SOFR floor and a 2.50% spread, the borrower's minimum all-in rate would be 3.00% even if SOFR fell to 0.10%.

Hedging Strategies for Floating Rate Borrowers

Floating rate debt introduces interest rate risk. If rates rise, your debt service increases. Borrowers have several tools to manage this risk beyond the required rate cap.

Interest Rate Caps

As discussed above, rate caps set a ceiling on SOFR. They provide one-sided protection: the borrower benefits if rates fall (paying less interest) while being protected if rates rise above the strike. This asymmetric profile makes caps the most popular hedging tool for shorter-term floating rate loans. The tradeoff is the upfront premium, which can be substantial.

Interest Rate Swaps

An interest rate swap effectively converts a floating rate loan into a fixed rate obligation. The borrower enters into a swap agreement with a counterparty (typically a bank) where the borrower pays a fixed rate and receives a floating rate tied to SOFR. The floating rate received offsets the floating rate paid on the loan, leaving the borrower with a net fixed rate.

Swap Mechanics

Net Rate = Swap Fixed Rate + Loan Spread

Example: 3.75% swap rate + 2.00% spread = 5.75% effective fixed rate

Swaps provide certainty but eliminate the benefit of falling rates. They also carry breakage costs: if the borrower needs to exit the swap early (due to a property sale or refinance), the termination cost can be significant, especially if rates have moved unfavorably. This makes swaps best suited for borrowers who are confident in their hold period and want fixed-rate economics on a floating rate loan structure.

Interest Rate Collars

A collar combines a cap (ceiling) and a floor (minimum rate) to create a band within which the borrower's rate can move. The borrower purchases a cap at a higher strike and simultaneously sells a floor at a lower strike. The premium received from selling the floor partially or fully offsets the cost of the cap, creating a lower-cost hedge.

For example, a borrower might purchase a 5.00% SOFR cap and sell a 3.00% SOFR floor. If SOFR stays between 3.00% and 5.00%, the borrower pays the actual rate. If SOFR rises above 5.00%, the cap provides protection. If SOFR falls below 3.00%, the borrower must still pay as if SOFR were 3.00%. The collar reduces the upfront cost compared to a standalone cap but limits the borrower's benefit from falling rates.

When Floating Rate Makes Sense vs. Fixed

The choice between floating and fixed rate financing is one of the most consequential decisions in commercial real estate lending. Neither option is universally better; the right choice depends on the borrower's business plan, hold period, and market outlook.

Floating Rate Is Typically Better When:

  • The hold period is short (1 to 3 years): Bridge loans, value-add acquisitions, and properties slated for quick renovation and sale or refinance align naturally with floating rate structures. Fixed rate loans for these short durations often carry prepayment penalties that make early exit expensive.
  • The property is transitional: Properties undergoing lease-up, renovation, or repositioning may not qualify for fixed rate financing from traditional lenders. Floating rate bridge lenders specialize in these situations.
  • Interest rates are expected to decline: When the rate environment is shifting downward, floating rate borrowers benefit automatically as their payments decrease at each reset. They do not need to refinance to capture lower rates.
  • Flexibility is a priority: Floating rate loans typically have minimal or no prepayment penalties, allowing borrowers to sell or refinance without significant exit costs.

Fixed Rate Is Typically Better When:

  • The hold period is long (5 to 10+ years): For stabilized, income-producing properties intended for long-term hold, fixed rate financing eliminates interest rate uncertainty over the full term.
  • Cash flow predictability is critical: Fixed rate loans allow for precise budgeting and investment return projections since debt service never changes.
  • Interest rates are expected to rise: Locking in a fixed rate before a rising rate environment protects the borrower from future cost increases.
  • The property is fully stabilized: Stabilized properties with strong occupancy and predictable income qualify for the best fixed rate terms from banks, CMBS lenders, agency lenders, and life companies.

Many experienced borrowers use a combination: floating rate debt during the acquisition and value-add phase, followed by a refinance into permanent fixed rate debt once the property is stabilized. This approach matches the financing structure to the business plan at each stage.

Current SOFR Environment and Trends (Early 2026)

As of early 2026, the SOFR rate environment reflects the Federal Reserve's monetary policy trajectory over the past two years. After reaching its cycle peak above 5.30% in late 2023 and holding at elevated levels through much of 2024, SOFR has declined as the Fed implemented a series of rate cuts beginning in September 2024.

By early 2026, 1-month Term SOFR is trading in the 4.00% to 4.50% range, down meaningfully from its 2023 peak but still well above the near-zero levels of 2020 and 2021. The futures market suggests a consensus expectation for gradual further easing, though the pace remains uncertain and data-dependent.

For floating rate borrowers, the current environment presents both opportunities and considerations:

  • Rate cap costs have moderated: With SOFR lower and less volatile than in 2023 and 2024, the cost of purchasing rate caps has decreased, reducing a major closing expense for floating rate loans
  • Spreads have tightened: As capital markets liquidity has improved, lender competition has increased and floating rate loan spreads have compressed from their 2023 and 2024 peaks
  • Refinance activity has increased: Many borrowers who took out floating rate bridge loans during 2021 and 2022 are now refinancing into permanent debt as rate cap expirations approach and property business plans mature
  • The yield curve shape matters: With the yield curve normalizing, fixed rates for longer-term loans may be competitive with floating rate all-in costs, making the fixed vs. floating analysis more nuanced than in prior years

Impact on Loan Sizing and DSCR

Floating rate loans introduce a unique challenge for loan sizing: because the rate can change, the Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) fluctuates with the rate. Lenders address this by using stress-tested rates rather than the current rate when sizing loans.

DSCR Formula

DSCR = Net Operating Income / Annual Debt Service

On a fixed rate loan, the DSCR calculation is straightforward because debt service is constant. On a floating rate loan, lenders typically size the loan based on the borrower's maximum possible rate (the cap strike rate plus the spread) rather than the current rate. This ensures the property can cover debt service even in a worst-case rate scenario.

Sizing Example

Consider a property with $500,000 in NOI and a lender requiring a minimum 1.25x DSCR:

  • Current SOFR: 4.30%, spread: 2.50%, current all-in rate: 6.80%
  • Rate cap strike: 5.50%, maximum all-in rate with cap: 8.00%
  • Maximum annual debt service at 1.25x DSCR: $500,000 / 1.25 = $400,000
  • At 8.00% interest-only, maximum loan: $400,000 / 0.08 = $5,000,000
  • At 6.80% current rate (if lender sized at current rate): $400,000 / 0.068 = $5,882,353

The stress-tested approach results in a smaller loan ($5,000,000 vs. $5,882,353) but ensures the deal works at the cap rate. This is a key reason why floating rate loans sometimes produce lower proceeds than a comparable fixed rate loan: the lender must account for rate volatility in their underwriting.

Some lenders use a hybrid approach, sizing the loan at a rate between the current rate and the cap strike. Others use their own internal stress rate, such as the current rate plus 200 basis points. The exact methodology varies by lender and is an important factor to evaluate when comparing term sheets.

For borrowers pursuing value-add strategies, this sizing dynamic creates a planning consideration: the lower the cap strike rate required by the lender, the smaller the loan proceeds. Negotiating a higher cap strike (where the lender permits it) can unlock additional loan proceeds, but the tradeoff is greater exposure to rising rates.
