Personal Guaranty on Commercial Loans: Types, Risks, and How to Negotiate

Nearly every commercial real estate loan from a bank or SBA lender requires a personal guaranty. Knowing the different types of guaranties, what they expose you to, and how to negotiate better terms is essential for protecting your personal wealth while accessing the financing you need.

What a Personal Guaranty Is

A personal guaranty is a legal document in which an individual agrees to be personally responsible for a commercial loan made to a business entity. In commercial real estate, properties are typically held by LLCs or corporations that sign the loan documents. Without a personal guaranty, the lender could only look to the entity's assets (primarily the property) for repayment. The guaranty creates a direct legal obligation between the individual guarantor and the lender, giving the lender access to the guarantor's personal wealth if the entity defaults.

The guaranty is a separate document from the mortgage, promissory note, and other loan documents. It survives independently, meaning the lender can enforce it even if there are technical issues with other loan documents. Guarantors should treat the guaranty agreement with the same seriousness as the loan itself, because in a default scenario, the guaranty is often the document that matters most to the individual's personal finances.

Full vs Limited Guaranty

The two fundamental categories of personal guaranty are full (unlimited) and limited. The difference between them determines the maximum amount the guarantor can be required to pay.

Full Guaranty

A full personal guaranty makes the guarantor liable for 100% of the outstanding loan balance, plus accrued interest, late fees, penalties, and the lender's collection costs (including attorney fees). There is no cap on the guarantor's exposure. If the property is foreclosed and sold for $1.5 million on a $2 million loan, the guarantor owes the $500,000 shortfall plus any additional costs. Full guaranty is the standard requirement for bank loans, SBA loans, credit union loans, and most bridge loans.

Limited Guaranty

A limited guaranty caps the guarantor's personal liability at a specific dollar amount or percentage of the loan. A 50% limited guaranty on a $4 million loan caps personal exposure at $2 million. Some limited guaranties decline over time (sometimes called a "declining balance guaranty"), automatically reducing the cap as the principal is paid down. Limited guaranties are not standard; they must be negotiated and are typically available to borrowers with strong financial profiles, proven track records, and properties that demonstrate solid performance.

Completion Guaranty for Construction Loans

Construction loans introduce a unique guaranty type: the completion guaranty. This document obligates the guarantor to personally fund any cost overruns necessary to complete the construction project according to the approved plans and budget. If the project runs over budget and the loan proceeds are exhausted, the guarantor must inject personal funds to finish the project.

Completion guaranties are separate from and in addition to the standard payment guaranty on a construction loan. A borrower might have a limited payment guaranty (say, 25% of the loan amount) but a full completion guaranty that requires them to finish the project regardless of cost. Lenders require completion guaranties because an unfinished building has minimal value as collateral. A half-built property cannot generate income and may actually cost money to maintain or demolish.

The completion guaranty typically remains in effect until the lender issues a "completion certificate" confirming that construction is substantially complete and the property has achieved a minimum occupancy or revenue threshold. At that point, the completion guaranty is released and only the payment guaranty remains.

Payment Guaranty vs Performance Guaranty

Guaranty agreements can be structured as payment guaranties, performance guaranties, or both. The distinction affects when and how the lender can enforce the guaranty.

A payment guaranty obligates the guarantor to pay the debt if the borrowing entity fails to do so. The lender can pursue the guarantor immediately upon default without first exhausting its remedies against the borrowing entity or the property. This is sometimes called a "guaranty of payment" as opposed to a "guaranty of collection."

A performance guaranty (or guaranty of collection) requires the lender to first exhaust its remedies against the borrowing entity and the property before pursuing the guarantor. The lender must foreclose on the property, obtain and attempt to collect on any deficiency judgment against the entity, and only then turn to the guarantor for the remaining shortfall. Performance guaranties provide more protection for the guarantor because the lender must demonstrate that the property was insufficient before making a personal claim.

Most commercial lenders require payment guaranties because they provide faster and more certain recovery. Performance guaranties are less common but may be available through negotiation with certain lenders.

Burnoff Provisions

A burnoff provision (also called a "burn-down" or "step-down") gradually reduces the guaranty amount over time. Burnoffs incentivize strong property performance and reward borrowers as the loan seasons and the lender's risk decreases.

Time-Based Burnoff

The simplest burnoff structure reduces the guaranty by a fixed percentage on each anniversary of the loan closing. For example:

  • Year 1: 100% guaranty
  • Year 2: 75% guaranty
  • Year 3: 50% guaranty
  • Year 4: 25% guaranty
  • Year 5: 0% (guaranty fully released)

This schedule provides certainty for the guarantor and is easy to administer. However, it does not account for changes in property performance or market conditions.

Performance-Based Burnoff

Some burnoff provisions are tied to property performance milestones. Common triggers include achieving a minimum DSCR (such as 1.30x or higher) for a specified period, reaching a target occupancy rate (such as 90% or higher), maintaining a certain debt yield threshold, or achieving a specified loan-to-value ratio through principal paydown or property appreciation.

Performance-based burnoffs align the guaranty reduction with the property's actual risk profile. They give the lender confidence that the guaranty is only released when the property can stand on its own financial merits.

Net Worth and Liquidity Requirements

Lenders evaluate guarantors against specific financial benchmarks to ensure the guaranty has real economic substance. A guaranty from someone with no assets is worthless to the lender.

Net Worth

Most lenders require the guarantor's total net worth (assets minus liabilities, excluding the subject property) to equal or exceed the loan amount. For larger loans, some lenders accept a ratio of 0.75x to 1.0x. Real estate assets are typically counted at their fair market value, though some lenders discount illiquid assets by 10% to 25%.

Liquidity

Liquid assets (cash, money market accounts, publicly traded securities) must typically equal 10% to 20% of the loan amount. This liquidity must be available post-closing, meaning it excludes the equity being contributed to the transaction. Lenders want assurance that the guarantor can cover unexpected shortfalls without being forced to liquidate illiquid holdings.

Ongoing Monitoring

Many lenders require annual delivery of personal financial statements and tax returns from the guarantor throughout the loan term. If the guarantor's financial condition deteriorates below required thresholds, the lender may require additional collateral, a replacement or additional guarantor, or may declare a technical default under the loan documents.

Spousal Guaranty Considerations

Spousal guaranty requirements create some of the most emotionally charged discussions in the commercial loan process. Understanding why they exist and how to navigate them reduces friction at closing.

In community property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin), assets acquired during marriage are generally considered jointly owned. A guaranty signed by only one spouse may not be enforceable against community property assets. Lenders in these states require both spouses to sign to ensure the guaranty is fully enforceable.

In separate property (common law) states, spousal guarantees are less common but may still be required depending on lender policy, especially if the guarantor's primary assets are jointly titled with the spouse. Some lenders require a "spousal consent" form (less than a full guaranty) that acknowledges the guaranty and waives certain homestead or marital property protections.

Strategies for addressing spousal guaranty concerns include discussing the requirement early in the process (not at the closing table), consulting a family law attorney about the specific implications under your state's laws, exploring non-recourse loan options that avoid the issue entirely, and considering whether existing prenuptial or postnuptial agreements affect community property treatment.

Strategies to Limit Personal Exposure

While personal guaranties are a fact of life in commercial lending, experienced borrowers use several strategies to manage and reduce their exposure:

  1. Negotiate a limited guaranty. Ask for a guaranty capped at 50% or 75% of the loan amount instead of full recourse. Strong borrowers with well-performing properties have leverage to negotiate this.
  2. Request burnoff provisions. Even if the lender requires 100% guaranty at closing, a burnoff schedule that reduces the guaranty over 3 to 5 years significantly limits long-term exposure.
  3. Bring in co-guarantors. Multiple guarantors can share the liability, reducing each individual's exposure. Partners, equity investors, or family members with sufficient net worth can serve as co-guarantors.
  4. Build banking relationships. Lenders are more willing to offer favorable guaranty terms to borrowers who maintain deposits, have multiple loans, and have a track record of on-time performance at the institution.
  5. Obtain competing term sheets. Nothing motivates a lender to improve guaranty terms like a better offer from a competitor. Getting two or three term sheets gives you negotiating leverage.
  6. Consider non-recourse programs. For qualifying properties, non-recourse loans through CMBS, agency, or life company programs eliminate the payment guaranty entirely (with only carve-out liability remaining).
  7. Use asset protection planning. Work with an estate planning attorney to structure your personal assets in a way that provides maximum protection under applicable state law. This must be done proactively, not after a default occurs.

When Guaranties Are Non-Negotiable

Certain situations leave little room for guaranty negotiation. SBA loans require personal guaranties from every individual owning 20% or more of the borrowing entity, and SBA policy does not permit limited guaranties or burnoffs on their portion. First-time borrowers without a track record almost always face full recourse requirements. Transitional or distressed properties with uncertain income also trigger full guaranty requirements because the lender cannot rely solely on property performance.

In these situations, the borrower's best strategy is to accept the guaranty requirement and focus negotiation on other loan terms such as rate, fees, prepayment flexibility, and interest-only periods. As the borrower builds a track record and the property stabilizes, future refinancing into more favorable guaranty terms (or non-recourse programs) becomes possible.
