Balloon Payment on a Commercial Mortgage: What It Means and How to Prepare

Most commercial real estate loans come with a balloon payment at maturity. Understanding what that means, how much you will owe, and how to plan for it is essential to protecting your investment.

Key Facts at a Glance

Definition The remaining loan balance due in a lump sum at maturity
When It Occurs At the end of the loan term (typically 5 to 10 years)
Why It Exists Commercial loans amortize over longer periods than their terms
Typical Timeline 5, 7, or 10-year terms with 25 to 30-year amortization
Action Required Refinance, sell, or negotiate extension before maturity
Risk Factor Rate environment and property value may change by maturity

What Is a Balloon Payment?

A balloon payment is the large lump sum of principal that remains due at the end of a commercial mortgage term. Unlike a residential mortgage that is typically structured to be fully repaid over 30 years of monthly payments, most commercial loans require the borrower to pay off the entire remaining balance on a specific date, usually 5, 7, or 10 years after origination.

The reason this balance is so large is that commercial mortgages are structured with a split between the loan term and the amortization schedule. The monthly payments are calculated as if the loan would be paid off over 25 or 30 years, keeping those payments manageable. But the loan itself comes due much sooner. When the term ends, whatever principal has not yet been repaid through monthly payments becomes the balloon payment.

For example, if you borrow $2 million with a 25-year amortization but a 10-year term, you make monthly payments sized for a 25-year payoff. After 10 years, a substantial portion of the original principal remains. That remaining balance is your balloon payment, and it must be paid in full at maturity.

The term "balloon" is fitting: the final payment balloons far beyond the size of any individual monthly payment. A borrower who has been making monthly payments of $14,000 might face a balloon payment of $1.6 million. This is not a penalty or a surprise fee. It is simply the mathematical consequence of calculating payments on a long amortization schedule while requiring full repayment on a shorter timeline.

Why Commercial Loans Have Balloon Payments

Balloon payment structures exist in commercial lending for reasons that benefit both lenders and borrowers.

From the lender's perspective, commercial real estate carries different risks than residential property. Tenant turnover, market cycles, property condition changes, and shifts in local economies can all affect a commercial property's value and income over time. By limiting the loan term to 5, 7, or 10 years, lenders create built-in checkpoints to reassess the loan. At maturity, the lender (or a new lender) can re-evaluate the property's condition, income, market value, and the borrower's financial strength before committing to another term of financing.

From the borrower's perspective, the balloon structure keeps monthly payments lower than they would be with a fully amortizing short-term loan. If a lender required full repayment of a $2 million loan over just 10 years, the monthly payment would be roughly $23,200 at 7% interest. With a 25-year amortization schedule, that same loan has a monthly payment of approximately $14,130. The difference of over $9,000 per month provides significant cash flow relief, even though it means a large balance remains at maturity.

This structure also reflects the reality of commercial real estate ownership. Many investors plan to hold properties for 5 to 10 years before selling or refinancing. The balloon maturity date often aligns with the borrower's natural investment horizon, making the structure practical rather than burdensome for investors with a clear exit strategy.

How to Calculate Your Balloon Balance

Understanding the math behind your balloon payment removes the mystery and helps you plan effectively. Let us walk through a detailed example.

Example Loan Terms

Original Loan Amount: $2,000,000

Interest Rate: 7.0% (fixed)

Amortization Period: 25 years (300 months)

Loan Term: 10 years (120 months)

Step 1: Calculate the monthly payment. Using the standard amortization formula with a $2,000,000 principal, 7.0% annual rate (0.5833% monthly), and 300-month amortization, the monthly payment is approximately $14,130.

Step 2: Understand how each payment is split. In the early years of the loan, most of each monthly payment goes toward interest rather than principal. On this example loan, the first monthly payment includes approximately $11,667 in interest and only $2,463 in principal reduction. Over time, the principal portion gradually increases while the interest portion decreases.

Step 3: Calculate total principal paid over 10 years. After 120 monthly payments, the cumulative principal repaid is approximately $389,000. That represents less than 20% of the original $2 million loan balance.

Step 4: Determine the balloon balance. The remaining balance after 10 years of payments is approximately $1,611,000. This is the balloon payment due at maturity.

Balloon Payment Summary

Original Loan: $2,000,000

Total Paid Over 10 Years: $1,695,600 (120 payments of $14,130)

Principal Repaid: ~$389,000 (19.5% of original balance)

Interest Paid: ~$1,306,600

Balloon Payment Due: ~$1,611,000

The numbers illustrate a key reality of balloon loans: after a full decade of payments totaling nearly $1.7 million, you still owe roughly 80% of the original loan amount. This is entirely normal for commercial mortgages, and it underscores why planning ahead for the balloon payment is so critical.

Several patterns emerge from this table. Shorter loan terms leave more principal outstanding at maturity. Higher interest rates mean a greater share of each payment goes to interest, slowing down principal reduction. Longer amortization periods (30 years vs. 25 years) also result in higher balloon balances because the payments are spread over a longer theoretical payoff period.

Planning for Balloon Maturity: Start 12 to 18 Months Early

The single most important piece of advice for managing a balloon payment is this: start planning well before the maturity date arrives. Twelve to eighteen months of lead time is the recommended minimum, and earlier is better.

Here is a timeline for a structured approach to balloon maturity preparation:

  1. 18 months before maturity: Initial assessment. Review your current loan terms, balloon balance, and property financials. Order a preliminary property valuation to understand your current equity position. Evaluate whether refinancing, selling, or requesting an extension makes the most sense for your situation.
  2. 12 to 15 months before maturity: Document preparation. Compile your T12 (trailing 12-month) operating statement, current rent roll, two to three years of tax returns (personal and entity), entity organizational documents, and a property condition summary. Having these documents ready accelerates the lending process significantly.
  3. 9 to 12 months before maturity: Lender outreach. Begin conversations with multiple lenders. Submit your deal package to at least three to five lenders to ensure competitive terms. Evaluate term sheets and select the best fit for your needs.
  4. 6 to 9 months before maturity: Underwriting and appraisal. Once you select a lender, the formal underwriting process begins. This includes a full property appraisal, environmental review (Phase I), title work, and financial analysis. Budget 60 to 120 days for this phase.
  5. 3 to 6 months before maturity: Legal and closing. Loan documents are prepared, reviewed by attorneys on both sides, and negotiated. Final closing conditions are satisfied, including insurance updates and entity documentation.
  6. At maturity: Close the new loan. The proceeds from the new loan pay off the balloon balance on the existing loan. The transition should be seamless if you have followed this timeline.

Starting late creates unnecessary risk. If your first lender falls through at the underwriting stage, you need enough runway to pivot to an alternative without triggering a maturity default.

Refinancing Options at Maturity

When your balloon payment comes due, refinancing is the most common resolution. Several loan types are available depending on your property type, financial profile, and goals.

  • Bank loan (conventional): Community and regional banks offer commercial mortgages with new 5 to 10-year terms. These lenders are flexible on structure and often maintain relationships with existing borrowers. Best for borrowers with strong financials and stabilized properties.
  • CMBS (conduit loan): Commercial mortgage-backed securities loans offer competitive rates and higher leverage for stabilized income-producing properties. Terms are typically 5 or 10 years with 25 to 30-year amortization. CMBS works well for larger loans ($2 million and above) on stable, well-occupied properties.
  • Agency (Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac): If your property is multifamily (5 or more residential units), agency loans typically offer the most favorable rates and terms in the market. These programs provide long-term fixed rates, high leverage (up to 80% LTV), and flexible prepayment structures.
  • SBA loans: For owner-occupied commercial properties, SBA 7(a) and SBA 504 loans can refinance existing commercial mortgages. SBA 504 loans are especially attractive because they offer below-market fixed rates on the CDC portion with terms up to 25 years, potentially eliminating future balloon payments altogether.
  • Bridge loan: If your property has occupancy issues, deferred maintenance, or other problems that prevent permanent financing, a short-term bridge loan (12 to 36 months) can pay off the balloon while you stabilize the asset. Bridge loans carry higher rates but provide the time needed to position the property for permanent financing.

What Happens If You Cannot Refinance: Maturity Default

When a borrower cannot pay the balloon payment and fails to refinance or sell the property before the maturity date, the loan enters technical default. This is sometimes called a "maturity default" to distinguish it from a payment default (missing monthly payments).

A maturity default triggers a sequence of potential consequences:

  1. Default notice: The lender issues a formal notice of default, starting the clock on cure periods and legal remedies outlined in the loan documents.
  2. Default interest rate: Most commercial loan agreements include a default rate provision, typically 2% to 5% above the contract rate. This begins accruing immediately upon default.
  3. Lender negotiation: In practice, most lenders prefer to work with borrowers rather than pursue foreclosure. Foreclosure is expensive, time-consuming, and often results in the lender recovering less than the outstanding balance. This creates an incentive for both parties to find a resolution.
  4. Forbearance agreement: The lender may offer a formal forbearance agreement that extends the maturity date by 3 to 12 months, usually with additional conditions such as a forbearance fee (0.25% to 1.0% of the loan balance), an increased interest rate, additional reserves or escrows, and a requirement to actively pursue refinancing or sale.
  5. Foreclosure or deed in lieu: If no resolution is reached, the lender may initiate foreclosure proceedings. Alternatively, the borrower may negotiate a deed in lieu of foreclosure, transferring the property to the lender to avoid the costs and timeline of formal foreclosure. This is a last resort for both parties.

The most important takeaway is that communication matters enormously. Lenders are far more likely to offer favorable workout terms to borrowers who communicate proactively and demonstrate good faith efforts to resolve the situation. Surprising your lender with a maturity default creates adversarial dynamics that limit your options.

The 2026 Maturity Wall: Context for Today's Borrowers

The balloon payment challenge is especially acute in 2026. Approximately $875 billion in commercial real estate loans are maturing this year, creating what the industry calls the "maturity wall." Many of these loans were originated between 2016 and 2021, when interest rates were significantly lower than today's levels.

This timing creates a specific problem: DSCR compression. Here is how it works in practice.

Consider a property that was financed in 2019 with a $3 million loan at 4.5% interest on a 25-year amortization. The monthly payment was approximately $16,680, and the property's NOI of $225,000 produced a healthy DSCR of 1.12x. Now that loan matures in 2026, and the remaining balance is approximately $2.67 million. To refinance at today's rates of 7.0%, the new monthly payment would be roughly $18,860 on the reduced balance. The annual debt service jumps from $200,160 to $226,320, and if the property's NOI has not grown proportionally, the DSCR may fall below lender minimums.

This scenario is playing out across the commercial real estate market. Properties that were comfortably financed at sub-5% rates now face refinancing at rates 200 to 300 basis points higher. For some property types, particularly office buildings with elevated vacancy rates, the combination of reduced income and higher borrowing costs creates a genuine inability to refinance at the balloon balance amount.

The result is that many borrowers will need to bring additional equity to the closing table, accept lower proceeds from a smaller loan, or negotiate with existing lenders for modifications. Understanding your property's position relative to this market dynamic is critical for planning your balloon payment strategy in the current environment.

Extension Options and Loan Modifications

When a borrower faces difficulty meeting the balloon payment, several modification and extension structures may be available. These are particularly relevant in the current rate environment.

  • Short-term extension: Many loan agreements include built-in extension options (one or two extensions of 6 to 12 months each), typically subject to conditions such as no existing default, payment of an extension fee, and meeting a minimum DSCR test. Even if your loan does not include a formal extension option, lenders can agree to extend on a case-by-case basis.
  • Rate modification: In some cases, the existing lender may agree to modify the interest rate to make the loan viable for an additional term. This is more common when the lender believes the property is fundamentally sound but the current rate environment makes refinancing impractical.
  • Principal reduction: Rarely, a lender may agree to write down a portion of the principal if the property value has declined significantly and the alternative (foreclosure) would result in even greater losses. This is more common in distressed loan workouts.
  • A/B note structure: The lender splits the existing loan into an "A note" at a lower amount that the property can service, and a "B note" for the remainder. The B note may carry a deferred payment structure or reduced interest rate. This allows the lender to keep the loan performing while acknowledging the property's current limitations.
  • Discounted payoff: If the property value has fallen below the loan balance, the borrower may negotiate to pay off the loan at less than the full amount owed. The lender accepts the loss to avoid the greater expense and uncertainty of foreclosure.

The availability of these options depends heavily on the lender type. Portfolio lenders (banks and credit unions that hold loans on their own books) have the most flexibility. CMBS loans are serviced by special servicers with specific guidelines and less room for creative solutions. Agency loans have standardized modification programs with defined eligibility criteria.

Strategies to Prepare for Balloon Maturity

Proactive preparation dramatically increases your chances of a smooth transition at balloon maturity. These strategies apply whether you plan to refinance, sell, or extend.

  1. Build cash reserves. Set aside funds throughout the loan term specifically designated for maturity costs. Refinancing involves appraisal fees, legal costs, title insurance, and potential rate lock deposits. Having 1% to 2% of the loan amount in liquid reserves provides a cushion for these expenses and demonstrates financial stability to new lenders.
  2. Maximize your NOI. Lenders underwrite based on actual property income. Every dollar of additional NOI improves your DSCR and increases the loan amount you can qualify for. Focus on reducing vacancy, achieving market-rate rents, controlling operating expenses, and adding revenue streams. Start this process 2 to 3 years before maturity so the improvements are reflected in your trailing financials.
  3. Maintain property condition. Deferred maintenance is a red flag for new lenders and appraisers. Address capital items proactively: roof repairs, HVAC replacements, parking lot resurfacing, and similar needs. A property in good physical condition appraises higher and underwriting proceeds more smoothly.
  4. Keep clean financial records. Lenders require detailed operating statements, rent rolls, and tax returns. Maintaining organized, accurate, and current financial records accelerates the refinancing process. Use professional property management software to generate standardized reports that lenders recognize and trust.
  5. Monitor your credit profile. For loans that involve personal guarantees (most bank, SBA, and smaller balance loans), your personal credit score and financial statement matter. Avoid taking on new personal debt in the 12 to 18 months before maturity. Resolve any credit issues well in advance.
  6. Stay informed about rate trends. Interest rate movements directly affect your refinancing options and costs. Follow Federal Reserve policy, Treasury yields, and commercial mortgage rate indices so you can time your rate lock effectively. Consider locking your rate 60 to 90 days before closing if you find favorable terms.
  7. Cultivate lender relationships. Maintaining a relationship with your current lender and building connections with alternative lenders gives you more options at maturity. Regular communication with your existing lender about property performance and your refinancing plans can also facilitate smoother extensions if needed.
  8. Consider early refinancing. If current rates are favorable and your loan allows prepayment without excessive penalties, refinancing before the balloon date can lock in better terms and eliminate maturity risk. Some borrowers refinance 1 to 2 years early to take advantage of a favorable rate environment.
  9. Have a backup plan. Even with thorough preparation, things can go wrong. A prospective lender may decline late in underwriting, property values may shift, or market conditions may tighten. Having identified backup lenders, a bridge loan option, or the possibility of selling the property ensures you are never without alternatives as the maturity date approaches.
