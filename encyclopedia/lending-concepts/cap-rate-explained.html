Cap Rate (Capitalization Rate) in Commercial Real Estate: What Every Investor Should Know

The capitalization rate is the most widely quoted metric in commercial real estate transactions. Understanding how cap rates work, what drives them, and how lenders use them is essential for making sound investment and financing decisions.

Key Facts at a Glance

Definition Annual return on an all-cash purchase
Formula NOI / Property Value x 100
Typical Range 4% to 10% (varies by property type)
Inverse Relationship Higher cap rate = lower value
Market Indicator Reflects investor demand and risk
Limitation Does not account for financing or appreciation

What Cap Rate Means

Cap rate (capitalization rate) represents the expected annual return on a commercial property if purchased with all cash. It measures the relationship between a property's net operating income (NOI) and its current market value or purchase price. In practical terms, a 7% cap rate means the property generates 7 cents of NOI for every dollar of value.

Cap rate is the most widely quoted metric in commercial real estate transactions. When investors, brokers, and lenders discuss a deal, cap rate is almost always the first number mentioned. It serves as a shorthand for both pricing and risk: a lower cap rate signals that investors view the property as lower risk and are willing to accept a smaller annual return, while a higher cap rate suggests greater perceived risk and a demand for higher returns.

Because cap rate strips out the effects of financing, it provides a level playing field for comparing properties. Two investors looking at the same building may have very different loan terms, but the cap rate remains the same for both. This makes it an indispensable tool for evaluating deals across property types, markets, and time periods.

How to Calculate Cap Rate

The cap rate formula is straightforward:

Cap Rate Formula

Cap Rate = Net Operating Income (NOI) / Property Value x 100

You can also rearrange the formula to solve for property value, which is how appraisers and investors frequently use it:

Value Formula (Income Approach)

Property Value = NOI / Cap Rate

Worked Example: Calculating Cap Rate

Consider a commercial property with the following profile:

  • Annual Net Operating Income: $350,000
  • Sale Price: $5,000,000
Cap Rate Calculation

Cap Rate = $350,000 / $5,000,000 x 100 = 7.0%

This tells us the buyer is purchasing a 7.0% unlevered yield on their investment.

Worked Example: Calculating Value from Cap Rate

Now assume you know the market cap rate for similar properties is 6.5%, and a building generates $350,000 in NOI. What is its implied value?

Value Calculation

Property Value = $350,000 / 0.065 = $5,384,615

Notice how a lower cap rate (6.5% vs. 7.0%) produces a higher value ($5,384,615 vs. $5,000,000) for the same NOI. This inverse relationship between cap rate and value is one of the most important concepts in commercial real estate. When cap rates compress (fall), property values rise. When cap rates expand (rise), property values decline.

Cap Rates by Property Type

Cap rates vary significantly across property types because each carries a different risk and return profile. The table below shows typical ranges as of early 2026:

Property Type Typical Cap Rate Range Key Drivers
Multifamily (Class A) 4.5% to 5.5% Strong demand, agency financing, essential-need product
Multifamily (Class B/C) 5.5% to 7.0% Value-add potential, higher turnover, deferred maintenance
Industrial / Logistics 5.0% to 6.5% E-commerce growth, long-term leases, limited new supply
Retail (NNN, Credit Tenant) 5.5% to 6.5% Predictable income, minimal landlord expense, credit tenants
Retail (Strip Center) 6.5% to 8.5% Tenant rollover risk, local tenant exposure, management intensity
Office (CBD Class A) 6.0% to 7.5% Remote work headwinds, longer lease terms, institutional quality
Office (Suburban) 7.0% to 9.0% Higher vacancy, shorter leases, weaker tenant credit
Self-Storage 5.5% to 7.0% Low operating costs, diversified tenant base, recession resilience
Hospitality 7.0% to 10.0% Revenue volatility, operating intensity, economic sensitivity

These ranges differ because of risk, tenant quality, income stability, and growth potential. Multifamily cap rates are lowest because housing is a fundamental need, tenant demand remains strong across economic cycles, and government-sponsored financing (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac) keeps borrowing costs low. Hospitality cap rates are highest because hotel revenue fluctuates with travel demand, operating costs are high, and there is no long-term lease protecting income.

Industrial and logistics properties have compressed significantly over the past decade due to e-commerce expansion and supply chain reconfiguration. Office cap rates have expanded since 2020 as remote and hybrid work patterns reduced demand for traditional office space, particularly in suburban markets.

What Drives Cap Rate Compression and Expansion

Cap rates are not static. They move in response to capital flows, interest rates, and property-level fundamentals.

Cap Rate Compression (Falling Cap Rates)

Cap rate compression happens when property values rise faster than NOI grows. The main catalysts include:

  • More capital chasing fewer deals: When institutional investors, private equity, and foreign capital increase their allocations to commercial real estate, competition for assets drives up prices and compresses cap rates.
  • Falling interest rates: Lower borrowing costs increase buyer purchasing power, allowing them to pay more for the same NOI stream.
  • Strengthening tenant demand: Declining vacancy and rising rents signal improving fundamentals, which attract more capital and reduce perceived risk.
  • Favorable supply dynamics: When new construction slows or barriers to entry limit supply, existing properties become more valuable.

Cap Rate Expansion (Rising Cap Rates)

Cap rate expansion is the opposite: property values decline relative to NOI. This happens when:

  • Capital retreats: Investors pull back from real estate, reducing competition for assets and allowing buyers to demand higher yields.
  • Interest rates rise: Higher borrowing costs reduce how much buyers can pay, pushing prices down and cap rates up.
  • Property fundamentals weaken: Rising vacancy, declining rents, or deteriorating tenant quality increase perceived risk and demand for higher returns.
  • Lending conditions tighten: When banks reduce their commercial real estate exposure or regulators impose stricter requirements, the pool of available capital shrinks.

The 2022-2025 cycle illustrates this clearly. Aggressive interest rate hikes by the Federal Reserve pushed borrowing costs sharply higher, triggering cap rate expansion across most property types. Multifamily cap rates rose approximately 100 to 150 basis points from their 2021-2022 lows. Office cap rates expanded even further as remote work trends compounded the interest rate effect. By early 2026, many markets are showing signs of stabilization as investors have adjusted their return expectations to the new rate environment.

Cap Rate vs. Interest Rate Relationship

The relationship between cap rates and interest rates is one of the most important dynamics in commercial real estate finance. The difference between the two is known as the "spread."

Positive leverage occurs when the cap rate exceeds the borrowing cost. In this scenario, each dollar of debt boosts the investor's equity return because the property earns more on every borrowed dollar than the cost of that borrowing. For example, buying at a 7% cap rate with a 6% loan means you earn 1% more on the borrowed portion than you pay for it.

Negative leverage occurs when the cap rate falls below the borrowing cost. In this case, debt actually drags down equity returns because the property earns less on borrowed dollars than the cost of borrowing. Buying at a 5% cap rate with a 6.5% loan means every dollar of debt reduces your return.

During the low-rate years of 2020-2021, cap rate spreads were healthy (often 200 to 300 basis points above Treasury rates), and positive leverage was easily achieved. The rapid rate increases of 2022-2024 compressed these spreads dramatically. Many deals traded at negative leverage during this period, which slowed transaction volume significantly. Buyers became unwilling to pay prices that produced negative leverage, while sellers resisted lowering prices to restore positive spreads. This standoff, often called the "bid-ask gap," froze many markets for over a year.

As of early 2026, spreads have normalized somewhat. Cap rates have adjusted upward enough that positive leverage is achievable on most property types, though spreads remain tighter than their pre-2022 levels.

Going-In Cap Rate vs. Terminal (Exit) Cap Rate

Investors use two distinct cap rate measures when modeling a deal:

  • Going-in cap rate: The cap rate at which you purchase the property. This is calculated by dividing Year 1 NOI by the acquisition price.
  • Terminal (exit) cap rate: The cap rate you assume will apply when you sell the property at the end of your hold period. This is applied to the projected NOI in the final year to estimate the sale price.

Prudent investors typically assume a terminal cap rate that is 50 to 100 basis points higher than their going-in cap rate. This conservative assumption accounts for the possibility that market conditions may be less favorable at the time of sale, or that the property may have aged and become less competitive.

How Terminal Cap Rate Affects Projected Returns

Consider an investor buying a property at a 6.5% going-in cap rate with $350,000 of Year 1 NOI and projecting NOI to grow to $400,000 over a 5-year hold:

  • Terminal cap rate of 6.5% (flat): Exit value = $400,000 / 0.065 = $6,153,846
  • Terminal cap rate of 7.0% (+50 bps): Exit value = $400,000 / 0.070 = $5,714,286
  • Terminal cap rate of 7.5% (+100 bps): Exit value = $400,000 / 0.075 = $5,333,333

The difference between a flat exit cap and a 100 basis point expansion is over $820,000 in exit value on the same property. This is why terminal cap rate assumptions are one of the most scrutinized inputs in any investment underwriting. Small changes in this single variable can swing a deal from profitable to break-even.

Limitations of Cap Rate Analysis

Cap rate is a powerful screening tool, but it has significant limitations that investors should understand. Cap rate does not account for:

  • Financing structure: A property's cap rate is the same whether you pay all cash or use 75% leverage. It tells you nothing about the actual return on your equity.
  • Capital expenditures: Two properties may trade at the same cap rate, but one may need $500,000 in deferred maintenance while the other is recently renovated. Cap rate does not reflect this difference.
  • Appreciation potential: A property in a rapidly growing market may justify a lower cap rate because the investor expects NOI growth and value appreciation. Cap rate is a snapshot metric that does not capture growth.
  • Lease rollover risk: A property with tenants whose leases expire in 12 months carries more risk than one with 10-year leases, even if both have the same cap rate today.
  • Future NOI changes: Cap rate is based on current or trailing NOI. If a major tenant is vacating next year, the current cap rate overstates the property's attractiveness.

Cap rate comparisons can be misleading when applied to properties with different quality levels, lease structures, or capital needs. A 6% cap rate on a fully stabilized, recently renovated Class A building is very different from a 6% cap rate on a Class C property with deferred maintenance and near-term lease expirations. Always dig beneath the headline cap rate to understand the full risk and return picture.

How Lenders Use Cap Rates in Underwriting

Lenders rely on cap rates at multiple stages of the underwriting process:

  • Appraised value validation: Lenders compare the implied cap rate of an appraisal against recent comparable sales and market benchmarks. If the appraised cap rate is significantly below market norms, the lender may question whether the valuation is aggressive.
  • Stress testing property values: Lenders apply higher "stressed" cap rates to the property's NOI to determine what the value would be under adverse conditions. If the stressed value falls below the loan amount, the deal may not pass underwriting.
  • Market condition assessment: Cap rate trends help lenders gauge whether a market is overheated (very low cap rates, compressed spreads) or undervalued (higher cap rates relative to historical norms).
  • Cap rate floors: Some lenders apply a minimum cap rate in their underwriting regardless of the actual transaction cap rate. For example, a lender might use a 6% cap rate floor even if the property sold at 5%. This effectively limits the appraised value and reduces the maximum loan amount, protecting the lender against the possibility that the buyer overpaid.
  • Exit analysis: For bridge and construction loans, lenders evaluate whether the projected stabilized cap rate supports a refinance into permanent financing. If the terminal cap rate assumptions are too aggressive, the borrower may not be able to refinance the loan at maturity.

2026 Cap Rate Trends

As of early 2026, cap rate trends vary significantly by property type:

  • Multifamily: Cap rates have stabilized in the 4.5% to 7.0% range after expanding from their 2021-2022 lows. Transaction volume is recovering as buyers and sellers find common ground on pricing. Agency lending (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac) remains active, supporting liquidity.
  • Industrial: Cap rates have settled in the 5.0% to 6.5% range. While they expanded slightly from the sub-4% levels seen at the peak, industrial remains a favored sector due to persistent demand from e-commerce and supply chain modernization.
  • Office: This sector remains under the most pressure. CBD Class A cap rates have expanded to 6.0% to 7.5%, and suburban office trades at 7.0% to 9.0% or higher. Many properties face significant value declines, and transaction activity is limited by the wide gap between buyer and seller expectations.
  • Retail: Retail cap rates are rebounding selectively. Grocery-anchored centers and single-tenant NNN properties with credit tenants have seen renewed investor interest and modest cap rate compression. Unanchored strip centers and non-essential retail continue to face headwinds.
  • Self-storage and hospitality: Self-storage cap rates remain steady in the 5.5% to 7.0% range, supported by consistent demand and low operating costs. Hospitality cap rates remain elevated at 7.0% to 10.0%, reflecting the sector's operating volatility and higher perceived risk.

The broader trend across commercial real estate in 2026 is one of recalibration. After several years of adjustment to higher interest rates, cap rates have largely found new equilibrium levels. Investors who can acquire properties at current cap rates with positive leverage are finding attractive risk-adjusted returns, particularly in multifamily and industrial sectors.
