Commercial Lease Types: NNN, Gross, and Modified Gross Explained for Borrowers

The lease structure on your commercial property directly shapes how lenders evaluate your deal. Whether your tenants are on triple net, gross, or modified gross leases, the type of lease determines your Net Operating Income, your risk profile, and ultimately how much financing you can secure.

Key Facts at a Glance

Why Lease Structure Matters for Loan Underwriting

When a lender evaluates a commercial real estate loan, the property's lease structure is one of the first things they examine. The lease type determines who pays for operating expenses, how predictable the property's cash flow is, and how much risk the landlord retains. These factors directly influence the loan amount, interest rate, and terms a borrower can obtain.

At its core, the distinction between lease types comes down to one question: who is responsible for property operating expenses? In some structures, the tenant pays virtually all costs beyond base rent. In others, the landlord absorbs every operating expense and charges a single, all-inclusive rental rate. Between these two extremes lies a spectrum of arrangements where expenses are split in various ways.

For borrowers seeking financing, this distinction is far from academic. A property with ten-year NNN leases from investment-grade tenants presents a fundamentally different risk profile than an identical building with short-term gross leases from local businesses. Lenders recognize this difference, and it shows up in every aspect of the loan terms they offer. Understanding lease structures allows you to present your property in the strongest possible light and anticipate how underwriters will assess your deal.

The three primary commercial lease structures are Triple Net (NNN), Full-Service Gross, and Modified Gross. Each has distinct characteristics, common use cases, and implications for financing. Let's examine each in detail.

Triple Net (NNN) Leases

A triple net lease, commonly abbreviated as NNN, is a lease structure in which the tenant pays base rent plus all three major categories of property operating expenses: property taxes, property insurance, and maintenance (including common area maintenance, or CAM). The "triple" in triple net refers to these three expense categories. Under a true NNN lease, the landlord receives rent that is almost entirely net income, with minimal deductions for operating costs.

How NNN Leases Work in Practice

In a standard NNN lease, the tenant's monthly payment consists of two components. The first is the base rent, which is a fixed amount (often with scheduled annual increases of 1% to 3%). The second component covers the tenant's proportionate share of property taxes, insurance premiums, and maintenance costs. These additional charges are sometimes called "triple net charges" or "NNN charges."

For single-tenant properties, the math is straightforward: the tenant pays 100% of all operating expenses. For multi-tenant NNN properties, each tenant pays a proportionate share based on the square footage they occupy relative to the total leasable area of the building.

Consider a 20,000-square-foot retail strip center with four tenants on NNN leases. If the annual property taxes are $40,000, insurance costs $12,000, and CAM expenses total $28,000, the total reimbursable expenses are $80,000. A tenant occupying 5,000 square feet (25% of the building) would pay $20,000 annually in NNN charges on top of their base rent.

Where NNN Leases Are Most Common

NNN leases dominate certain property types. Single-tenant retail properties (such as bank branches, fast food restaurants, pharmacies, and dollar stores) are almost always structured as NNN. Industrial and warehouse properties also commonly use NNN or modified net structures. The NNN model is particularly prevalent in sale-leaseback transactions, where a company sells its real estate and then leases it back from the new owner under long-term NNN terms.

Credit tenants (nationally recognized companies with investment-grade credit ratings) signing long-term NNN leases create what the industry calls "bond-like" income streams. Properties with these lease profiles trade at premium valuations and attract the most favorable financing terms.

Full-Service Gross Leases

A full-service gross lease sits at the opposite end of the spectrum from NNN. Under this structure, the tenant pays a single rental amount that covers everything: base rent plus all operating expenses including property taxes, insurance, maintenance, utilities, janitorial services, and common area costs. The landlord is responsible for paying all of these expenses out of the gross rent collected.

How Gross Leases Work in Practice

With a gross lease, the tenant's obligation is simple and predictable. They pay one monthly amount and the landlord handles everything else. If a tenant signs a gross lease for $30 per square foot per year, that $30 covers their share of all building operating costs. The landlord then uses a portion of that rent to pay taxes, insurance, utilities, janitorial, and maintenance, keeping whatever remains as net income.

This structure requires the landlord to accurately estimate operating expenses when setting rental rates. If expenses rise faster than anticipated, the landlord's profit margin shrinks. Conversely, if the landlord manages expenses efficiently, profits improve. The financial risk of operating cost fluctuations rests squarely on the landlord's shoulders.

Where Gross Leases Are Most Common

Full-service gross leases are the standard in Class A and Class B office buildings, particularly in major metropolitan markets. Office tenants generally prefer the simplicity of a single, predictable monthly payment without the administrative burden of tracking and reimbursing individual expense categories. Multi-tenant office buildings in central business districts are overwhelmingly leased on a gross or modified gross basis.

Some mixed-use and co-working spaces also use gross lease structures to simplify billing for smaller tenants who may lack the sophistication or desire to manage variable expense reimbursements.

Modified Gross Leases

A modified gross lease falls between the NNN and full-service gross structures. Under a modified gross arrangement, the landlord and tenant negotiate a custom allocation of operating expenses. There is no single standard for what a "modified gross" lease looks like, and the specific expense split varies from deal to deal.

Common Modified Gross Structures

Several variations appear frequently in the market:

  • Base Year Stop: The landlord pays all operating expenses in the first year of the lease (the "base year"). In subsequent years, the tenant pays their proportionate share of any increases above the base year amount. This is the most common modified gross structure in office leasing.
  • Expense Stop: Similar to the base year approach, but the landlord sets a fixed dollar amount per square foot as the expense cap rather than using actual first-year costs. The tenant pays their share of any expenses exceeding that cap.
  • Partial Net: The tenant pays base rent plus one or two specific expense categories (for example, the tenant covers utilities and janitorial while the landlord covers taxes, insurance, and structural maintenance).
  • Hybrid Structures: Some leases combine elements, such as NNN for taxes and insurance but gross for utilities and janitorial. The specific allocation depends on market norms, negotiating leverage, and property characteristics.

Where Modified Gross Leases Are Most Common

Modified gross leases appear most frequently in suburban office properties, medical office buildings, and Class B/C multi-tenant properties. They are also common in markets where neither NNN nor full-service gross is the dominant standard. Many landlords prefer modified gross structures because they share expense risk with tenants while still offering a relatively simple lease structure that tenants find acceptable.

How Each Lease Type Affects NOI Calculations

Net Operating Income (NOI) is the single most important metric in commercial real estate valuation and loan underwriting. The lease type directly shapes how NOI is calculated, and understanding this connection is essential for borrowers seeking financing.

NOI Calculation by Lease Type

For NNN-leased properties, NOI is relatively straightforward. Since tenants pay operating expenses directly (or reimburse the landlord), the base rent collected is close to the actual NOI. The landlord may still incur minor expenses such as management fees or vacancy-related costs, but the bulk of operating expenses flow through to tenants.

For gross-leased properties, the lender must subtract all operating expenses from gross rent to arrive at NOI. This creates more variability and requires the underwriter to make assumptions about future expense levels. If the lender projects expenses higher than the borrower expects, the resulting NOI will be lower, and the loan amount will shrink accordingly.

For modified gross properties, the calculation falls somewhere in between. The underwriter must carefully parse the lease to determine which expenses the landlord bears and which are reimbursed by tenants. This analysis requires detailed review of each lease's expense provisions, base year amounts, and reimbursement caps.

Lender Preferences by Lease Type

Different lender types have distinct preferences when it comes to lease structures, and understanding these preferences helps borrowers target the right capital sources for their properties.

Life Company and CMBS Lenders

Life insurance companies and CMBS lenders strongly prefer NNN-leased properties with credit tenants and long remaining lease terms. These lenders are drawn to the bond-like quality of NNN income streams, which align with their own long-term investment horizons. A single-tenant NNN property leased to a national retailer with 15 years remaining on the lease can command the most aggressive pricing and highest proceeds from these capital sources.

When underwriting gross-leased properties, these lenders apply more conservative expense assumptions. They may use higher expense ratios than the property's actual historical performance indicates, building in a cushion for potential cost increases. This conservatism can reduce the loan amount available compared to what a NNN property of similar value would receive.

Bank and Credit Union Lenders

Local and regional banks tend to be more flexible regarding lease type, especially for relationship borrowers. They evaluate the full picture, including the borrower's track record, the property's location, tenant quality, and lease terms. Banks are often comfortable with modified gross and gross-leased properties, particularly when the borrower has a history of managing these assets profitably.

That said, banks still recognize the risk differential between lease types. A gross-leased office building will typically receive more conservative underwriting treatment than a NNN-leased retail center, even from the same bank. The borrower may need to provide additional reserves or accept a slightly lower loan-to-value ratio.

Agency Lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac)

For multifamily properties, which are the exclusive domain of agency lenders, lease structures are inherently gross. Apartment tenants pay a single rent amount, and the landlord covers all property operating expenses. Agency lenders are comfortable with this structure because multifamily is their sole focus, and their underwriting models are calibrated for gross lease dynamics. They apply standardized expense ratios and stress-test for rising costs as part of their standard underwriting process.

Tenant Reimbursements and Pass-Through Expenses

Tenant reimbursements are a critical component of income for properties that use NNN or modified gross leases. These payments, also known as expense recoveries or pass-throughs, represent the tenant's share of property operating costs. Lenders scrutinize reimbursement structures closely during underwriting because they directly affect the property's effective income and risk profile.

How Reimbursements Are Calculated

In a multi-tenant NNN property, each tenant's reimbursement is calculated based on their proportionate share of the building. The standard formula divides the tenant's leased square footage by the building's total leasable square footage. This percentage is then applied to each reimbursable expense category.

For example, in a 100,000-square-foot office park with $300,000 in annual reimbursable expenses, a tenant leasing 10,000 square feet would owe $30,000 per year (10% of total expenses). Landlords typically estimate annual expenses and bill tenants monthly based on those estimates, then perform a year-end reconciliation to true up actual versus estimated amounts.

Recovery Rate and Its Importance

Lenders pay close attention to the property's "recovery rate," which measures how much of total operating expenses is recovered through tenant reimbursements. A recovery rate of 95% or higher indicates that nearly all expenses are passed through to tenants, which is typical for a well-leased NNN property. A recovery rate of 70% to 80% might indicate modified gross leases, vacant space (which generates no reimbursements), or lease provisions that cap certain reimbursable amounts.

A low recovery rate is a red flag for underwriters. It suggests that the landlord bears significant expense risk, which could erode cash flow if costs rise. Lenders may adjust their NOI projections downward or require larger reserves to compensate for the gap between actual expenses and tenant reimbursements.

CAM Reconciliation and Audit Rights

Most commercial leases with reimbursement provisions include clauses governing Common Area Maintenance (CAM) reconciliation. The landlord bills estimated CAM charges throughout the year and then performs an annual reconciliation comparing estimates to actual expenses. If actual costs exceeded estimates, the tenant owes the difference. If estimates were too high, the landlord issues a credit.

Many leases also grant tenants the right to audit the landlord's CAM calculations. Lenders view strong CAM provisions favorably because transparent expense tracking reduces disputes and ensures consistent cash flow. When reviewing a loan application, underwriters may request copies of recent CAM reconciliation statements to verify that the landlord's reimbursement practices are sound and consistent.

Impact on DSCR and Debt Yield

Lease type affects two of the most important loan sizing metrics: Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) and debt yield. Both metrics rely on NOI as their starting point, and since lease type determines how NOI is calculated and how stable it remains over time, the choice of lease structure ripples through every aspect of loan sizing.

DSCR Considerations

DSCR measures the property's ability to cover its annual debt service payments from net operating income. Most lenders require a minimum DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x, meaning the property must generate at least 20% to 25% more income than is needed to service the debt.

For NNN-leased properties, DSCR tends to remain stable over the life of the loan because the landlord's income is insulated from rising operating expenses. If property taxes increase by 15% or insurance premiums spike, those costs pass through to tenants, leaving the landlord's NOI (and therefore DSCR) largely unaffected.

For gross-leased properties, DSCR can erode over time if operating expenses rise faster than rental income. A property that starts with a healthy 1.35x DSCR could see that ratio decline to 1.15x or below if expenses outpace rent growth. Lenders account for this risk by either requiring a higher initial DSCR for gross-leased properties or stress-testing the NOI with elevated expense growth assumptions.

Debt Yield Considerations

Debt yield, calculated as NOI divided by loan amount, serves as a lender's measure of return on their investment independent of interest rate or amortization. Typical minimum debt yield requirements range from 8% to 10% depending on the lender and property type.

Because NNN leases produce more predictable NOI, lenders may accept slightly lower debt yield thresholds for well-leased NNN properties compared to gross-leased assets. A lender that requires a 9% minimum debt yield for a gross-leased office building might accept 8.5% for a NNN retail center with long-term credit tenants. This seemingly small difference can translate to meaningful additional loan proceeds. On a property generating $500,000 in NOI, the difference between an 8.5% and 9% debt yield requirement represents approximately $327,000 in additional borrowing capacity.

Expense Growth and Long-Term Stability

Lenders underwrite loans for the full term, not just the first year. This long-term perspective magnifies the importance of lease type. A NNN lease with 2% annual rent escalations and full expense pass-throughs provides a clear, upward-trending income trajectory. A gross lease with the same rent escalations but no expense pass-throughs faces the risk that expense growth will outstrip income growth over time, gradually compressing margins.

Sophisticated lenders model these scenarios explicitly. They project NOI over the full loan term under both base-case and stress-case assumptions, testing how different expense growth rates affect DSCR and debt yield in each year. Properties with NNN leases perform better under stress testing because the landlord's income is buffered from expense volatility.

Lease Analysis in the Underwriting Process

When you submit a loan application for a commercial property, the lender's underwriting team will conduct a thorough review of every lease on the property. This lease analysis, sometimes called a "lease abstract" review, is one of the most time-intensive parts of the underwriting process, and the level of detail can surprise borrowers who are unfamiliar with institutional lending standards.

What Lenders Review in Each Lease

For each tenant lease, underwriters examine the following elements:

  • Lease type and expense structure: Whether NNN, gross, or modified gross, and exactly which expenses the tenant is responsible for
  • Remaining lease term: How many years remain before expiration, including renewal option periods
  • Rent escalation provisions: Fixed annual increases, CPI adjustments, or fair market value resets
  • Tenant creditworthiness: Financial strength of the tenant, especially for larger tenants that represent a significant share of property income
  • Termination and co-tenancy clauses: Any provisions that allow the tenant to exit the lease early, such as kick-out clauses tied to sales performance or co-tenancy requirements
  • Expense cap provisions: Any limits on the amount of expenses that can be passed through to tenants, which could expose the landlord to cost overruns
  • Renewal option terms: Whether renewal rents are at market rate, at a fixed amount, or at a percentage increase over the expiring rent
  • Subletting and assignment rights: Whether the tenant can transfer the lease to another party

How Lease Quality Affects Loan Terms

The quality of the lease portfolio, not just the lease type, determines how aggressively a lender will underwrite the property. A property with long-term NNN leases, investment-grade tenants, and strong rent escalation provisions represents the ideal scenario. At the other end, a property with short-term gross leases, local tenants with limited financial disclosure, and flat rents presents more challenges.

Between these extremes, most properties fall somewhere in the middle. A suburban office building might have a mix of modified gross leases with varying terms, a blend of national and local tenants, and base year expense stops. The underwriter must analyze each lease individually and then model the aggregate income stream to determine the property's overall risk profile.

Preparing Your Lease Package for Lenders

Borrowers can streamline the underwriting process and improve their chances of favorable terms by preparing a well-organized lease package that includes:

  • Complete copies of all executed leases, including amendments and extensions
  • A current rent roll showing tenant names, suite numbers, square footage, base rent, NNN charges, lease start and end dates, and any scheduled rent increases
  • Trailing 12-month income and expense statements showing actual reimbursement collections
  • CAM reconciliation statements for the most recent two to three years
  • Tenant estoppel certificates confirming lease terms and current payment status
  • Financial statements or credit reports for tenants representing more than 10% to 15% of total rental income

Providing this information upfront signals professionalism and can accelerate the underwriting timeline by several weeks. Lenders are more likely to offer favorable terms when they can verify the property's income stream quickly and without repeated document requests.
