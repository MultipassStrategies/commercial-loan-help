Documents Required for a Commercial Loan: The Complete Checklist

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Total documents: 15 to 25+
  • Personal: Tax returns, PFS, credit authorization
  • Business: P&L, balance sheet, tax returns
  • Property: Rent roll, T12, photos
  • Entity: Operating agreement, articles, EIN letter
  • Prep time: 1 to 2 weeks

Why Documentation Matters

Every commercial loan begins with paperwork. While the volume of documents may feel overwhelming at first, each item in a lender's checklist serves a specific purpose: to verify who you are, confirm your ability to repay, evaluate the property securing the loan, and ensure the legal structure of the deal is sound. Understanding why lenders need each document helps you approach the process with confidence rather than frustration.

Incomplete or poorly organized loan packages are one of the most common reasons commercial loan applications stall or get declined. When a lender receives a submission missing key items, the deal moves to the bottom of the underwriting queue while the loan officer chases the borrower for the remaining pieces. Each round of back-and-forth can add days or even weeks to the timeline. On the other hand, borrowers who submit a complete, well-organized package from day one consistently close faster and often receive more competitive terms, because they signal to the lender that the deal is serious and the borrower is professional.

The documents required for a commercial loan generally fall into four categories: personal and guarantor documents, business financial documents, property-specific documents, and entity and legal documents. The exact list varies by lender and loan type, but the core requirements are remarkably consistent across the industry. Below, we walk through every category in detail so you know exactly what to prepare before you apply.

Personal and Guarantor Documents

Nearly every commercial loan requires personal documentation from the principals (owners and guarantors) behind the deal. Even when the borrowing entity is an LLC or corporation, lenders look through the entity to the individuals who control and guarantee the loan. This section covers the personal documents you should have ready.

Personal Tax Returns

Lenders require two to three years of complete personal federal tax returns for every individual who owns 20% or more of the borrowing entity, as well as any additional guarantors. "Complete" means all pages, all schedules, and all K-1s from partnerships, S-corporations, or trusts. The tax returns allow the lender to verify your income, identify all sources of revenue, and cross-check the numbers against other documents in the package. If you have filed extensions for the most recent tax year, provide the extension filing along with a copy of your most recent available return.

Personal Financial Statement (PFS)

A personal financial statement is a one-page or two-page summary of your assets, liabilities, and net worth as of a specific date. Every guarantor must submit one. Most lenders accept the SBA Form 413 or their own proprietary template. The PFS should include all real estate owned (with current market values and mortgage balances), bank and investment account balances, retirement accounts, vehicle values, and all outstanding debts. Accuracy is critical, because lenders will compare your PFS against your tax returns and credit report for consistency.

Credit Authorization

You will need to sign a credit authorization form that allows the lender to pull your credit report. Commercial lenders typically review both your credit score and the detailed credit history, looking for patterns of timely payment, total outstanding debt, and any derogatory marks such as collections, judgments, or bankruptcies. Some lenders perform a soft pull during pre-qualification and a hard pull only after you have accepted a term sheet, so ask about the process upfront if you are concerned about the impact on your score.

Government-Issued Identification

A copy of a valid driver's license or passport is required for every guarantor. This is used for identity verification, particularly for anti-money-laundering (AML) and know-your-customer (KYC) compliance requirements that all regulated lenders must follow.

Resume or Professional Background

Many lenders, especially SBA lenders, ask for a brief resume or bio for each principal. This document should highlight relevant industry experience, management background, and any prior real estate ownership or business operation history. Lenders want to see that the people behind the loan have the skills and experience needed to successfully operate the business or manage the property.

Business Financial Documents

The business financials are the heart of any commercial loan application. These documents tell the lender whether your business generates enough income to service the proposed debt, whether the business is financially stable, and whether there are any red flags in the financial history.

Business Tax Returns

Lenders require two to three years of complete business federal tax returns for the borrowing entity and any affiliated businesses. For partnerships and S-corporations, this means the full 1065 or 1120S return with all schedules. For sole proprietorships, the business income is reported on Schedule C of the personal return. As with personal returns, "complete" means every page and every schedule, not just the first two pages.

Profit and Loss Statement (P&L)

A current year-to-date profit and loss statement is required for the operating business. This document should be dated within 60 to 90 days of the loan application. The P&L shows revenue, cost of goods sold, gross profit, operating expenses, and net income for the current period. Lenders use it to confirm that the business is still performing in line with the trends shown in the tax returns. If your business is seasonal, be prepared to provide a trailing 12-month P&L as well, so the lender can see a full annual cycle.

Balance Sheet

A current balance sheet (also dated within 60 to 90 days) accompanies the P&L. The balance sheet lists the business's assets, liabilities, and equity as of a specific date. Lenders analyze the balance sheet for liquidity (cash and near-cash assets), leverage (total debt relative to equity), and working capital (current assets minus current liabilities). A strong balance sheet reassures the lender that the business has sufficient reserves to handle unexpected expenses or revenue dips.

Business Debt Schedule

The debt schedule is a summary of all outstanding business debts, including the lender name, original loan amount, current balance, monthly payment, interest rate, maturity date, and collateral. This document helps the lender calculate your global debt service coverage ratio (DSCR), which measures whether your total income is sufficient to cover all existing debt payments plus the proposed new loan payment. Some lenders provide a template for this; others simply ask for the information in a spreadsheet format.

Bank Statements

Two to three months of business bank statements are commonly requested to verify cash flow, confirm account balances listed on the PFS or balance sheet, and identify the source of any large deposits. Lenders look for consistent cash flow patterns and sufficient reserves. If you maintain accounts at multiple banks, provide statements for all of them.

Accounts Receivable and Payable Aging

For businesses with significant receivables or payables, some lenders request an aging report that breaks down outstanding amounts by how long they have been outstanding (current, 30 days, 60 days, 90+ days). Large amounts in the 90+ day category can raise concerns about collection risk or cash flow management.

Property-Specific Documents

Because the property itself serves as collateral for the loan, lenders need detailed information about the real estate being financed. The property documents help the lender evaluate the asset's value, income potential, physical condition, and any environmental or legal risks.

Rent Roll

For income-producing properties, a current rent roll is one of the most important documents in the package. The rent roll is a detailed listing of every tenant, their unit or suite number, lease start and end dates, monthly rent amount, security deposit, and any rent escalations or concessions. The rent roll gives the lender a snapshot of the property's current income and occupancy level. It should be dated as of the most recent month-end.

Trailing 12-Month Operating Statement (T12)

The T12 is a month-by-month income and expense statement covering the most recent 12 months of property operations. It includes all revenue (rent, parking, laundry, other income) and all operating expenses (property taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance, management fees, and reserves). Lenders use the T12 to calculate the property's net operating income (NOI) and to verify that the income shown on the rent roll is actually being collected. If the property is newly acquired, provide whatever operating history is available from the seller.

Prior Year Operating Statements

In addition to the T12, many lenders request two to three years of historical operating statements to see performance trends over time. Are revenues growing or declining? Are expenses stable or escalating? These trends influence the lender's underwriting assumptions and can affect the loan terms you receive.

Lease Copies

For multi-tenant properties, lenders may request copies of all executed leases, especially for the largest tenants. Leases confirm the rental rates, terms, renewal options, and any special provisions (such as tenant improvement allowances or free rent periods) that affect the property's income stream. For single-tenant properties, the lease is particularly critical because the entire income depends on that one tenant's financial strength and commitment.

Property Photos

Exterior and interior photographs of the property help the lender assess the asset's condition and marketability before ordering a formal appraisal. High-quality photos of the building exterior, common areas, representative unit interiors, parking areas, and any deferred maintenance issues give the lender a visual overview of what they are financing.

Purchase Agreement or Letter of Intent

If the loan is for a property acquisition, the lender needs a copy of the fully executed purchase and sale agreement or, at the preliminary stage, a signed letter of intent. This document establishes the purchase price, closing timeline, contingencies, and other deal terms that the lender must factor into its underwriting.

Environmental Reports

A Phase I Environmental Site Assessment is required for nearly all commercial real estate loans. This report evaluates the property's history and current conditions for potential environmental contamination. Phase I reports are typically ordered by the lender after preliminary approval, but if you already have a recent Phase I (completed within the last 180 days), providing it upfront can save time. If the Phase I identifies areas of concern, a Phase II assessment involving soil or groundwater testing may be required.

Entity and Legal Documents

Commercial loans are made to legal entities (LLCs, corporations, partnerships), not to individuals directly. Lenders need to confirm the entity's legal standing, ownership structure, and authority to borrow.

Operating Agreement or Bylaws

For LLCs, the operating agreement defines the ownership percentages, management structure, and decision-making authority. For corporations, the bylaws serve a similar function. The lender reviews this document to confirm who owns the entity, who has authority to sign loan documents, and whether there are any restrictions on borrowing or encumbering assets.

Articles of Incorporation or Organization

The articles of incorporation (for corporations) or articles of organization (for LLCs) are the founding documents filed with the state. These confirm the entity's legal name, formation date, registered agent, and state of formation. Lenders use these documents to verify that the entity is properly formed and in good standing.

Certificate of Good Standing

A certificate of good standing (sometimes called a certificate of existence or certificate of status) is issued by the state where the entity was formed. It confirms that the entity is current on all state filings and fees. Most lenders require a certificate dated within 30 to 60 days of closing. You can usually obtain one online from your state's Secretary of State website for a small fee.

EIN Verification Letter

The IRS assigns an Employer Identification Number (EIN) to every business entity. The EIN verification letter (IRS Form CP 575 or 147C) confirms the entity's tax identification number. Lenders need this to verify the entity's identity and for tax reporting purposes. If you have lost your original EIN letter, you can request a replacement (Form 147C) by calling the IRS Business and Specialty Tax Line.

Organizational Chart

For entities with complex ownership structures (such as a holding company that owns an operating company, or multiple members with varying ownership percentages), lenders often request an organizational chart showing all related entities, ownership percentages, and the relationships between them. This chart helps the lender understand the full picture of who controls the borrowing entity and any affiliated businesses.

Additional Documents by Loan Type

While the documents listed above form the core package for any commercial loan, different loan programs and lender types have additional requirements. Here is what to expect for the most common loan types.

SBA Loans (7(a) and 504)

SBA loans are among the most documentation-intensive commercial loan programs. In addition to the standard package, SBA lenders require several government-specific forms: SBA Form 1919 (Borrower Information Form), SBA Form 413 (Personal Financial Statement, if the lender does not accept an alternative), SBA Form 912 (Statement of Personal History for background checks), and a formal business plan with financial projections. You must also provide proof of equity injection (showing where the down payment is coming from), documentation of any franchise agreements, and evidence that you were unable to obtain comparable financing from non-government sources (the "credit elsewhere" test). Because the SBA guarantees a portion of the loan, the documentation standards are stricter and the review process is more thorough than for conventional programs.

CMBS Loans

Conduit or CMBS (Commercial Mortgage-Backed Securities) loans place heavy emphasis on property-level documentation because these loans are securitized and sold to bond investors. Expect to provide extremely detailed property documents, including a full rent roll with lease abstracts, three or more years of historical operating statements, copies of all leases, a property condition report, seismic reports (in applicable zones), zoning verification letters, and detailed capital expenditure histories. CMBS lenders also require borrowing entity documents that demonstrate the entity is a single-purpose, bankruptcy-remote entity, which means the LLC's sole purpose is to own and operate the subject property.

Construction Loans

Construction financing adds a layer of documentation beyond what is required for a standard acquisition or refinance. You will need to provide a detailed construction budget with line-item costs, a complete set of architectural plans and specifications, all building permits and approvals, a construction timeline with milestones, a general contractor agreement, the contractor's license and insurance certificates, and proof of the contractor's financial stability (often including the contractor's own financial statements). If the project involves ground-up development, the lender may also require soil reports, surveys, utility availability letters, and zoning confirmation.

Bridge Loans

Bridge lenders tend to have lighter documentation requirements than banks or SBA lenders, especially for experienced borrowers with strong track records. The focus is primarily on the property's value and exit strategy. You may need less historical business documentation but more detail about the property's value-add potential, the renovation scope (if applicable), and the planned permanent financing or sale that will repay the bridge loan. A clear, realistic exit strategy is the single most important item for a bridge lender.

How to Organize Your Loan Package

A well-organized loan package does more than save time. It signals professionalism and builds confidence with your lender from the first interaction. Here is a proven approach to organizing your submission.

  1. Create a master folder with four subfolders. Label them "Personal," "Business," "Property," and "Entity." Place each document in its appropriate subfolder. This mirrors how most underwriters organize their own files, which makes their job easier and speeds up the review.
  2. Name files clearly. Use descriptive file names such as "2025_Personal_Tax_Return_John_Smith.pdf" or "Current_Rent_Roll_March_2026.pdf" rather than generic names like "scan001.pdf." Clear naming eliminates confusion and prevents the lender from asking you to identify documents.
  3. Include a document index. Create a one-page cover sheet listing every document in the package, organized by category, with checkmarks confirming each item is included. This helps the lender quickly verify completeness and gives you a final checklist before submitting.
  4. Submit everything in PDF format. Convert spreadsheets, word documents, and images to PDF before submitting. PDFs are universally compatible, maintain formatting, and are easier for lenders to file. If the lender needs editable spreadsheets (such as for a rent roll or operating statement), provide both the PDF and the original file.
  5. Send it all at once. Resist the temptation to submit documents piecemeal as you gather them. A complete package submitted in one batch moves through underwriting far faster than a trickle of individual documents over several days or weeks.

Common Documentation Mistakes

Even experienced borrowers make documentation mistakes that slow down the loan process. Here are the most frequent errors and how to avoid them.

  • Incomplete tax returns. Submitting only the first two pages of a tax return is one of the most common mistakes. Lenders need every page, every schedule, and every K-1. Missing pages trigger an immediate request for the complete return, adding days to the timeline.
  • Outdated financial statements. Submitting a P&L or balance sheet that is more than 90 days old will almost always result in a request for updated statements. Keep your bookkeeping current so you can produce fresh financials quickly when the time comes.
  • Mismatched numbers. When the income on your tax return does not match the income on your P&L, or the assets on your balance sheet do not match your PFS, lenders will flag the discrepancies and ask for explanations. Reconcile your documents before submitting to catch and resolve any inconsistencies.
  • Missing signatures. Many forms (credit authorization, PFS, SBA forms) require signatures. Unsigned documents are invalid and will be returned. Review every form for signature lines before submitting.
  • Illegible scans. Poorly scanned documents with cut-off edges, dark shadows, or unreadable text create problems during underwriting. Use a flatbed scanner or a high-quality scanning app, and review each scan before including it in your package.
  • Omitting affiliated entities. If you own other businesses or properties, disclose them upfront. Lenders will discover them during the underwriting process (through tax returns, credit reports, or UCC searches), and failing to disclose them upfront can raise concerns about transparency.

Pro Tips for Faster Processing

Experienced commercial borrowers use these strategies to accelerate the documentation and approval process.

  • Build a "loan-ready" file before you need it. Keep a folder on your computer with current versions of all standard loan documents (tax returns, PFS, entity docs, property financials). Update it quarterly so you can respond to a lender's request within hours, not weeks.
  • Get your CPA involved early. Let your accountant know you are planning to apply for a loan. They can prepare interim financial statements, organize tax returns, and help you anticipate questions the lender may have about unusual items on your returns.
  • Ask for the lender's checklist before you start gathering documents. Every lender has a specific checklist, and while most are similar, each one has its quirks. Getting the actual checklist upfront ensures you do not waste time preparing documents the lender does not need or miss items they do require.
  • Prepare a deal summary or executive overview. A one-page summary of the transaction (who you are, what the property is, how much you need, and your proposed terms) helps the lender understand the deal at a glance and prioritize your file. Think of it as the cover letter for your loan application.
  • Respond to follow-up requests within 24 hours. When a lender asks for additional information during underwriting, speed matters. A 24-hour turnaround shows that you are organized and committed, and it keeps your file at the top of the underwriter's stack.
  • Use a secure file-sharing platform. Rather than emailing dozens of large attachments, use a secure platform (such as Dropbox, Google Drive, or a lender-provided portal) to share your documents. This makes it easy for the lender to access everything in one place and avoids email size limits.

What to Do When Lenders Request More Documents

Even after submitting a thorough initial package, you should expect follow-up requests from the lender during underwriting. This is normal and does not necessarily mean there is a problem with your application. Underwriters are trained to dig into the details, and they often need additional context to fully understand specific items in your financial history.

Common follow-up requests include explanation letters for unusual income fluctuations, large deposits in bank statements, or gaps in employment history. Lenders may also ask for updated financials if your application is still in process when a new quarter or year-end arrives. If the property has tenants, the underwriter might request estoppel certificates (signed confirmations from tenants verifying their lease terms, rent amounts, and security deposits).

The best way to handle follow-up requests is to respond quickly and thoroughly. Provide the requested document along with a brief explanation if the context is not immediately obvious. If a request seems unusual or you are unsure what the lender is looking for, call the loan officer and ask for clarification rather than guessing. A five-minute phone call can save days of back-and-forth.

Keep in mind that each round of follow-up requests typically adds three to five business days to the underwriting timeline. This is why submitting a complete initial package is so valuable: the fewer follow-up rounds needed, the faster you get to approval and closing.

Documents by Loan Stage

The documentation process is not all-or-nothing. Different documents are needed at different stages of the loan process, and understanding this timeline helps you manage your preparation efficiently.

Pre-Qualification Stage

At the pre-qualification stage, lenders need just enough information to determine whether the deal is viable and worth pursuing. Typically, this means a brief overview of the property (address, type, size, price or value), a summary of the borrower's financial position (approximate net worth, credit score range, and relevant experience), and basic business financials (most recent year's revenue and net income). Many lenders will issue a preliminary term sheet based on this limited information, which allows you to evaluate the proposed terms before investing time in the full documentation process.

Full Application Stage

Once you accept a term sheet, the lender issues a formal checklist and the full documentation process begins. This is when you submit the complete package described throughout this article: personal and business tax returns, financial statements, the PFS, entity documents, and property materials. The lender also orders third-party reports at this stage, including the appraisal, environmental assessment, and title search. The full application stage is the most document-intensive phase, and completing it efficiently is where your advance preparation pays off.

Closing Stage

The closing stage introduces a final set of documents, most of which are prepared by the lender, title company, or closing attorney rather than the borrower. These include the loan agreement, promissory note, mortgage or deed of trust, personal guaranty, environmental indemnity, UCC financing statements, title insurance policy, and proof of property insurance. The borrower's main responsibilities at closing are to review and sign the documents, provide proof of insurance (with the lender named as loss payee and additional insured), wire the equity injection or down payment, and confirm that any pre-closing conditions (such as tenant estoppels or final inspections) have been satisfied.

By understanding which documents belong to which stage, you can spread your preparation over time rather than scrambling to gather everything at once. Start assembling your core documents (tax returns, financial statements, entity documents) as soon as you begin considering a loan. Add property-specific documents as you identify the target property. By the time you receive a formal checklist from the lender, you will have most of the work already done.

Frequently Asked Questions

Three years is the standard for SBA loans and many conventional lenders, though some portfolio lenders and bridge lenders may accept only one or two years. The reason lenders want multiple years is to establish a trend line: they want to see whether your income is stable, growing, or declining over time.

If your business is newer and does not have three years of history, lenders will typically require all available returns plus interim financial statements and a detailed business plan or financial projection. Providing the full three years upfront, when available, avoids delays and signals to the lender that you are well-prepared and transparent about your financial history.

A personal financial statement (PFS) is a document that summarizes an individual's assets, liabilities, and net worth as of a specific date. For commercial loan applications, every guarantor (typically anyone who owns 20% or more of the borrowing entity) must submit a PFS. The form lists all assets (real estate, bank accounts, investments, retirement funds, vehicles, and other valuables) along with all liabilities (mortgages, car loans, credit card balances, student loans, and other debts). The difference between total assets and total liabilities equals your net worth.

Lenders use the PFS to evaluate the guarantor's overall financial strength and ability to support the loan if the business cannot. Most lenders accept the standard SBA Form 413 or their own proprietary template. Be sure to fill it out completely and accurately, as lenders will verify the information against your tax returns and credit report.

The appraisal is almost always ordered by the lender after preliminary approval or during the underwriting phase, and the lender must select the appraiser to ensure independence. Ordering your own appraisal before applying is generally unnecessary and could be a wasted expense, since many lenders will not accept a borrower-ordered appraisal due to regulatory requirements around appraiser independence.

These items give the lender enough information to evaluate the deal and issue a preliminary term sheet before committing to the cost of a full appraisal.

These include SBA Form 1919 (Borrower Information Form), SBA Form 413 (Personal Financial Statement) for each owner with 20% or more equity, SBA Form 912 (Statement of Personal History) for background checks, a detailed business plan with financial projections, proof of business insurance, and documentation of equity injection or down payment sources. If the business is a franchise, a copy of the franchise agreement is also required.

SBA lenders also require that the borrower demonstrate inability to obtain financing on reasonable terms from non-government sources, which is known as the credit elsewhere test. Because SBA loans are government-guaranteed, the documentation standards are stricter and the review process is more thorough than for conventional loans. Working with an experienced SBA lender or Certified Development Company (CDC) can help you prepare a complete package and avoid the repeated follow-up requests that slow down the process.

Lenders typically require interim financial statements (a profit and loss statement and balance sheet) that are dated within 90 days of the loan application. Some lenders are stricter and want statements dated within 60 days, especially for larger loan amounts or more complex transactions. If your application spans a calendar or fiscal year-end, you may also need to provide preliminary year-end statements until your tax returns for that year are finalized.

The key is that lenders want to see the most up-to-date picture of your business finances, not just a snapshot from many months ago. If your business is seasonal, be prepared to explain any fluctuations, and consider including a trailing 12-month statement to give the lender a full-year view. Having your bookkeeping current before you apply is one of the simplest ways to avoid delays in the loan process.
