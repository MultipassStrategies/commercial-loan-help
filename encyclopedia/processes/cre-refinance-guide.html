How to Refinance a Commercial Property: The Step-by-Step Guide

Whether you are chasing better terms, approaching maturity, or accessing equity, refinancing is one of the most important financial decisions a commercial property owner will make.

When Should You Refinance?

Refinancing a commercial property is not something you do on a whim. There are specific scenarios where refinancing makes strong financial sense:

  • Loan maturity approaching: Most commercial loans have 5 to 10 year terms with balloon payments at maturity. Refinancing is not optional; it is necessary. Start the process 6 to 12 months before your maturity date.
  • Rate improvement opportunity: If market rates have dropped significantly below your current rate, refinancing can reduce your annual debt service by tens of thousands of dollars. But you must account for prepayment penalties and closing costs.
  • Accessing equity (cash-out): If your property has appreciated in value or you have paid down substantial principal, a cash-out refinance lets you access that equity for new acquisitions, capital improvements, or other investments.
  • Switching rate structure: Moving from a floating rate to a fixed rate (or vice versa) can align your financing with your investment strategy and risk tolerance.
  • Property improvement completed: After a value-add renovation that increased NOI and property value, refinancing from a bridge loan into permanent financing at the new, higher value is a standard strategy.
  • Better loan terms available: Moving from recourse to non-recourse, extending the amortization schedule, removing onerous covenants, or accessing a different lender program can all justify refinancing.

Types of Commercial Real Estate Refinance

Rate-and-Term Refinance

The most straightforward type. You replace your existing loan with a new one at a different rate, term, or both. The new loan amount is equal to the existing loan balance (plus closing costs if rolled in). No cash out. This is the standard approach for loans approaching maturity.

Cash-Out Refinance

You borrow more than your current balance and receive the difference as cash. Most lenders limit cash-out to 65% to 70% LTV, which is lower than the maximum for rate-and-term refinancing. The property must demonstrate strong DSCR at the higher loan amount. Proceeds can be used for any purpose.

Construction Takeout (Bridge to Permanent)

After completing a construction or renovation project, refinancing from the bridge or construction loan into a long-term permanent loan. The new loan is based on the completed, stabilized property value rather than the original acquisition cost.

Debt Consolidation

Combining multiple loans (first mortgage, mezzanine, preferred equity) into a single, simplified loan structure. This can reduce total debt service and simplify property management.

The Refinancing Process: Step by Step

Step 1: Evaluate Your Current Loan

Before pursuing a refinance, understand your existing situation. Pull out your loan documents and identify:

  • Current interest rate and remaining term
  • Outstanding loan balance
  • Prepayment penalty provisions (yield maintenance, defeasance, step-down, or none)
  • Maturity date
  • Any extension options available

Step 2: Calculate the Breakeven Point

Determine whether refinancing makes financial sense by calculating how long it takes to recoup the costs:

Breakeven Formula

Breakeven (months) = Total Refinancing Costs / Monthly Payment Savings

Include all costs: prepayment penalty on the existing loan, origination fee on the new loan, appraisal, legal fees, title insurance, and escrow setup. If the breakeven period is longer than your expected hold period, refinancing may not make sense unless you are forced by maturity.

Step 3: Gather Your Documents

Having your documentation package ready before you approach lenders significantly speeds up the process. Standard documents include:

  • Current rent roll
  • Trailing 12-month (T12) operating statements
  • Two to three years of property tax returns
  • Two to three years of personal and business tax returns
  • Personal Financial Statement (PFS)
  • Entity documents (operating agreement, articles of organization)
  • Schedule of real estate owned
  • Current loan payoff statement
  • Recent property photos

Step 4: Shop Lenders and Compare Terms

Do not simply call your current lender and accept their offer. Shopping multiple lenders is one of the most effective ways to get competitive terms. Different lenders have different appetites, and the variance in quotes can be significant.

Step 5: Application and Underwriting

Once you select a lender (or receive a term sheet you want to pursue), submit your full application with the complete documentation package. The lender will order:

  • A commercial appraisal (2 to 4 weeks, $3,000 to $10,000 depending on property size)
  • A Phase I Environmental Site Assessment (if not recently completed)
  • A title search and title insurance commitment
  • A property condition report (for some lenders)

Step 6: Closing

Once the loan is approved, the closing process involves executing legal documents, funding the new loan, paying off the existing loan (including any prepayment penalty), and recording the new mortgage. The entire process from application to closing typically takes 30 to 90 days depending on the lender type.

Prepayment Penalties Explained

Before refinancing, you must understand the prepayment provisions in your current loan. Prepayment penalties can make refinancing prohibitively expensive or nearly free, depending on your loan structure.

  • Yield maintenance: A "make-whole" penalty that ensures the lender receives the same yield as if you held the loan to maturity. Calculated based on the present value of remaining payments discounted at a Treasury rate. Can be very expensive, especially if rates have risen since origination.
  • Defeasance: Instead of paying a penalty, you purchase a portfolio of government securities (typically Treasury bonds) that replicate the remaining loan payments. The securities become the new collateral, releasing your property from the mortgage. Common in CMBS loans. Requires a defeasance consultant and can cost $50,000 or more in total.
  • Step-down: A declining percentage based on remaining term. For example, 5% in year one, 4% in year two, 3% in year three, and so on. The most borrower-friendly structure and the easiest to calculate.
  • No penalty or open period: Some loans have an open period (typically the last 3 to 6 months before maturity) during which no prepayment penalty applies. Bank loans and bridge loans often have minimal or no prepayment penalties.

SBA Refinance Options

The SBA offers specific refinance programs for existing commercial property owners:

  • SBA 504 Refinance: Allows owner-occupied property owners to refinance existing debt (and in some cases access limited cash for eligible business expenses). 90% LTV with the 504 structure. Fixed rate on the CDC portion.
  • SBA 7(a) Refinance: More flexible; can refinance non-SBA debt, access working capital, and consolidate business debts. Maximum $5 million.

SBA refinancing is particularly valuable for small business owners who occupy their property and want to reduce monthly payments or lock in a fixed rate.

How to Determine if Refinancing Makes Sense

Run through this cost-benefit checklist:

  1. Calculate your current annual debt service (monthly payment multiplied by 12)
  2. Estimate the new annual debt service based on the new loan terms
  3. Calculate annual savings (current debt service minus new debt service)
  4. Total all refinancing costs (origination, appraisal, legal, title, prepayment penalty)
  5. Divide total costs by annual savings to get the breakeven period in years
  6. Compare breakeven to your hold period. If you plan to hold for 5 more years and the breakeven is 18 months, refinancing makes sense. If the breakeven is 4 years, it is a closer call.

Common Refinancing Mistakes

  • Waiting too long: Starting the refinance process only 2 to 3 months before maturity leaves very little room for complications. Begin 6 to 12 months early.
  • Ignoring prepayment penalties: A borrower who sees great new rates but forgets about a 5% yield maintenance penalty may find the savings wiped out by the penalty cost.
  • Not shopping lenders: Accepting the first term sheet without comparison shopping can cost tens of thousands of dollars over the loan term.
  • Underestimating the appraisal: If your property appraises lower than expected, the lender may reduce the loan amount, requiring you to bring cash to closing or accept less favorable terms.
  • Neglecting property condition: Deferred maintenance, fire code violations, or life safety issues discovered during the lender's property inspection can delay or derail the refinance.
  • Incomplete documentation: Missing or outdated financial documents are the most common cause of refinancing delays. Have everything ready before you apply.
