The Commercial Loan Application Process: From Start to Funding

Commercial lending is fundamentally different from residential. Here is what to expect at every stage, what documents to prepare, and how to avoid the most common delays.

Why Commercial Lending Differs From Residential

If you are accustomed to residential mortgages, the commercial loan process will feel very different. Residential lending is largely standardized: Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac guidelines, automated underwriting systems, and predictable timelines. Commercial lending is far more customized.

Each commercial deal is evaluated on its own merits. The property's income, the borrower's experience, the market conditions, and the loan structure all factor into the decision. There is no automated approval system. A human underwriter analyzes every aspect of your deal, which is why the process takes longer and requires more documentation.

Step 1: Pre-Qualification and Loan Sizing

The process begins with an initial conversation between the borrower and a lender (or loan advisor) to determine if the deal is viable and approximately what size loan the property can support.

During pre-qualification, the lender evaluates:

  • Property type and location: Not all lenders work with all property types. A lender that specializes in multifamily may not finance restaurants.
  • Borrower experience and credit: How many similar deals have you completed? What is your approximate credit score?
  • Estimated property value and income: What are the current rents? What is the occupancy? What are the operating expenses?
  • Loan amount needed and use of proceeds: Acquisition, refinance, cash-out, construction?
  • Preliminary DSCR and LTV: Does the property's income support the loan amount at current rates?

Pre-qualification is not a commitment. It is a preliminary assessment that tells you whether it is worth investing time and money into a full application.

Step 2: Choosing a Lender

Choosing the right lender is one of the most consequential decisions in the process. Different lenders offer dramatically different terms, and the wrong choice can cost tens of thousands of dollars or cause you to miss a closing deadline.

Factors to consider:

  • Loan type match: Make sure the lender offers the specific loan product you need (SBA, CMBS, agency, bridge, etc.).
  • Property type experience: Lenders with experience in your property type underwrite faster and ask fewer unnecessary questions.
  • Speed and reliability: Ask for references and recent closing examples. Can they meet your timeline?
  • Terms offered: Compare rate, fees, amortization, prepayment provisions, and recourse requirements.
  • Responsiveness: How quickly does the lender respond to questions and requests? This is a preview of the entire loan experience.

Step 3: Letter of Intent (LOI) or Term Sheet

Once a lender is interested, they issue a Letter of Intent (LOI) or term sheet that outlines the proposed loan terms. This is not a binding commitment, but it establishes the framework for the deal.

A typical term sheet includes:

  • Loan amount
  • Interest rate (or rate range)
  • Loan term and amortization schedule
  • Recourse provisions
  • Prepayment penalty structure
  • Estimated closing costs and fees
  • Required reserves and escrows
  • Conditions for approval
  • Application deposit or good faith deposit

Review the term sheet carefully. Pay special attention to the prepayment provisions, recourse requirements, and any conditions that could affect the loan later. If terms are negotiable (and they often are), this is the time to negotiate.

Step 4: Full Application and Document Submission

After accepting the term sheet, you submit the full application package. This is the most document-intensive phase of the process. Having everything organized and ready accelerates the timeline significantly.

Complete Document Checklist

Borrower and Guarantor Documents:

  • Personal Financial Statement (PFS) for each principal and guarantor
  • Two to three years of personal tax returns
  • Two to three years of business tax returns (for all related entities)
  • Resume or bio highlighting relevant CRE experience
  • Authorization to run credit
  • Schedule of real estate owned (all properties, values, debt, and income)
  • Photo identification

Property Documents:

  • Current rent roll with lease terms, expiration dates, and monthly rents
  • Trailing 12-month (T12) operating statements
  • Year-to-date income and expense statement
  • Two to three years of historical operating statements
  • Property tax bills (current and prior year)
  • Insurance declaration page
  • Property photos (exterior and interior)
  • Copies of all leases (for commercial tenants)
  • Capital improvement history and planned improvements

Entity Documents:

  • Operating Agreement or Partnership Agreement
  • Articles of Organization or Incorporation
  • Certificate of Good Standing
  • Organizational chart showing ownership structure

Deal-Specific Documents:

  • Purchase and Sale Agreement (for acquisitions)
  • Business plan or executive summary (for value-add or development)
  • Construction budget and timeline (for construction loans)
  • Existing loan payoff statement (for refinances)

Step 5: Appraisal and Environmental Review

Once the application is submitted, the lender orders third-party reports. These are typically the longest-lead items in the process.

  • Commercial appraisal: A licensed MAI appraiser evaluates the property using income, sales comparison, and cost approaches. Timeframe: 2 to 4 weeks. Cost: $3,000 to $10,000 depending on property size and complexity. The appraisal determines the property value that drives the LTV calculation.
  • Phase I Environmental Site Assessment (ESA): Identifies potential environmental contamination risks through historical research, site inspection, and regulatory database review. Timeframe: 2 to 3 weeks. Cost: $2,000 to $5,000. Required for virtually all commercial loans. If concerns are identified, a Phase II (soil and groundwater testing) may be required, adding 3 to 6 weeks and $10,000 to $30,000.
  • Property condition report: An engineering assessment of the building's physical condition, identifying deferred maintenance and estimated remaining useful life of major systems. Not required by all lenders.
  • Survey: A boundary survey confirming property lines, easements, and encroachments. Required by most lenders for acquisitions.

Step 6: Underwriting

With all documents and third-party reports in hand, the lender's underwriting team performs a detailed analysis of the deal. This typically takes 1 to 3 weeks.

Underwriters evaluate:

  • Property cash flow: Verifying the rent roll, operating expenses, and NOI. Calculating DSCR using the lender's underwriting assumptions (which may differ from the borrower's).
  • Property value: Reviewing the appraisal and determining the appropriate LTV.
  • Borrower strength: Credit score, net worth, liquidity, and experience. Background check and litigation search.
  • Market analysis: Evaluating the local market conditions, comparable properties, and demand drivers.
  • Risk assessment: Identifying potential risks and determining if they can be mitigated through loan structuring (reserves, guarantees, covenants).

It is common for the underwriter to come back with additional questions or document requests. Responding promptly keeps the timeline on track.

Step 7: Loan Committee Approval

For most lenders, the underwriter's analysis is presented to a loan committee (a group of senior decision-makers) for final approval. The committee may approve the loan as proposed, approve with modifications (lower loan amount, additional conditions), or decline.

Committee meetings typically happen on a set schedule (weekly or biweekly), so timing your submission to align with the committee calendar can save days or weeks.

Step 8: Closing and Funding

After approval, the closing process involves:

  • Lender's legal counsel drafts loan documents (loan agreement, promissory note, mortgage, guaranty, etc.)
  • Borrower's attorney reviews and negotiates documents
  • Title company prepares the title commitment and closing statement
  • Borrower deposits required equity, reserves, and closing costs
  • All parties sign documents at closing
  • Lender funds the loan
  • Title company records the mortgage and disburses funds

How to Speed Up the Process

  • Prepare documents before applying: Having your full package ready when you submit the application eliminates the most common source of delays.
  • Respond to lender requests within 24 hours: Every day you delay responding adds a day (or more) to the timeline.
  • Order the appraisal early: Ask the lender to engage the appraiser as soon as the term sheet is signed. Appraisals are consistently the longest-lead item.
  • Keep a recent Phase I on file: Most lenders accept Phase I reports that are less than 12 months old. If you have a recent one, it saves 2 to 3 weeks.
  • Use clean, well-organized financials: Messy or inconsistent financial statements trigger additional underwriter questions. Consider having your CPA prepare or review them before submission.
  • Choose a lender experienced in your deal type: Specialized lenders move faster because they understand the asset class and can underwrite with confidence.

Red Flags That Delay or Kill Deals

  • Low appraisal: If the property value comes in below expectations, the lender may reduce the loan amount, requiring additional equity from the borrower or restructuring the deal.
  • Environmental contamination: A Phase I that identifies recognized environmental conditions (RECs) triggers a Phase II investigation, adding weeks and significant cost. Active contamination can kill the deal entirely.
  • Title issues: Outstanding liens, boundary disputes, easement conflicts, or unresolved judgments must be cleared before closing.
  • DSCR shortfall: If the property's NOI cannot support the loan at the lender's minimum DSCR, the borrower must either reduce the loan amount, increase the down payment, or find alternative capital.
  • Borrower credit or legal issues: Bankruptcies, foreclosures, pending litigation, or criminal history discovered during the background check can disqualify the borrower.
  • Code violations: Fire code, ADA, or building code violations discovered during the property inspection must be resolved before or at closing.
