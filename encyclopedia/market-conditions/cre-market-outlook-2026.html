Commercial Real Estate Market Outlook 2026: Trends, Rates, and Opportunities

The commercial real estate market is navigating a historic period of transition. Here is what borrowers, investors, and operators need to know heading into the rest of 2026.

The Big Picture for 2026

Commercial real estate is at an inflection point. After two years of elevated interest rates, compressed transaction volume, and falling property values, the market is showing early signs of recovery. But the path forward is uneven.

U.S. property sales rose 12% year over year through mid-2025, and new loan origination surged more than 90% from the prior year period. There is $585 billion in dry powder waiting to be deployed into CRE. Transaction activity is picking up, but it remains well below the 2021 and 2022 peaks.

The biggest variable is the interest rate environment. The Fed has cut short-term rates meaningfully, but long-term rates remain stubbornly elevated. For borrowers, this means financing costs are lower than 2023 peaks but still far above the ultra-low rates that underwrote much of the 2020 to 2022 activity.

Meanwhile, the largest wave of commercial loan maturities in history is forcing action. Borrowers who have been waiting on the sidelines are running out of time to refinance, recapitalize, or sell.

Interest Rate Environment and Fed Outlook

The Federal Reserve reduced the federal funds rate three times in late 2024, cutting a total of 100 basis points. Additional cuts through 2025 brought the target range down to 3.50% to 3.75% as of early 2026, with an effective rate of 3.64%.

The rate environment has two stories to tell:

  • Short-term rates are down significantly. Floating-rate borrowers and those with SOFR-based loans are seeing meaningful payment relief compared to 2023 and 2024.
  • Long-term rates remain elevated. The 10-year Treasury yield sits around 4.28%, reflecting persistent inflation concerns and fiscal policy uncertainty. This is the benchmark that drives most fixed-rate commercial mortgage pricing.

For borrowers, the practical impact is nuanced. Bridge loans and floating-rate products are cheaper. But permanent, fixed-rate financing (bank loans, CMBS, life company) still carries rates in the 6% to 8% range for most borrowers, depending on property type, leverage, and credit quality.

Most market forecasters expect modest additional Fed easing in 2026, but a rapid decline in long-term rates is not the consensus view. Borrowers should plan around current rate levels rather than betting on dramatic improvement.

The $875 Billion Maturity Wall

This is the defining issue for commercial real estate in 2026. Over $875 billion in commercial mortgages are maturing, part of a broader $1.7 trillion wall of CRE debt that has been building through extensions and "extend-and-pretend" strategies since 2023.

The numbers tell a stark story:

  • More than 50% of CRE firms face loan maturity within the coming year
  • Only 21% expect to pay off upcoming maturities in full
  • Many loans were originated at much lower rates (3% to 5%) and higher valuations, creating a significant gap between original terms and today's market

The challenge is straightforward: a property that was financed at a 3.5% rate with a 1.25x DSCR may not support a refinance at 7% without additional equity, a lower loan amount, or both. Property values in many sectors have compressed 15% to 30% from 2022 peaks, compounding the problem.

What This Means for Borrowers

If your loan matures in 2026, start the refinancing process immediately if you have not already. Options include:

  • Conventional refinance: If the property's income supports today's rates and the LTV works, this is the cleanest path forward.
  • Bridge loan: A short-term bridge can buy time while you stabilize income, complete renovations, or wait for better permanent financing terms.
  • Loan modification or extension: Some existing lenders prefer extending the current loan (with modified terms) over taking back the property.
  • Capital infusion: Bringing in additional equity, mezzanine debt, or preferred equity to right-size the loan balance.
  • Sale: If the property does not pencil under any financing scenario, an orderly sale may be the best option.

Property Sector Breakdown

Performance varies dramatically by property type. Here is where each major sector stands heading into the rest of 2026.

Industrial and Logistics

Industrial remains one of the strongest CRE sectors, though the record-breaking growth of 2021 to 2023 has moderated. Vacancy rates are steady at 7.1% (stable for three consecutive quarters). Net absorption reached 176.8 million square feet in 2025, up 16.3% year over year. Rent growth has slowed to 1.5% annually, and new completions are at their lowest level since 2017.

The supply pipeline is thinning, which should support rent growth going forward. E-commerce, nearshoring, and supply chain reconfiguration continue to drive demand. Tariff uncertainty is creating some near-term hesitation among tenants, but the long-term demand picture remains strong.

Multifamily

Multifamily is in a transitional phase. Vacancy rates hit a record 9.3% as a massive supply wave (the largest in decades) worked through the market. Rent growth slowed to 1.1% year over year, the weakest since the pandemic. However, absorption has been robust: approximately 355,000 units were absorbed in 2025, making it the third-strongest year in the past 25 years.

The key variable going forward is supply. New deliveries are dropping 26% year over year, and construction starts have slowed sharply. As the current supply wave is absorbed and new construction slows, vacancy should tighten and rent growth should reaccelerate. Markets with limited new supply and strong job growth (Midwest, Northeast, smaller Sun Belt cities) are best positioned.

Retail

Retail has been one of the surprise performers. After years of "retail apocalypse" narratives, the sector has benefited from extremely limited new construction and strong tenant demand for well-located space. Fundamentals improved through 2025 on robust backfilling of vacant space.

Grocery-anchored centers, open-air lifestyle centers, and single-tenant net lease properties continue to attract strong investor interest. The risk factors to watch include potential tariff impacts on consumer spending and the 79% of tenants reporting that rent burden is forcing them to cut essential spending.

Office

Office is the most challenged sector, but the story is becoming more nuanced. February 2026 saw the highest office foot traffic since 2020, suggesting that the return-to-office trend is gaining momentum. New construction is at record lows, which will eventually reduce supply pressure. Sublease availability has begun to improve.

The market is sharply bifurcated:

  • Class A and trophy buildings in top markets are performing reasonably well, with tenants upgrading to higher-quality space (the "flight to quality" trend).
  • Class B and C buildings face severe occupancy challenges. Many older buildings will need to be repositioned, converted to other uses, or demolished.

Lenders remain cautious about office lending. Borrowers with well-located, high-quality office assets can still find financing, but expect lower leverage, higher rates, and more conservative underwriting.

Hospitality

Hotel performance has recovered to near pre-pandemic levels in most markets, though the recovery is uneven by segment. Leisure travel remains strong, while business and convention travel continues to rebuild. Revenue per available room (RevPAR) growth has moderated after strong gains in 2023 and 2024.

Select-service and limited-service hotels in leisure markets are performing well. Full-service hotels in urban markets dependent on business travel are still working toward full recovery. New construction is limited, which supports fundamentals for existing properties.

Data Centers and Digital Infrastructure

Data centers are the top-ranked CRE sector for 2026. Driven by the explosion in artificial intelligence workloads, cloud computing, and digital infrastructure demand, the sector has unprecedented fundamentals: 100% of new pipeline is pre-committed in the nine major data center markets.

This is a specialized sector with high barriers to entry (power availability, permitting, capital requirements), but for investors and developers with the capability, it offers the strongest risk-adjusted returns in commercial real estate today.

Capital Markets and Lending Trends

The CRE lending landscape has shifted dramatically since the rate tightening cycle began. Several key trends define the capital markets environment in 2026:

Private Credit is Filling the Gap

Alternative debt sources now account for 24% of U.S. CRE lending volume, up from a 10-year average of 14%. Private credit funds, debt funds, and non-bank lenders are stepping in where traditional banks have pulled back. The global private credit market reached $238 billion in 2024 and is projected to grow to $400 billion in assets under management by the end of the decade.

For borrowers, this means more options but at higher cost. Private credit typically charges 200 to 400 basis points above comparable bank rates. The trade-off is speed, flexibility, and willingness to finance deals that banks will not touch.

Bank Lending is Recovering

Total CRE loans at commercial banks have reached $3.075 trillion as of early 2026. Banks remain the largest source of CRE financing, but they are lending more selectively, with tighter underwriting standards and a preference for lower-leverage, higher-quality deals.

Regional banks, which historically accounted for a large share of CRE lending, are under particular pressure from regulators to reduce CRE concentration. This has created a gap that larger banks, life insurance companies, and private credit are working to fill.

CMBS Market is Recovering

The CMBS market is slowly rebuilding after near-shutdown in 2023. New issuance has increased, though volume remains below pre-2022 levels. Spreads have tightened from their peaks, making CMBS more competitive for the right deals (stabilized, lower-leverage properties with strong tenancy).

Loan Origination Volume

New loan origination is up more than 90% from the prior year period, signaling that the market is thawing. However, this recovery is from historically low levels. Full normalization of transaction and lending volume will take several more quarters.

Where the Opportunities Are

Despite the challenges, 2026 presents real opportunities for well-positioned borrowers and investors:

  • Value resets: Property values have declined 15% to 30% from 2022 peaks in many markets, creating attractive entry points for buyers who can finance at today's rates.
  • Maturity wall distress: Borrowers who cannot refinance will be forced to sell or recapitalize, creating opportunities for those with available capital.
  • Multifamily in supply-constrained markets: As the current supply wave is absorbed and new construction slows, fundamentals should improve. Markets with limited new supply and strong employment growth offer the best opportunity.
  • Industrial and logistics: Despite moderating rent growth, the declining supply pipeline and structural demand drivers (e-commerce, nearshoring) support long-term value.
  • Retail repositioning: Well-located retail properties with strong tenancy and limited competition from new supply continue to outperform.
  • Distressed office conversions: While risky, converting underperforming office buildings to residential, life sciences, or mixed-use can unlock significant value in the right markets with the right basis.

Regional Market Highlights

CRE performance varies significantly by geography. Key regional themes for 2026:

  • Sun Belt markets: Population and job growth continue to drive demand, but some markets (Austin, Phoenix, Nashville) are working through a supply glut, particularly in multifamily. Markets that have absorbed the supply wave will be first to see rent growth accelerate.
  • Midwest and Northeast: These regions have seen less new construction and are benefiting from relative supply discipline. Multifamily fundamentals in the Midwest are among the strongest nationally.
  • Gateway cities (NYC, LA, Chicago, SF): Mixed performance. Strong demand for top-tier assets, but office challenges weigh on overall sentiment. San Francisco continues to face headwinds from tech sector right-sizing and remote work.
  • Secondary markets: Growing investor interest in secondary markets (Boise, Raleigh, Salt Lake City, Columbus) as capital seeks better risk-adjusted returns outside of primary gateway cities.

Impact of AI and Technology on CRE

Artificial intelligence is reshaping commercial real estate in several ways:

  • Data center demand: AI workloads are the single largest driver of data center demand. Power availability has become the primary constraint on new data center development in many markets.
  • Underwriting and lending: Lenders are increasingly using AI-powered tools for property valuation, risk assessment, and document processing, which can speed up the loan process.
  • Property operations: Smart building technology and AI-driven property management tools are helping owners reduce operating costs and improve tenant satisfaction, which directly impacts NOI and DSCR.
  • Office demand implications: AI-driven productivity gains may ultimately reduce the amount of office space companies need per employee, reinforcing the structural challenge for that sector.
  • Investment analysis: AI tools are enabling faster and more granular market analysis, helping investors identify opportunities and assess risk across larger portfolios.

Strategies for Borrowers in the Current Market

Whether you are refinancing, acquiring, or holding, here are practical strategies for navigating the 2026 market:

  1. Start early on maturities. If your loan matures in 2026 or 2027, begin the refinancing process now. Six to twelve months of lead time gives you the widest range of options.
  2. Right-size your expectations. Cap rates, interest rates, and property values have all shifted. Underwrite to today's market, not 2021 assumptions.
  3. Explore all capital sources. Banks, CMBS, life companies, debt funds, SBA, and private credit all have different appetites and pricing. The best deal may not come from your traditional lender.
  4. Focus on NOI improvement. In a higher-rate environment, the most reliable way to maintain or improve your financing position is to grow net operating income through higher rents, lower expenses, or better occupancy.
  5. Consider bridge financing strategically. If your property is in transition (lease-up, renovation, repositioning), a bridge loan may be more appropriate than trying to force permanent financing on an unstabilized asset.
  6. Lock in rates when terms are acceptable. Waiting for significantly lower long-term rates is a bet that may not pay off. If the available terms support your investment thesis, execute.
  7. Use a loan advisor. The market is complex and evolving quickly. A knowledgeable loan advisor or broker can identify lenders and programs you may not know about and negotiate better terms.

What to Watch in the Second Half of 2026

Key indicators that will shape the CRE market through the rest of the year:

  • Fed policy decisions: Additional rate cuts would provide relief for floating-rate borrowers and could eventually pull long-term rates lower.
  • Maturity wall resolution: How the market digests the wave of maturing debt (through refinancing, sales, modifications, or defaults) will determine whether the recovery accelerates or stalls.
  • Office return trends: Continued improvement in office utilization would be a positive signal for the most challenged sector.
  • Multifamily supply absorption: If the market absorbs the current supply wave as expected, rent growth should reaccelerate in the second half.
  • Trade and tariff policy: Tariff uncertainty is affecting supply chain decisions in industrial and consumer spending patterns in retail. Clarity on trade policy would reduce uncertainty.
  • Private credit deployment: With $585 billion in dry powder, the pace at which private capital is deployed will influence transaction volume and pricing.
