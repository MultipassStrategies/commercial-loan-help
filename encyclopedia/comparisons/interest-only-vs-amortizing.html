Interest-Only vs Fully Amortizing Commercial Loans: Payment Structure Comparison

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Monthly payment difference: IO payments are 35-50% lower than fully amortizing
  • Total interest cost: IO loans cost more in total interest over the same period
  • Cash flow impact: IO preserves maximum cash flow for operations or improvements
  • Balloon risk: IO loans have larger balloon payments at maturity
  • Equity building: Amortizing builds equity with every payment; IO builds none
  • Typical use cases: IO: bridge, value-add, construction; Amortizing: stabilized long-term holds

How Each Payment Structure Works

The most fundamental decision in structuring a commercial loan, beyond rate and term, is how each monthly payment is divided between interest and principal. This choice determines your cash flow, your equity position, and the balance you owe at maturity. The two primary structures are interest-only (IO) and fully amortizing, and they work in fundamentally different ways.

With an interest-only loan, your monthly payment covers only the interest accrued on the outstanding principal balance. You are not paying down any of the loan itself. The formula is straightforward: multiply the loan balance by the annual interest rate, then divide by 12. On a $5,000,000 loan at 7.00%, the monthly IO payment is $5,000,000 x 0.07 / 12, which equals approximately $29,167. That payment stays the same every month (assuming a fixed rate) because the principal balance never changes.

With a fully amortizing loan, each monthly payment includes both interest on the remaining balance and a portion that reduces the principal. The payment amount is calculated using a standard amortization formula that factors in the loan amount, interest rate, and amortization term (commonly 25 or 30 years for commercial loans). On the same $5,000,000 loan at 7.00% with a 30-year amortization, the monthly payment is approximately $33,265. In the early months, most of that payment goes toward interest, with only a small portion reducing principal. Over time, as the balance decreases, the interest portion shrinks and the principal portion grows.

The difference between these two structures has a direct and measurable impact on your monthly cash flow. In this example, the IO payment is about $4,098 per month less than the amortizing payment, a roughly 12% reduction. For borrowers executing a business plan that demands maximum available cash (renovations, lease-up, or capital reserves), that difference can be significant. For borrowers focused on long-term wealth building and reducing debt, the amortizing structure accomplishes that goal automatically with every payment.

Interest-Only Period Mechanics

Interest-only periods on commercial loans vary widely depending on the loan product and the lender's program. Understanding how these periods work, and what happens when they end, is essential for planning your cash flow and exit strategy.

For bridge loans, the entire loan term is typically interest-only. A standard bridge loan runs 12 to 36 months with optional extensions, and the borrower makes IO payments for the full duration. There is no conversion to amortizing payments because the loan is designed to be repaid in full at maturity through a sale, refinance into permanent debt, or recapitalization. The borrower's exit is the payoff event, not a gradual paydown.

For permanent loans, IO is usually offered as an initial period before the loan converts to an amortizing schedule. CMBS loans commonly offer 2 to 5 years of IO at the beginning of a 10-year term. A borrower on a 10-year CMBS loan with 3 years of IO would make interest-only payments for months 1 through 36, then begin making amortizing payments (based on a 30-year schedule, for example) for months 37 through 120. The balloon payment at maturity in year 10 would reflect only 7 years of amortization rather than 10, meaning the remaining balance is higher than it would be on a loan that amortized from day one.

Agency multifamily loans (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac) offer some of the most generous IO terms in the market. Depending on the program, borrower qualifications, and property profile, agency loans may offer 1 to 10 years of interest-only payments. Full-term IO (where the entire loan term is interest-only) is available on select agency programs for strong borrowers and well-located multifamily assets.

When the IO period ends on a permanent loan, the transition to amortizing payments represents a meaningful increase in monthly debt service. Using the $5 million example at 7%, the jump from $29,167 per month (IO) to $33,265 per month (amortizing on a 30-year schedule) is an increase of roughly $4,098, or about 14%. Borrowers must plan for this step-up in their cash flow projections. If the property's net operating income has not increased during the IO period (through rent growth, improved occupancy, or reduced expenses), the higher payment may strain debt service coverage.

Fully Amortizing Loan Mechanics

A fully amortizing loan is structured so that the borrower's regular payments, made over the full amortization term, will completely pay off the loan balance by the final payment date. Each payment is identical in amount but contains a shifting mix of interest and principal. In the early years, interest dominates. In the later years, principal dominates. This is sometimes called "front-loaded interest" and it has important implications for borrowers.

Consider a $5,000,000 loan at 7.00% with a 30-year amortization. The monthly payment is approximately $33,265. In the very first month, $29,167 of that payment goes to interest (the same amount as a full IO payment) and only $4,098 goes to principal. By month 60 (year 5), the interest portion has dropped to approximately $27,422 and the principal portion has risen to approximately $5,843. By month 120 (year 10), the split is roughly $25,045 in interest and $8,220 in principal. The acceleration of principal paydown becomes increasingly powerful over time.

It is important to distinguish between the amortization term and the loan term. Most commercial loans do not actually run for the full 25- or 30-year amortization period. A typical structure might be a 10-year term with a 30-year amortization schedule. The borrower makes payments based on the 30-year schedule, but the remaining balance comes due as a balloon payment at the end of year 10. After 10 years of amortizing payments on a $5 million loan at 7%, the remaining balance would be approximately $4,430,000, meaning the borrower has paid down roughly $570,000 in principal. That $570,000 represents real equity built through debt service alone, independent of any property appreciation.

Some loan products, particularly SBA 504 loans and certain bank portfolio loans, offer full amortization with no balloon payment. On an SBA 504 loan with a 25-year fully amortizing term, the borrower simply makes payments until the loan is paid off. There is no refinance risk at maturity because there is no maturity event short of the final payment. This structure is attractive for owner-occupied properties where the borrower intends to hold the asset indefinitely and wants the certainty of a defined payoff date.

Partial Interest-Only (Hybrid Structures)

Many commercial loans blend interest-only and amortizing periods into a single loan structure. These hybrid arrangements are common in CMBS and agency lending, and they represent a middle ground between the cash flow benefits of IO and the equity-building discipline of amortization.

The most typical hybrid structure is an initial IO period followed by amortizing payments for the remainder of the term. A CMBS loan might be structured as "3+7," meaning 3 years of IO followed by 7 years of amortization within a 10-year term. An agency multifamily loan might offer "5+5" or "2+8" structures depending on the program and borrower profile.

Lenders offer partial IO for several reasons. First, it helps borrowers who are acquiring stabilized properties with a business plan that includes near-term capital expenditures. The IO period provides breathing room to execute improvements, absorb lease-up costs, or manage the transition from prior ownership. Second, partial IO allows borrowers to qualify at a lower initial payment, which can be the difference between meeting DSCR thresholds and falling short. Third, lenders recognize that many commercial properties experience the most cash flow variability in the first few years of a new loan (due to lease rollovers, deferred maintenance, or management transitions), and IO provides a cushion during that adjustment period.

The trade-off is clear: partial IO results in higher total interest paid over the loan term and a larger remaining balance at maturity compared to a loan that amortizes from day one. On a $5 million, 10-year CMBS loan at 7% with a 30-year amortization schedule, a loan with 3 years of IO would have a remaining balance at maturity of approximately $4,623,000, compared to approximately $4,430,000 for the same loan with no IO period. That roughly $193,000 difference represents principal that was not paid down during the 3-year IO window.

Monthly Payment Comparison Example

To make the difference between interest-only and fully amortizing payments concrete, let us walk through a detailed example using a $5,000,000 commercial loan at a 7.00% fixed interest rate with a 30-year amortization schedule (for the amortizing version).

Interest-Only Payment

The IO payment is constant every month: $5,000,000 x 7.00% / 12 = $29,167. No portion goes to principal. After 12 months of IO payments, the borrower has paid $350,000 in interest and the loan balance remains $5,000,000.

Cash Flow Difference

The IO borrower paid $29,167 per month. The amortizing borrower paid $33,265 per month. The difference of $4,098 per month, or $49,176 per year, represents additional cash that the IO borrower had available for property improvements, reserves, distributions to investors, or deployment into other opportunities. Over a 5-year IO period, that cumulative difference totals approximately $245,880 in additional available cash.

Total Cost of Capital Over Loan Life

When comparing interest-only and fully amortizing structures, it is not enough to look only at the monthly payment. The total cost of capital over the loan life tells the full story, and it includes both the interest paid and the principal position at the end of the holding period.

The IO borrower paid $1,750,000 in total interest over five years. The amortizing borrower paid approximately $1,645,900 in interest, which is about $104,100 less. However, the amortizing borrower also made $245,900 more in total payments. The difference went toward principal reduction of roughly $252,000.

From a net cost perspective, the amortizing borrower is ahead by approximately $252,000 in equity (the principal paid down), but behind by approximately $245,900 in total cash spent. The "true cost" difference depends entirely on what the IO borrower does with the cash savings. If the IO borrower reinvests that $245,900 into property improvements that increase value by $500,000 or more, the IO structure clearly wins. If the IO borrower simply keeps the cash idle, the amortizing structure produces a better financial outcome because the equity built exceeds the incremental cash outlay.

Cash Flow Advantages of Interest-Only

The primary reason commercial real estate sponsors choose interest-only structures is cash flow preservation. For many business plans, the ability to retain more cash during the early years of ownership is not merely convenient; it is operationally essential.

Value-add execution. A sponsor acquiring a property with below-market rents, deferred maintenance, or operational inefficiencies needs capital to fund improvements. If the acquisition loan requires amortizing payments, more cash goes to the lender each month, leaving less for renovations. An IO structure frees up that capital differential for direct investment in the property. On a $5 million loan, the roughly $4,100 per month saved by choosing IO over amortizing payments translates to nearly $50,000 per year that can be directed toward unit upgrades, common area improvements, or enhanced management.

DSCR optimization. Debt service coverage ratio is calculated by dividing net operating income (NOI) by annual debt service. Because IO payments are lower, the DSCR on an IO loan is higher than it would be on the same property with an amortizing payment. This is particularly useful during the stabilization phase of a value-add project, when NOI may be temporarily depressed due to vacancy from renovations or below-market legacy leases. A higher DSCR provides more cushion before triggering loan covenant violations or cash management provisions.

Capital recycling. Sophisticated sponsors use IO structures to preserve capital for deployment into additional acquisitions or investments. Rather than locking capital into principal paydown on a single property, the IO borrower retains that capital and puts it to work elsewhere. If the sponsor can generate returns above the loan's interest rate on that recycled capital (through additional acquisitions, value-add plays, or other investments), the IO structure creates a positive arbitrage.

Investor distributions. For syndicated deals with multiple limited partners, IO structures can support more consistent distributions during the hold period. Investors receive cash flow from operations rather than seeing it consumed by principal payments. This can make the investment more attractive to yield-oriented limited partners and improve the sponsor's ability to raise capital for future deals.

Equity Building in Amortizing Loans

While interest-only structures offer clear short-term cash flow benefits, fully amortizing loans deliver a different kind of value: forced equity accumulation. Every amortizing payment reduces the loan balance, building the borrower's ownership stake in the property regardless of what happens to market values.

This "forced savings" mechanism is especially powerful over long holding periods. On a $5 million loan at 7% with a 30-year amortization, the borrower builds approximately $252,000 in equity over the first 5 years through principal paydown alone. Over 10 years, that figure grows to roughly $570,000. Over 15 years, it exceeds $1,000,000. The acceleration effect means each year produces more equity than the last, because the principal portion of each payment grows as the interest portion shrinks.

From a refinancing perspective, amortization steadily improves the borrower's loan-to-value ratio. A property purchased at 75% LTV with an amortizing loan will have a significantly lower LTV at maturity than the same property with an IO loan (assuming no change in property value). Lower LTV at refinance means more financing options, better pricing, and reduced refinance risk. If property values have declined, the amortizing borrower has a built-in cushion that the IO borrower lacks.

For wealth-building oriented borrowers, particularly those acquiring owner-occupied commercial properties or long-term hold investments, amortization is a disciplined path to debt freedom. Each payment brings the borrower closer to outright ownership of the asset. Once the loan is fully paid off, the entire NOI becomes free cash flow with no debt service obligation. This end state is particularly attractive for business owners using SBA loans to acquire their operating premises, where the goal is to eliminate occupancy costs over time.

The counterargument from IO proponents is that forced equity through amortization earns a return equal to the loan's interest rate, which may be lower than returns available from other uses of that capital. This is a valid point for active investors with reinvestment opportunities, but it carries execution risk. The equity built through amortization is guaranteed; the returns from reinvesting cash flow savings are not.

Risk Considerations for Each Structure

Both interest-only and fully amortizing structures carry distinct risk profiles that borrowers must evaluate in the context of their overall financial position and business plan.

Interest-Only Risks

Larger balloon payment. Because no principal is paid during the IO period, the full loan balance comes due at maturity. The borrower must either sell the property, refinance, or pay off the balance in full. If market conditions have deteriorated (rising interest rates, declining property values, tighter lending standards), refinancing may be difficult or impossible on favorable terms. This is the single largest risk of IO lending and the primary reason lenders view IO loans as carrying higher credit risk.

No equity cushion. If property values decline during the hold period, the IO borrower has no principal paydown to offset the loss. A property purchased at 75% LTV with an IO loan is still at 75% LTV (or worse, if values have dropped) at maturity. In a severe downturn, the borrower may owe more than the property is worth, creating a situation where the only options are to bring additional equity, negotiate with the lender, or face foreclosure.

Refinance rate risk. IO borrowers face the full impact of interest rate changes at refinance because they must refinance the entire original balance. If rates have risen 200 basis points since origination, the new payment on the full balance will be substantially higher. An amortizing borrower refinancing a reduced balance absorbs less rate shock.

Fully Amortizing Risks

Higher payments reduce cash reserves. The additional monthly payment required for amortization leaves less cash available for unexpected expenses, capital improvements, or economic downturns. If a property experiences a spike in vacancy or an unplanned capital expense, the amortizing borrower has less financial flexibility to absorb the shock.

Reduced operational flexibility. Capital that is "locked up" in principal payments cannot be redirected to higher-return opportunities. For active investors managing multiple properties or pursuing growth strategies, the opportunity cost of mandatory principal payments can be significant.

Lower DSCR reduces covenant cushion. Because amortizing payments are higher, the property's DSCR is lower compared to the same property with IO payments. In a declining revenue environment, the amortizing borrower hits DSCR covenant thresholds sooner, potentially triggering cash sweeps, lockbox provisions, or default notices.

Which Loan Types Offer Interest-Only

Not all commercial loan products offer interest-only payment options. Understanding which programs provide IO, and under what conditions, helps borrowers target the right lenders and set realistic expectations during the financing process.

Bridge loans. Interest-only is standard on virtually all bridge loan products. Bridge lenders (debt funds, private lenders, and some banks) structure their loans as 100% IO because the short-term nature of bridge financing (1 to 3 years) makes amortization impractical. The borrower's business plan, not principal paydown, is the exit strategy.

CMBS loans. Partial IO is common in CMBS lending. Many CMBS programs offer 2 to 5 years of IO at the beginning of a 10-year term, and some offer full-term IO for strong borrowers and high-quality assets. IO availability in CMBS depends on leverage, property type, DSCR, and market conditions at the time of securitization. During periods of aggressive lending, full-term IO becomes more widely available; during tighter credit environments, IO periods shrink or disappear.

Agency multifamily (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac). Agency programs offer partial IO on many products, with IO periods ranging from 1 to 10 years depending on the specific program, borrower qualifications, and property location. Fannie Mae's standard DUS program and Freddie Mac's Optigo platform both include IO options, though the terms vary by deal. Full-term IO is available on select agency executions for top-tier borrowers and assets.

Bank portfolio loans. Some banks offer 1 to 2 years of IO as a negotiated feature on permanent commercial mortgages, particularly for established borrower relationships or properties undergoing a transition. IO availability from banks is highly relationship-dependent and varies significantly from institution to institution.

SBA loans. The Small Business Administration's 7(a) and 504 loan programs generally do not offer interest-only periods. These programs are designed for owner-occupied businesses and require fully amortizing payments. In rare circumstances, SBA lenders may offer a brief IO period during a construction or renovation phase, but this is the exception rather than the norm.

Lender Requirements for Interest-Only Approval

Because interest-only loans carry more risk for lenders (no principal reduction means no growing equity cushion), the qualification requirements for IO are generally stricter than for amortizing loans. Here is what most lenders require when a borrower requests an IO structure.

Higher DSCR requirements. Lenders typically require a DSCR of 1.30x to 1.50x for IO loans, compared to 1.20x to 1.25x for amortizing loans. The higher threshold provides a larger income cushion to offset the risk of no principal paydown. Some lenders apply a "stressed" DSCR test, calculating what the DSCR would be under amortizing payments even when granting IO, to ensure the property could service a fully amortizing loan if needed.

Lower loan-to-value ratios. IO loans are typically capped at 65% to 70% LTV, compared to 75% to 80% for amortizing loans. The lower leverage compensates for the fact that the loan balance will not decline over time. Lenders want more equity in the deal from the outset to protect against potential value declines.

Borrower experience. Lenders are more willing to offer IO to experienced commercial real estate operators who have a demonstrated track record of executing similar business plans. A first-time borrower requesting full-term IO on a value-add deal will face greater scrutiny than a sponsor with 20 completed projects.

Property type restrictions. Some lenders limit IO to specific property types that they view as lower risk. Multifamily properties, which benefit from broad and consistent rental demand, are the most common candidates for IO approval. Office, retail, and specialty property types may face additional hurdles or outright restrictions on IO availability.

Reserve requirements. Lenders granting IO often require larger reserves than they would for amortizing loans. This may include larger escrow accounts for taxes and insurance, capital expenditure reserves, and debt service reserves (often 6 to 12 months of payments). These reserves protect the lender in the event that the property's cash flow deteriorates during the IO period.
