Defeasance vs Yield Maintenance: Comparing Commercial Loan Prepayment Options

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Process difference: Defeasance replaces collateral with Treasuries; Yield maintenance pays a calculated penalty
  • Cost comparison: Defeasance is cheaper when rates rise; Yield maintenance is cheaper when rates are flat or falling
  • Loan types: Defeasance: CMBS; Yield maintenance: agency, life company, some bank loans
  • Timeline: Defeasance: 30-45 days; Yield maintenance: 5-10 days
  • Rate environment impact: Rising rates lower defeasance cost; Falling rates raise yield maintenance cost
  • Professional requirements: Defeasance requires a consultant/intermediary; Yield maintenance is a formula calculation

Overview of Both Prepayment Mechanisms

Commercial real estate loans are fundamentally different from residential mortgages when it comes to prepayment. On a home loan, you can typically pay off your mortgage early with little or no penalty. Commercial loans do not work that way. Lenders who originate fixed-rate commercial mortgages have priced the loan to earn a specific return over the full term, and they need protection against borrowers paying off early and disrupting that expected income stream. This protection takes the form of prepayment provisions written into every loan agreement at origination.

The two most common prepayment mechanisms in commercial lending are defeasance and yield maintenance. Both serve the same underlying purpose: they compensate the lender (or, in the case of securitized loans, the bondholders) for the interest income they would lose if the loan is paid off before maturity. However, they accomplish this goal through entirely different methods, with different cost structures, timelines, and levels of complexity.

Defeasance works by substitution. Rather than paying off the loan, the borrower purchases a portfolio of U.S. Treasury securities that generates cash flows matching the remaining scheduled debt service payments on the loan. These Treasuries are assigned to a successor borrower entity, the original property is released from the lien, and the loan continues to exist on paper, serviced by the Treasury cash flows until maturity. The lender's income stream is preserved, and the borrower walks away with the property free and clear.

Yield maintenance works by calculation. The borrower pays a lump-sum penalty equal to the present value of the interest income the lender will forgo for the remaining loan term. This is determined by comparing the loan's note rate to the current Treasury yield of matching maturity and calculating the difference. The borrower wires the payoff amount plus the yield maintenance penalty, the loan is retired, and the lien is released.

Understanding which method applies to your loan, and what it will cost in the current rate environment, is essential for any borrower considering a sale, refinance, or recapitalization before the loan's maturity date.

How Defeasance Works

The word "defeasance" comes from the Old French word meaning to undo or defeat. In the context of commercial lending, defeasance defeats (or nullifies) the borrower's obligation under the original loan by replacing the real estate collateral with a portfolio of U.S. Treasury securities. The loan itself continues to exist, but the property is released. Here is how the process works step by step.

Step 1: Engage a defeasance consultant. Because defeasance involves purchasing specific Treasury securities, coordinating with multiple parties, and navigating securitization trust requirements, borrowers must work with a specialized defeasance intermediary. The consultant manages the entire transaction from start to finish. Selecting an experienced consultant is critical, as errors in the Treasury portfolio construction or legal documentation can delay or derail the process.

Step 2: Notify the servicer. The borrower (through the consultant) sends a formal defeasance request to the loan servicer. The servicer reviews the loan documents to confirm that defeasance is permitted and identifies any lockout periods or other restrictions. Most CMBS loans have an initial lockout period (typically two years) during which neither defeasance nor any other prepayment is allowed.

Step 3: Purchase the Treasury portfolio. The consultant works with a securities broker to assemble a portfolio of U.S. Treasury securities (bonds, notes, or STRIPS) whose coupon and principal payments exactly match the remaining scheduled debt service payments on the loan through maturity. This is the core of the defeasance transaction, and the cost of this portfolio is the primary variable in the total defeasance expense. When current Treasury yields are higher than the loan's note rate, these securities can be purchased at a discount to par, meaning the portfolio costs less than the outstanding loan balance. When Treasury yields are lower than the note rate, the portfolio costs more.

Step 4: Create the successor borrower entity. A new single-purpose entity (the successor borrower) is created to assume the defeased loan obligation. This entity holds the Treasury portfolio and has no other assets or liabilities. The successor borrower's sole function is to hold the Treasuries and allow the scheduled payments to flow through to the trust until the loan matures.

Step 5: Obtain legal opinions and rating agency consent. For CMBS loans, the transaction requires legal opinions confirming that the defeasance complies with the loan documents and the securitization trust's pooling and servicing agreement. Rating agencies (such as Moody's, Fitch, or KBRA) must acknowledge the defeasance to ensure it does not adversely affect the credit ratings of the CMBS bonds. This step often takes the most time and is the primary driver of the 30- to 45-day timeline.

Step 6: Close and release the property. On the closing date, the Treasury portfolio is delivered to the successor borrower entity, the original borrower is released from the loan obligation, and the lien on the property is discharged. The borrower now owns the property free and clear, and the defeased loan continues to be serviced by the Treasury cash flows until it reaches maturity.

Total professional fees for a defeasance transaction typically range from $30,000 to $75,000, including the consultant's fee ($10,000 to $25,000), legal fees ($10,000 to $30,000), accounting fees, and rating agency fees. These costs are incurred regardless of the interest rate environment, making them a significant fixed expense that must be weighed against the potential savings from favorable Treasury pricing.

How Yield Maintenance Works

Yield maintenance is a prepayment penalty designed to make the lender "whole" by compensating for the interest income lost when a borrower pays off the loan early. The name reflects the concept directly: the lender's yield is maintained as if the borrower had continued making payments through maturity.

The calculation follows a straightforward present-value formula. The servicer takes the remaining scheduled interest payments on the loan, discounts them back to the present using the current Treasury yield of matching maturity, and the resulting present value (minus the outstanding loan balance) is the yield maintenance penalty. In simplified terms:

Yield Maintenance Penalty = Present Value of Remaining Payments - Outstanding Loan Balance

Or, expressed another way:

Penalty = Outstanding Balance x (Note Rate - Treasury Rate) x Remaining Term Factor

The key variables in this calculation are the note rate on the loan, the current Treasury yield for a maturity matching the remaining loan term, and the number of months remaining until maturity. When the note rate is significantly higher than the current Treasury rate, the penalty is large because the lender is losing a valuable above-market income stream. When the note rate is close to or below the current Treasury rate, the penalty shrinks because the lender can reinvest the returned principal at a comparable or better rate.

Most yield maintenance provisions include a minimum penalty floor, often set at 1% of the outstanding loan balance. This floor ensures the lender receives some compensation even when current rates exceed the note rate and the formula would otherwise produce a zero or negative result. Some loan documents set the floor at the greater of 1% or the calculated amount.

The process for executing a yield maintenance prepayment is simple compared to defeasance. The borrower notifies the servicer of the intent to prepay, the servicer runs the yield maintenance calculation per the formula in the loan documents, the borrower reviews and approves the payoff statement, and the borrower wires the outstanding balance plus the yield maintenance penalty. The lien is released, and the loan is fully retired. The entire process typically takes 5 to 10 business days, though some servicers can process it faster with advance notice.

Professional fees for yield maintenance are minimal. The servicer may charge a calculation or processing fee of $0 to $5,000. No outside consultants, securities brokers, or rating agency approvals are needed.

Cost Comparison in Different Interest Rate Environments

The interest rate environment is the single most important factor in determining the cost of both defeasance and yield maintenance. Understanding how each method responds to rate changes is essential for estimating your prepayment expense and choosing the right timing for a sale or refinance.

When rates have risen above the note rate. This is the most favorable environment for prepayment under either method. For defeasance, rising rates mean Treasury securities trade at a discount. The portfolio needed to replicate your remaining loan payments costs less than the outstanding loan balance, so the net cost of the Treasury purchase can be very low, or even produce a savings. After accounting for the fixed professional fees of $30,000 to $75,000, the total defeasance cost in a sharply rising rate environment can be remarkably modest. For yield maintenance, rising rates also reduce the penalty. When the current Treasury rate exceeds your note rate, the formula produces a zero or negative result, and the penalty falls to the contractual floor (usually 1% of the outstanding balance). On a $10 million loan, that floor is $100,000.

When rates have fallen below the note rate. This is the most expensive environment for prepayment. For defeasance, falling Treasury yields mean the securities needed to match your loan's higher coupon payments are more expensive. The Treasury portfolio may cost significantly more than the outstanding loan balance, and the professional fees add to the total. For yield maintenance, the penalty is large because the spread between your note rate and the current Treasury rate is wide. The lender is losing a valuable above-market income stream, and the present-value calculation reflects that loss dollar for dollar.

When rates are roughly flat. In a stable rate environment, both methods produce moderate costs. The yield maintenance penalty is small (the rate spread is minimal), and the 1% floor may or may not apply. The defeasance Treasury portfolio costs approximately par value, so the net securities cost is near zero, but the $30,000 to $75,000 in professional fees still apply. In this scenario, yield maintenance is almost always the cheaper option because it avoids those fixed costs.

The critical difference: the floor. The key distinction between the two methods in a rising rate environment is the floor. Yield maintenance almost always has a minimum penalty (typically 1%), which creates a cost floor regardless of how much rates have risen. Defeasance has no such floor. When Treasuries are trading at a deep discount, the net securities cost can be very small, and the total defeasance expense is driven almost entirely by the fixed professional fees. For large loans in a sharply rising rate environment, this difference can save hundreds of thousands of dollars compared to the yield maintenance floor.

When Defeasance Is Cheaper vs Yield Maintenance

Defeasance tends to be the less expensive option under a specific set of conditions. When Treasury yields have risen substantially above the loan's note rate (a spread of 150 basis points or more), the cost of the required Treasury portfolio drops well below the outstanding loan balance. On a $20 million loan, the Treasury savings might be $300,000 to $500,000 or more, which easily offsets the $30,000 to $75,000 in professional fees. Meanwhile, yield maintenance on that same loan would be capped at the 1% floor, costing $200,000. In this scenario, defeasance could save $125,000 or more.

The advantage of defeasance over yield maintenance grows with loan size. On a $50 million CMBS loan with rates 200 basis points above the note rate, the Treasury discount could produce $800,000 or more in savings against the outstanding balance. The $75,000 in professional fees is a rounding error at that scale. The yield maintenance floor on the same loan would be $500,000 (1% of $50 million). Defeasance wins by a wide margin.

Yield maintenance is the cheaper option when the rate environment is flat or modestly rising and the loan balance is relatively small. On a $3 million loan in a flat rate environment, yield maintenance might cost $30,000 to $50,000 (the 1% floor or a small calculated penalty). Defeasance on the same loan would cost $30,000 to $75,000 in professional fees alone, before any net Treasury cost. The fixed overhead of defeasance makes it uneconomical for smaller loans unless the rate environment is extremely favorable.

As a general rule of thumb: for loans under $5 million, defeasance's fixed costs usually outweigh the potential Treasury savings, making yield maintenance more economical in most rate environments. For loans above $10 million in a rising rate environment, defeasance frequently delivers meaningful savings. For loans between $5 million and $10 million, the calculation depends on the specific rate spread and remaining term.

Process Complexity and Timeline

One of the most significant practical differences between defeasance and yield maintenance is the time and effort required to execute each one. For borrowers selling a property or closing a refinance on a deadline, the timeline can be just as important as the dollar cost.

Defeasance timeline: 30 to 45 days. A standard defeasance transaction involves multiple parties working in sequence. The defeasance consultant coordinates the overall process, the securities broker purchases the Treasury portfolio, attorneys prepare the legal opinions, the servicer reviews all documentation, and (for CMBS loans) the rating agencies must acknowledge the transaction. Each of these steps takes time, and delays in any one area can push the overall timeline. Experienced consultants can sometimes compress the process to 25 days for straightforward deals, but borrowers should plan for 30 to 45 days as a baseline. If you are selling a property with a CMBS loan, the defeasance timeline must be factored into your purchase and sale agreement. Buyers and their counsel should be aware that the seller needs adequate time between contract execution and closing to complete the defeasance.

Yield maintenance timeline: 5 to 10 days. A yield maintenance prepayment is comparatively simple. The borrower requests a payoff statement from the servicer, the servicer calculates the penalty using the formula in the loan documents, the borrower reviews the numbers and wires the total payoff amount (outstanding balance plus penalty plus any accrued interest), and the servicer releases the lien. The entire process can often be completed within a week, sometimes faster if the servicer is responsive and the borrower has all documentation ready. There are no outside consultants, no securities purchases, and no rating agency approvals required.

For time-sensitive transactions (a sale with a tight closing deadline, a refinance to capture favorable rates, or a 1031 exchange with identification period constraints), the speed of yield maintenance can be a decisive advantage. Defeasance requires advance planning. If you know you will need to defease a CMBS loan, begin the process as early as possible, ideally before you even list the property for sale or sign a purchase contract.

Which Loan Types Require Which Method

The prepayment method is not a choice the borrower makes freely. It is specified in the loan documents at origination and is closely tied to the type of loan and lender.

CMBS loans. The vast majority of CMBS conduit and single-asset/single-borrower (SASB) loans require defeasance as the prepayment mechanism. This is because CMBS loans are securitized and sold to bondholders as part of a trust. The trust structure depends on predictable cash flows, and defeasance preserves those cash flows by substituting Treasuries for the property collateral. Most CMBS loans also include an initial lockout period (typically two years from securitization) during which no prepayment of any kind is permitted. After the lockout, defeasance is available until a specified date near maturity (often 90 days before the maturity date), at which point the loan can be paid off at par. Some newer CMBS securitizations, particularly those issued after 2020, have begun offering yield maintenance as an alternative or supplement to defeasance, but this is still the exception rather than the norm.

Agency loans (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac). Agency multifamily loans almost universally use yield maintenance as the prepayment mechanism. These loans are held or guaranteed by the agencies, and the yield maintenance formula is standardized in the agency loan documents. Fannie Mae DUS (Delegated Underwriting and Servicing) loans and Freddie Mac Optigo loans both use yield maintenance with a 1% floor, though the specific formula details vary slightly between the two programs. Some agency loans offer a declining prepayment premium structure as an alternative, but yield maintenance is the default.

Life insurance company loans. Life company lenders (Metropolitan Life, Prudential, New York Life, TIAA, and similar institutions) predominantly use yield maintenance. Life companies hold their loans in portfolio, and yield maintenance provides clean, efficient compensation for early payoff without the administrative complexity of defeasance. The yield maintenance formulas used by life companies are generally similar to the agency formulas, though each lender may have its own variation.

Bank portfolio loans. Banks that originate and hold commercial real estate loans on their own balance sheet have the most flexibility in structuring prepayment provisions. Some bank loans use yield maintenance, some use step-down penalties (a declining percentage such as 5-4-3-2-1 over five years), and some are fully prepayable after a short lockout period. In many cases, the prepayment terms on a bank portfolio loan are negotiable at origination. Borrowers with strong banking relationships may be able to negotiate favorable prepayment terms, including the right to prepay at par after a specified date.

Tax Implications

The tax treatment of prepayment costs differs between defeasance and yield maintenance, and the distinction can affect the net after-tax cost of each method. Both situations require consultation with a qualified tax advisor, but here are the general principles.

Yield maintenance tax treatment. A yield maintenance penalty is generally treated as prepaid interest for federal income tax purposes. This means it is typically deductible as an interest expense in the year it is paid, similar to the treatment of other loan-related costs. For borrowers selling a property, the yield maintenance penalty reduces the net proceeds from the sale and may affect the capital gain calculation. The deductibility of the penalty can provide meaningful tax savings, particularly for borrowers in higher tax brackets or entities with significant other income to offset.

Defeasance tax treatment. The tax treatment of defeasance is more nuanced and depends on the specific structure of the transaction. Because defeasance involves the substitution of collateral rather than the payoff of the loan, some tax advisors have argued that it can qualify for installment sale treatment or other favorable treatment depending on the deal structure. The creation of the successor borrower entity and the assignment of the Treasury portfolio may also have tax implications. Any premium paid above par for the Treasury securities may be treated differently than a direct penalty payment. Given the complexity, borrowers pursuing defeasance should engage a tax advisor early in the process to understand the specific implications for their situation.

The tax treatment can meaningfully shift the effective cost comparison between the two methods. A yield maintenance penalty that is fully deductible as interest expense provides an immediate tax benefit, while the tax treatment of defeasance costs may be less straightforward. This is one more reason to model both options with the help of qualified professionals before committing to a prepayment strategy.

Decision Framework: Choosing the Right Prepayment Method

In many cases, borrowers do not have a choice between defeasance and yield maintenance because the loan documents dictate the method. However, understanding when each method is more advantageous helps with two important decisions: (1) structuring prepayment terms at loan origination, and (2) timing your prepayment to minimize cost under the method that applies to your loan.

Consider the remaining loan term. If your loan is within 12 to 18 months of maturity, yield maintenance is almost always cheaper because the penalty calculation is based on the remaining term. A shorter remaining term means fewer months of lost interest income, which drives the penalty toward the 1% floor. Defeasance also becomes less expensive near maturity (fewer Treasury payments to replicate), but the fixed professional fees remain constant regardless of remaining term. On a loan with only 12 months remaining, spending $50,000 on defeasance fees to save $20,000 on the net Treasury cost makes no sense.

Consider the interest rate environment. If rates have risen sharply (150+ basis points above your note rate) and you have a large loan ($10 million or more), defeasance offers the potential for substantial savings over the yield maintenance floor. Run both calculations before deciding. If rates are flat or have fallen, yield maintenance is typically less expensive because it avoids the fixed defeasance costs.

Consider your timeline. If you need to close a sale or refinance within two to three weeks, defeasance is not a realistic option. The 30- to 45-day process cannot be reliably compressed to meet a tight deadline. Yield maintenance can be executed in 5 to 10 business days, making it far more practical for time-sensitive transactions. If you know you will need to defease, build the timeline into your transaction calendar from the outset.

Consider the loan size. For loans under $5 million, defeasance's fixed costs ($30,000 to $75,000) consume a disproportionate share of any potential savings. Yield maintenance is nearly always the more economical choice at this scale. For loans above $10 million, the fixed costs become a smaller percentage of the total, and the variable savings from discounted Treasuries can be significant.

Consider the exit strategy. If you are selling the property, the prepayment cost is typically part of the closing calculation and reduces your net proceeds. If you are refinancing, the prepayment cost must be weighed against the savings from the new loan terms. In either case, modeling the total cost (including professional fees, penalties, and tax implications) under both methods gives you the clearest picture of the most efficient path forward.
