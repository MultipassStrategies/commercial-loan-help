Fannie Mae vs Freddie Mac Multifamily Loans: Head-to-Head Comparison

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Fannie Mae model: DUS (Delegated Underwriting and Servicing)
  • Freddie Mac model: Optigo (formerly Program Plus)
  • Minimum loan size: $1M (small loan/small balance programs)
  • Max LTV: Up to 80% (both programs)
  • Green programs: Fannie Green Rewards; Freddie Green Up
  • Market share: Roughly 50/50 split of agency multifamily lending

Overview of Agency Multifamily Lending

Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are the two government-sponsored enterprises (GSEs) that dominate the multifamily mortgage market in the United States. Together, they provide the majority of liquidity for apartment building financing, enabling property owners and investors to access long-term, fixed-rate and floating-rate capital at rates lower than what most private lenders can offer. Their role is essential to the functioning of the multifamily housing market, and their programs shape how apartment buildings are financed across the country.

Neither Fannie Mae nor Freddie Mac originates loans directly to borrowers. Instead, both agencies work through networks of approved lenders that originate, underwrite, and service loans on their behalf. Fannie Mae uses its DUS (Delegated Underwriting and Servicing) lender network, while Freddie Mac uses its Optigo network of approved seller/servicers. When you apply for an agency multifamily loan, you work with one of these approved lenders, not with the agency itself. The lender handles the application, underwriting, closing, and ongoing servicing, while the agency purchases or securitizes the loan on the secondary market.

The reason both agencies can offer the lowest rates in multifamily lending comes down to their implied government backing. Although Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are technically private entities (both have been in government conservatorship since 2008), investors in their mortgage-backed securities treat them as near-sovereign credit risks. This allows the agencies to raise capital at very low costs, which translates into lower borrowing rates for multifamily property owners. For stabilized apartment buildings with solid cash flow, agency loans consistently offer the most competitive pricing available in the market.

DUS vs. Optigo: How the Programs Work

The fundamental structural difference between Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac multifamily programs lies in how each agency delegates authority and shares risk with its approved lenders.

Fannie Mae's DUS (Delegated Underwriting and Servicing) program gives approved lenders significant autonomy. DUS lenders have the authority to underwrite and close loans on Fannie Mae's behalf without requiring prior approval from Fannie Mae on most standard transactions. In exchange for this delegation, DUS lenders share in the credit risk. Typically, a DUS lender bears approximately one-third of the loss if a loan defaults. This risk-sharing arrangement aligns the lender's incentives with Fannie Mae's and has historically produced strong credit performance. There are approximately 25 active DUS lenders nationwide, including major institutions like Walker & Dunlop, Berkadia, CBRE, and Wells Fargo.

Freddie Mac's Optigo program (formerly known as Program Plus) also relies on approved seller/servicers to originate and service loans. However, Freddie Mac retains more direct oversight of the underwriting process compared to Fannie Mae. Optigo lenders underwrite loans according to Freddie Mac's guidelines, and Freddie Mac maintains closer involvement in approving individual transactions, particularly for larger or more complex deals. The Optigo network is larger than the DUS network, which means borrowers may have more lender options to choose from when seeking a Freddie Mac execution.

From the borrower's perspective, the day-to-day experience of obtaining a Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac loan is quite similar. You apply through an approved lender, provide financial statements and property documentation, go through underwriting, and close the loan. The differences in how risk is shared and authority is delegated operate behind the scenes. However, these structural differences can affect pricing, underwriting flexibility, and how quickly a deal can close. Because DUS lenders bear loss risk, they may be more conservative on marginal deals but can also move faster on straightforward transactions since they do not need to wait for agency approval.

Rate Structure and Execution Differences

Both Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac offer highly competitive fixed and floating interest rates for multifamily loans. Rates from both agencies are typically quoted as a spread over a benchmark: U.S. Treasury yields for fixed-rate loans and SOFR for floating-rate loans. The DUS or Optigo lender adds a servicing fee on top of the agency's required spread, and the all-in rate you pay reflects the combination of the benchmark, the agency spread, and the lender's servicing strip.

In practice, the rate difference between a Fannie Mae loan and a Freddie Mac loan on the same property is usually minimal, often falling within 5 to 15 basis points. The specific rate you receive depends more on which lender you work with, how competitive that lender wants to be on your deal, the loan amount, and the property's risk profile than on which agency is behind the loan. Both agencies price off similar capital markets benchmarks and face similar investor demand for their mortgage-backed securities.

That said, there are some historical tendencies worth noting. Freddie Mac has generally been more competitive on floating-rate executions, partly because of how it structures and sells its floating-rate securities. Borrowers seeking variable-rate loans with longer floating-rate terms (up to 10 years through Freddie Mac, compared to 7 years through Fannie Mae) may find Freddie Mac's pricing more attractive. Fannie Mae, on the other hand, has been particularly strong on longer-term fixed-rate pricing, especially for 12-year, 15-year, and 18-year fixed terms that Freddie Mac does not offer in exactly the same way.

Both agencies allow early rate locks of up to 180 days, giving borrowers the ability to secure pricing well before closing. This is especially valuable in volatile rate environments, where locking in a favorable rate early can save significant money. The mechanics of rate locking are similar between the two agencies, though the specific lock fees and extension policies may vary by lender.

Loan Size Sweet Spots

Both agencies have developed dedicated programs for smaller multifamily loans, recognizing that the standard underwriting process can be overly burdensome for loans below a certain threshold. These small loan programs feature streamlined documentation, faster processing, and pricing that is specifically tailored to smaller balance transactions.

Fannie Mae's Small Loan program covers loans from $1 million to $6 million, with the upper limit extending to $9 million in certain high-cost markets. The program offers simplified underwriting with reduced documentation requirements, which can translate into faster closings and lower transaction costs for borrowers. Small Loan pricing is competitive and often includes interest-only options during the initial years of the loan term.

Freddie Mac's Small Balance Loan (SBL) program covers a slightly wider range, from $1 million to $7.5 million. Like Fannie Mae's Small Loan program, the SBL features streamlined underwriting, competitive rates, and flexible prepayment options. Freddie Mac has been particularly active in the small balance space and has built a strong reputation for fast execution on these smaller deals.

For standard loans above the small loan thresholds, both agencies are highly competitive and offer similar terms. The choice between Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac for a $15 million or $30 million deal typically comes down to which lender relationship offers the best pricing and terms at that moment, rather than any inherent advantage of one agency over the other. For very large loans above $50 million, borrowers may find that one agency is more or less receptive depending on current capacity, portfolio concentrations, and strategic priorities. Working with multiple lenders across both agencies is the best way to ensure competitive pricing on larger transactions.

Green and Affordable Housing Programs

Both Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac have developed specialized programs that reward property owners for investing in energy efficiency and affordable housing. These programs can meaningfully reduce borrowing costs and are worth evaluating for any multifamily transaction.

Fannie Mae's Green Rewards program offers a rate reduction of typically 10 to 25 basis points for properties that commit to energy or water efficiency improvements. To qualify, borrowers must obtain a property energy and water assessment that identifies potential savings, then commit to implementing specified improvements within 12 months of closing. The rate reduction is applied at origination, meaning borrowers benefit from lower debt service immediately. Green Rewards can also increase available loan proceeds, as the projected utility savings are credited toward the property's income in the underwriting analysis.

Freddie Mac's Green Up program offers a similar incentive structure, providing rate reductions for properties that meet green certification standards or commit to implementing efficiency improvements. The specific requirements, eligible improvements, and reduction amounts vary from the Fannie Mae program, so borrowers should request quotes under both programs to determine which offers better execution for their particular property.

On the affordable housing side, both agencies have strong mandates to support affordable and workforce housing. Fannie Mae offers dedicated affordable housing programs and mission-driven financing options, with enhanced terms for properties with income-restricted units or that serve low- and moderate-income renters. Freddie Mac's Targeted Affordable Housing (TAH) program provides similar benefits for properties with affordability restrictions, including higher leverage (up to 90% LTV), lower DSCR requirements, and reduced pricing. For borrowers with properties that qualify, these programs can significantly improve loan economics compared to conventional agency terms.

Supplemental Loan Programs

One of the most valuable features of agency multifamily lending is the ability to obtain supplemental financing behind an existing agency first mortgage. Both Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac allow borrowers to take out a second mortgage (supplemental loan) on a property that already has an agency first mortgage in place, typically after the first mortgage has been in place for at least 12 months.

Supplemental loans allow borrowers to access equity that has built up in a property through appreciation, rent growth, or principal paydown without refinancing the first mortgage. This is particularly important when the existing first mortgage has a favorable interest rate that the borrower does not want to give up, or when prepayment penalties on the first mortgage would make refinancing prohibitively expensive.

Both agencies limit supplemental loan amounts to maintain overall loan-to-value and debt service coverage ratio requirements. The combined first mortgage and supplemental loan must still meet the agency's LTV and DSCR thresholds, which typically means the supplemental loan amount depends on how much the property has appreciated and how much the first mortgage has been paid down since origination. Supplemental loans are popular among experienced multifamily operators who use them to extract value appreciation over time, fund capital improvements, or acquire additional properties without disturbing their existing financing structure.

Specialty Property Programs

While conventional apartment buildings make up the bulk of agency multifamily lending, both Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac offer financing for several specialty property types within the multifamily sector. Each agency has different strengths and levels of activity across these niches.

Manufactured housing communities. Both agencies provide dedicated programs for manufactured housing communities (also known as mobile home parks). Freddie Mac has historically been more active in this space and has developed specialized underwriting guidelines that reflect the unique characteristics of manufactured housing, including pad rent structures, tenant-owned home dynamics, and community infrastructure considerations. Fannie Mae also lends on manufactured housing communities, though borrowers in this space often find Freddie Mac lenders more experienced with the product type.

Student housing. Both agencies will finance student housing properties, but with specific underwriting requirements that reflect the unique occupancy patterns and risk profile of student-oriented apartments. Properties must typically be located near a college or university campus, and underwriting accounts for the by-the-bed leasing structures and higher turnover rates common in student housing. Both agencies generally require strong historical occupancy and may impose additional reserves or lower leverage for student housing deals compared to conventional apartments.

Seniors housing (independent living). Independent living communities for seniors are available through both Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, with specialized underwriting that considers the healthcare-adjacent nature of the properties, the age demographics of the tenant base, and the specific amenity and service offerings. Assisted living and skilled nursing facilities are generally outside the scope of agency lending, but independent living communities with minimal healthcare components can qualify.

Cooperative housing. Cooperative apartment financing is primarily a Fannie Mae product. Fannie Mae has well-established programs for co-op blanket mortgages, and the DUS lender network includes several firms with deep expertise in cooperative housing. Freddie Mac has limited co-op lending activity, so borrowers seeking financing for cooperative apartment buildings will typically find better options through the Fannie Mae DUS channel.

Which Agency Fits Which Borrower

Choosing between Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac is not a matter of one agency being universally better than the other. Both offer excellent products at competitive rates, and for many deals, the differences are marginal. However, certain situations may favor one agency over the other.

Freddie Mac Optigo may be preferred when:

  • You want access to a wider range of approved lenders and more competitive bidding on your deal
  • You prefer a floating-rate execution, especially with a term longer than 7 years
  • Your property is in a smaller or secondary market where Freddie Mac lenders may be more active
  • You are seeking Small Balance Loan (SBL) financing in the $1 million to $7.5 million range
  • Your property is a manufactured housing community, where Freddie Mac has deeper expertise
  • You want more prepayment flexibility, as Freddie Mac offers step-down options in addition to yield maintenance and defeasance

Fannie Mae DUS may be preferred when:

  • You want longer-term fixed-rate pricing, particularly for 12-year, 15-year, or 18-year fixed terms
  • You have an established relationship with a strong DUS lender who can leverage delegated authority for faster execution
  • You want to take advantage of Green Rewards pricing and the additional proceeds benefit
  • Your property is a cooperative apartment building, where Fannie Mae is the primary agency option
  • The DUS lender's delegated authority means your deal can close faster without waiting for agency approval on standard transactions

For many borrowers, the best strategy is to work with a mortgage banker or broker who has relationships across both agency platforms. By soliciting quotes from both Fannie Mae DUS lenders and Freddie Mac Optigo lenders simultaneously, you create competition that drives better pricing regardless of which agency ultimately funds your loan. Many of the largest multifamily operators in the country maintain active relationships with lenders approved by both agencies and select the best execution for each deal on a case-by-case basis.
