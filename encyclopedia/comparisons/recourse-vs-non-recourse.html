Recourse vs Non-Recourse Commercial Loans: What Borrowers Must Understand

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Personal liability: Recourse: full personal guarantee; Non-recourse: limited to collateral (with carve-outs)
  • Typical loan types: Recourse: bank loans, SBA; Non-recourse: CMBS, agency, life company
  • Rate/term trade-offs: Non-recourse may carry slightly higher rates or require lower LTV
  • Bad boy carve-outs: Non-recourse loans include exceptions that trigger personal liability
  • Guarantor requirements: Non-recourse still requires a guarantor for carve-outs
  • Default consequences: Recourse: lender pursues all assets; Non-recourse: lender takes property only (unless carve-outs triggered)

What Recourse Means in Commercial Lending

A recourse commercial loan gives the lender the right to pursue the borrower's personal assets if the property's value does not fully cover the outstanding loan balance after a default and foreclosure. In practical terms, when you sign a full personal guarantee on a commercial mortgage, you are pledging everything you own (real estate, bank accounts, investment portfolios, and other personal property) as additional security for the loan. If the property is sold at foreclosure for less than what you owe, the lender can obtain a deficiency judgment against you for the remaining balance.

Deficiency judgments are the mechanism through which recourse lenders recover shortfalls. After foreclosing on the collateral property and applying the sale proceeds to the outstanding loan balance, the lender petitions the court for a judgment equal to the difference. For example, if you owe $3,000,000 on a commercial mortgage and the property sells at foreclosure for $2,200,000, the lender can pursue a deficiency judgment of $800,000 against you personally. This judgment can be enforced through wage garnishment, bank account levies, liens on other properties, and seizure of non-exempt personal assets.

The scope of personal liability under a recourse loan is broad. Most recourse guarantees are "unlimited" or "full," meaning the guarantor is liable for the entire loan balance, accrued interest, late fees, legal costs, and any other expenses the lender incurs in the collection process. Some lenders also require guarantors to sign environmental indemnification agreements, which add potential liability for property contamination remediation costs that can exceed the original loan amount.

Recourse loans dominate the smaller end of the commercial real estate lending market. Banks, credit unions, and community lenders almost universally require personal guarantees because they hold these loans on their own balance sheets and want the additional layer of borrower commitment that a personal guarantee provides. From the lender's perspective, a borrower who has personal assets at stake is more likely to work through property challenges rather than simply walking away and surrendering the keys.

What Non-Recourse Means in Commercial Lending

A non-recourse commercial loan limits the lender's recovery to the collateral property itself. If the borrower defaults and the property is sold for less than the loan balance, the lender absorbs the loss and cannot pursue the borrower's personal assets for the shortfall. The borrower's exposure is limited to losing the property and the equity invested in it.

Non-recourse lending is the standard structure for larger commercial real estate transactions originated through capital markets channels. CMBS (commercial mortgage-backed securities) loans, agency loans from Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, and life insurance company loans are all typically non-recourse. These lenders underwrite primarily to the property's cash flow and value rather than the borrower's personal financial strength, though borrower experience and track record still matter in the approval process.

Most non-recourse loans require the borrowing entity to be structured as a special purpose entity (SPE), sometimes called a single-asset entity or bankruptcy-remote entity. An SPE is a legal entity (usually an LLC or limited partnership) that is created solely to hold title to the property and the loan. The SPE cannot have other business operations, debts, or employees beyond what is necessary to own and operate the collateral property. This structure isolates the property from the borrower's other business risks and prevents creditors of the borrower's other ventures from making claims against the collateral property.

SPE requirements typically include maintaining separate books and records, having at least one independent director or manager who must consent to any bankruptcy filing, holding no assets other than the collateral property, and avoiding commingling funds with the borrower's other entities. These structural requirements protect the lender by ensuring that the property remains a clean, identifiable source of repayment and that a voluntary bankruptcy filing cannot be made casually.

It is important to understand that "non-recourse" does not mean "no liability." Every non-recourse loan contains carve-outs (discussed in detail below) that can trigger personal liability for specific bad acts. The guarantee is narrower than a full recourse guarantee, but it is not zero.

Which Loan Types Are Typically Recourse vs Non-Recourse

The recourse or non-recourse structure of a commercial loan is largely determined by the type of lender and the lending channel. Understanding which products carry personal liability helps you plan your financing strategy before you begin the application process.

Bank and credit union loans (recourse). Traditional commercial bank loans and credit union loans are almost always full recourse. These lenders hold the loans on their balance sheets and rely on the personal guarantee as a key risk mitigant. Banks view the guarantee as both a financial backstop and a behavioral incentive: borrowers with personal assets at risk are more engaged in property management and more willing to inject additional capital when needed. Even for strong borrowers with excellent track records, most banks will require a full personal guarantee. Some community banks may offer partial recourse or limited guarantees for their best borrower relationships, but this is the exception rather than the rule.

SBA loans (recourse). Both SBA 7(a) and SBA 504 loans require personal guarantees from any individual owning 20% or more of the borrowing entity. The SBA guarantee to the lender (typically 75% to 85% of the loan amount) protects the lender from loss, not the borrower. If the business defaults, the SBA pays the lender and then the SBA (through its collection arm) pursues the borrower personally for the guaranteed portion. This is a common misconception among small business owners who believe the SBA guarantee somehow reduces their personal exposure.

CMBS loans (non-recourse). CMBS loans are the most recognized non-recourse product in commercial real estate. Because these loans are pooled and sold to bond investors, the underwriting focuses on the property's cash flow and value rather than the borrower's personal balance sheet. CMBS loans typically require a minimum loan amount of $2 million or more, an SPE borrowing entity, and a carve-out guarantor who meets specific net worth and liquidity thresholds. The standardized non-recourse structure is one of the key advantages of CMBS lending for borrowers who want to limit personal exposure on larger deals.

Agency loans (non-recourse). Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac multifamily loan programs are non-recourse with carve-out guarantees. Agency lending is the primary source of non-recourse financing for multifamily properties, with loan amounts starting as low as $1 million for smaller apartment buildings. Agency loans combine the asset protection of non-recourse lending with competitive interest rates and high leverage (up to 80% LTV), making them the preferred financing tool for multifamily investors nationwide.

Life insurance company loans (non-recourse). Life companies are conservative, long-term lenders that provide non-recourse financing for high-quality, stabilized commercial properties. They typically lend at lower leverage (55% to 65% LTV), offer the lowest interest rates in the market, and focus on Class A properties in primary markets. Life company non-recourse loans are the gold standard for borrowers seeking maximum asset protection with the most favorable terms, though the low leverage and strict property quality requirements limit access to well-capitalized borrowers with institutional-grade assets.

Bridge loans (varies). Bridge loans can be either recourse or non-recourse depending on the lender. Debt fund bridge lenders typically offer non-recourse terms (with carve-outs) for loans above $5 million, while bank bridge loans and smaller balance bridge products are usually full recourse. The recourse structure of a bridge loan often depends on the leverage, the borrower's experience, and the perceived risk of the business plan being executed.

Bad Boy Carve-Outs: The Recourse Trigger in Non-Recourse Loans

Bad boy carve-outs are the most important provisions in any non-recourse loan document. They define the specific borrower actions that will convert the loan from non-recourse to full recourse, exposing the guarantor to personal liability for the entire outstanding loan balance. Every commercial real estate professional and borrower should understand these provisions before signing a non-recourse loan.

The term "bad boy" reflects the nature of the triggering events: they are actions that the borrower should never take under normal circumstances. The carve-outs exist to prevent borrowers from abusing the non-recourse structure by engaging in behavior that harms the lender's collateral position. Common bad boy carve-out triggers include the following.

Voluntary bankruptcy filing. Filing for bankruptcy protection without the lender's consent is the most frequently triggered carve-out. Non-recourse loan documents typically require the SPE to have an independent director or manager whose approval is needed to file for bankruptcy. If the borrower circumvents this requirement or causes the SPE to file voluntarily, the carve-out guarantor becomes personally liable for the full loan balance. This provision exists because a bankruptcy filing can delay foreclosure for months or years, during which the property may deteriorate and the lender's collateral value may decline.

Fraud and intentional misrepresentation. Any fraud committed in connection with the loan (fabricated financial statements, inflated rent rolls, forged documents) triggers full personal liability. This carve-out is broadly interpreted by courts, and even material omissions in loan applications have been held to constitute misrepresentation sufficient to trigger recourse.

Misapplication of rents and insurance proceeds. Non-recourse loans require that property income be used first to pay operating expenses and debt service. Diverting rents to other purposes (funding another project, personal expenses, or paying down other debts) triggers the carve-out. Similarly, insurance proceeds from a casualty event must be applied to repair the property or pay down the loan. Using these funds for any other purpose exposes the guarantor to personal liability.

Unauthorized property transfers. Non-recourse loans contain "due on sale" and "due on transfer" provisions that prohibit selling, transferring, or encumbering the property without the lender's written consent. Transferring ownership interests in the borrowing entity without approval also triggers the carve-out. This includes adding new members, changing the managing member, or pledging ownership interests as collateral for another loan.

Environmental violations. If the property becomes contaminated due to the borrower's actions or negligence, the carve-out guarantor is personally liable for all remediation costs plus any resulting damage to the lender's collateral position. Environmental carve-outs are uncapped, meaning the guarantor's liability can exceed the original loan amount if remediation costs are high.

SPE violations. Failing to maintain the SPE structure (commingling funds, allowing other business operations in the entity, failing to maintain separate books) can trigger the carve-out. Lenders view SPE integrity as fundamental to the non-recourse structure, and violations are treated seriously.

The carve-out guarantor (sometimes called the "non-recourse carve-out guarantor" or "springing recourse guarantor") is typically the principal of the borrowing entity or a creditworthy individual or entity designated at closing. Even though the loan is non-recourse, the guarantor must meet specific financial thresholds, which we cover in the guarantor requirements section below.

Rate and LTV Trade-Offs

The recourse vs non-recourse decision has a direct impact on your loan pricing and available leverage. Understanding these trade-offs helps you evaluate the true cost of asset protection.

Recourse loans generally carry lower interest rates than non-recourse loans because the personal guarantee reduces the lender's risk. When a lender has the ability to pursue the borrower's personal assets in addition to the collateral property, the expected loss in a default scenario is lower. This reduced risk translates to tighter pricing. The rate differential is typically 0.25% to 0.75%, though the exact spread depends on the specific loan products being compared, the borrower's credit profile, and market conditions.

Recourse loans also tend to offer higher leverage. Banks commonly lend up to 80% or even 85% LTV on recourse loans for strong borrowers, while non-recourse CMBS loans typically cap at 75% LTV and life company loans rarely exceed 65% LTV. Agency multifamily loans are an exception, offering up to 80% LTV on a non-recourse basis, which is one reason agency lending is so popular among apartment investors.

The lower rate and higher leverage of recourse loans come at a cost: your personal net worth is at risk. For a borrower deciding between a recourse bank loan at 6.50% and 80% LTV versus a non-recourse CMBS loan at 6.75% and 75% LTV, the analysis goes beyond the rate. The additional 5% equity required for the CMBS loan means more cash out of pocket at closing, but the borrower's personal assets are protected. On a $5 million property, the equity difference is $250,000 and the annual interest cost difference on a $3.75 million CMBS loan versus a $4 million bank loan is relatively modest.

For many experienced investors, the asset protection of non-recourse financing is worth the higher cost. This is especially true for borrowers with significant personal wealth outside of commercial real estate, those with multiple properties and concentrated exposure, and borrowers engaged in estate planning where personal liability could affect heirs and trust structures.

Conversely, recourse financing often makes more sense for smaller deals (under $2 million) where non-recourse products are simply unavailable, for borrowers who need maximum leverage to make a deal work, and for investors with strong bank relationships who can negotiate favorable recourse terms including partial guarantees or burn-off provisions.

Personal Guaranty Requirements

Understanding the different types of personal guarantees in commercial lending is essential for evaluating your true risk exposure on any transaction.

Full personal guaranty (recourse loans). A full guaranty makes the guarantor liable for the entire loan balance, accrued interest, fees, and collection costs. This is the standard guarantee on recourse commercial loans. The guarantor is obligated to pay any deficiency after the collateral property is liquidated. Full guarantees are typically joint and several when multiple guarantors are involved, meaning the lender can pursue any one guarantor for the entire amount. Courts have consistently enforced full personal guarantees, and they are difficult to discharge in bankruptcy proceedings.

Carve-out guaranty (non-recourse loans). A carve-out guaranty limits the guarantor's liability to the bad boy carve-out events discussed above. Under normal circumstances (a straightforward default without any bad acts), the guarantor has no personal liability. The guarantee only "springs" into effect if a triggering event occurs. However, the carve-out guaranty is still a serious obligation because the triggers can be activated inadvertently (for example, an SPE violation that the borrower did not realize constituted a breach), and once triggered, the guarantor faces full recourse liability for the entire loan balance.

Completion guaranty (construction loans). Construction loans, whether recourse or non-recourse, typically include a completion guaranty that makes the guarantor personally liable for the cost to complete the project if the borrower is unable to finish construction. The completion guaranty usually burns off once the project achieves a certificate of occupancy and meets specific performance milestones (such as reaching a minimum occupancy rate or debt service coverage ratio). This guaranty protects the lender from being left with a partially completed building that may be worth less than the outstanding loan balance.

Burn-off guarantees. A burn-off guarantee starts as full recourse and reduces or eliminates the personal guarantee once specific conditions are met. Common burn-off triggers include the loan balance reaching a target LTV (such as 65% or lower), the property achieving a target DSCR (such as 1.40x or higher), or a specified period of time elapsing (such as 36 months of on-time payments). Burn-off provisions are most common on bank loans and are a valuable negotiating tool for borrowers who want recourse terms initially but plan to stabilize or delever the property over time.

Spousal Guarantees

Spousal guarantees are a sensitive but important aspect of commercial loan guaranty requirements. Many lenders require the spouse of the primary guarantor to also sign the personal guarantee, particularly in community property states and in situations where marital property is at risk.

In the nine community property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin), assets acquired during the marriage are generally owned equally by both spouses. A lender seeking to enforce a personal guarantee against the guarantor's community property assets may need the spouse's signature on the guarantee to have a valid claim against those assets. Without the spousal guarantee, the lender's ability to collect from community property may be limited or require additional legal proceedings.

Even in common law property states, lenders may request a spousal guarantee if the spouse has a significant ownership interest in the borrowing entity, if marital assets constitute a substantial portion of the guarantor's net worth, or if the lender believes the guarantor could attempt to transfer assets to the spouse to avoid collection. The spousal guarantee ensures that the lender can reach the full scope of assets that support the guarantor's financial representation.

Spousal guarantees can create personal and financial tension, particularly when one spouse is actively involved in commercial real estate investing and the other is not. Borrowers should discuss spousal guarantee requirements with their attorney before signing, and couples should understand that a spousal guarantee puts the non-investing spouse's assets at risk in the event of a default and deficiency judgment.

How to Negotiate Recourse Provisions

While non-recourse loans from capital markets lenders (CMBS, agency, life company) have standardized terms with limited room for negotiation, recourse provisions on bank loans are often negotiable. The following strategies can help reduce your personal exposure on recourse financing.

Burn-off provisions. Request that the personal guarantee burn off (reduce to zero or convert to a carve-out-only guarantee) once the loan reaches a specific LTV threshold or DSCR level. For example, you might negotiate for the guarantee to burn off once the LTV drops below 65% through a combination of amortization and property value appreciation. Banks are often willing to include burn-off provisions because they demonstrate the borrower's confidence in the property's performance.

Partial recourse. Instead of a full guarantee of 100% of the loan balance, negotiate for a partial guarantee of 25% to 50% of the loan amount. Partial recourse still gives the lender additional protection beyond the collateral property but limits the guarantor's maximum personal exposure. This structure is more common in competitive lending environments and for established borrowers with strong banking relationships.

Recourse reduction triggers. Structure the guarantee to reduce over time based on property performance milestones. For instance, the guarantee might start at 50% of the loan balance and reduce by 10% for each year that the property maintains a DSCR above 1.30x, eventually reaching zero. This approach rewards the borrower for successful property management and aligns the lender's risk reduction with actual property performance.

Time-based burn-off. Negotiate for the guarantee to expire after a fixed period (commonly 24 to 36 months) of timely loan payments and covenant compliance. The logic is that the greatest default risk occurs in the early years of the loan, and once the borrower has demonstrated consistent performance, the personal guarantee is less critical to the lender's credit analysis.

Limiting guarantee scope. Even if full recourse cannot be avoided, you may be able to limit the guarantee to specific categories. For example, negotiate for the guarantee to exclude environmental liability, or cap the guarantee at the original loan amount (excluding accrued interest and legal fees). These limitations may seem small, but they can meaningfully reduce worst-case exposure in a default scenario.

The strength of your negotiating position depends on several factors: the competitiveness of the lending market, your relationship with the bank, your track record as a borrower, the quality of the collateral property, and whether other lenders are competing for the deal. Working with an experienced commercial mortgage broker can help you identify which provisions are most likely to be negotiable for your specific situation.

Guarantor Net Worth and Liquidity Requirements

Even on non-recourse loans where the guarantor is only responsible for carve-out events, lenders impose strict financial requirements on the designated guarantor. These requirements ensure that the carve-out guarantee has real economic substance and that the guarantor can meet their obligations if a triggering event occurs.

Net worth requirement. The most common standard requires the guarantor to have a net worth (total assets minus total liabilities) at least equal to the loan amount. On a $5 million CMBS loan, the carve-out guarantor must demonstrate a net worth of $5 million or more. Some lenders allow the equity in the collateral property to count toward the net worth calculation, while others require the net worth to be established independent of the subject property. Life insurance companies and some CMBS special servicers require net worth to exceed the loan amount by a specified margin.

Liquidity requirement. In addition to net worth, lenders typically require the guarantor to maintain liquid assets equal to at least 10% of the loan amount. Liquid assets include cash, publicly traded securities, and money market funds. Real estate equity, retirement accounts, and illiquid investments generally do not count toward the liquidity threshold. On a $5 million loan, the guarantor must show $500,000 or more in readily accessible liquid assets.

Ongoing financial reporting. Guarantor financial requirements are not just a closing condition. Most non-recourse loan documents require the guarantor to provide annual personal financial statements and, in some cases, tax returns to the lender or servicer. If the guarantor's net worth or liquidity falls below the required thresholds during the loan term, the borrower may be required to provide a replacement guarantor or face a loan default.

Entity guarantors. In some cases, an entity (rather than an individual) can serve as the carve-out guarantor. This is common for institutional borrowers, real estate funds, and family office structures. The entity must meet the same net worth and liquidity thresholds, and the lender will typically look through the entity to evaluate the financial strength of its principals. Entity guarantors must also maintain their organizational structure and financial condition throughout the loan term.

Why do lenders require financial qualifications for guarantors on non-recourse loans? The carve-out guarantee is only as strong as the guarantor behind it. A guarantor with no assets provides no meaningful protection against bad acts, which defeats the purpose of the carve-out provisions. By requiring substantial net worth and liquidity, lenders ensure that the threat of personal liability is real and that the guarantor has a genuine financial incentive to comply with all loan covenants and avoid triggering events.

For borrowers who cannot personally meet the guarantor requirements, options include bringing in a partner or co-investor who qualifies, using a family member or business associate as the guarantor (with their informed consent and independent legal counsel), or seeking out lenders with more flexible guarantor standards. Some CMBS lenders will accept guarantors whose net worth is slightly below the loan amount if the borrower can demonstrate other compensating factors, such as a large portfolio with a strong performance history or additional collateral pledged as security for the carve-out guarantee.
