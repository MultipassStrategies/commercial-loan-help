Mezzanine Debt vs Preferred Equity: Capital Stack Comparison for CRE

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Position in capital stack: Both sit between senior debt and common equity
  • Typical cost: Mezz 10-15%; Pref equity 12-18% preferred return
  • Security type: Mezz: pledge of ownership interest; Pref equity: equity interest with priority distributions
  • Foreclosure rights: Mezz can foreclose on ownership; Pref equity has control rights but not foreclosure
  • Tax treatment: Mezz interest is deductible; Pref equity returns are profit distributions
  • When to use each: Mezz for leverage, cost certainty; Pref equity when senior lender prohibits mezz

Overview of Capital Stack Financing

The capital stack is the complete set of financing sources used to fund a commercial real estate acquisition or development. It defines who gets paid first, who bears the most risk, and how returns are distributed among all parties in the deal. Understanding the capital stack is essential for any sponsor or investor evaluating how to structure a transaction, because each layer carries different costs, rights, and risk profiles.

A typical commercial real estate capital stack has four layers, ordered from lowest risk (and lowest return) at the bottom to highest risk (and highest return) at the top. Senior debt sits at the base, usually representing 55% to 75% of the total capital needed. Senior lenders have the first claim on property cash flow and the first lien on the real property, giving them the strongest security position. Above senior debt is mezzanine financing, which fills a portion of the gap between the senior loan and the sponsor's equity. Preferred equity occupies the next layer, providing capital with priority over common equity but subordinate to all debt. Common equity sits at the top of the stack, representing the sponsor's own investment plus any joint venture equity partners. Common equity holders bear the most risk but also capture the largest share of upside when the deal performs well.

Sponsors turn to subordinate capital (mezzanine debt or preferred equity) for several practical reasons. First, senior lenders rarely provide more than 65% to 75% of the capital needed, leaving a funding gap that must be filled. Second, using subordinate capital allows sponsors to reduce the amount of their own equity required, which increases the return on equity through leverage. Third, in competitive acquisition markets, the ability to assemble a complete capital stack quickly can be the difference between winning and losing a deal. The choice between mezzanine debt and preferred equity to fill that gap depends on deal specifics, lender requirements, cost considerations, and the legal and tax implications explored in detail below.

Mezzanine Debt: Structure and Mechanics

Mezzanine debt is a loan that sits behind the senior mortgage in the capital stack. Unlike the senior loan, which is secured by a mortgage or deed of trust on the real property itself, a mezzanine loan is secured by a pledge of the borrower's ownership interest in the entity that owns the property. This distinction is fundamental to how mezzanine financing works and how it is enforced in a default scenario.

In a typical structure, the property is owned by a single-purpose entity (SPE), often a limited liability company. The senior lender holds a first lien on the real property. The mezzanine lender holds a pledge of the membership interests in the SPE from its parent entity (the borrower). If the borrower defaults on the mezzanine loan, the mezzanine lender can foreclose on those pledged membership interests through a Uniform Commercial Code (UCC) Article 9 foreclosure process. This process is significantly faster than a traditional real estate foreclosure. A UCC foreclosure can often be completed in 60 to 90 days, compared to six months to two years for a judicial real estate foreclosure. By taking ownership of the membership interests, the mezzanine lender effectively gains control of the entity that owns the property, and therefore indirect control of the property itself.

Mezzanine loan terms typically include a fixed interest rate between 10% and 15%, a maturity of two to five years, and interest payments that may be structured as current pay (paid monthly), accrued (added to the loan balance and paid at maturity), or a combination of both. Origination fees of 1% to 2% are standard. Most mezzanine loans are interest-only during the term, with the full principal balance due at maturity. Prepayment provisions vary but are generally less restrictive than those on senior fixed-rate loans.

Because mezzanine debt is structurally senior to preferred equity and common equity (meaning the mezzanine lender gets paid before equity holders in a liquidation), the cost of mezzanine financing is typically lower than the cost of preferred equity. However, this structural advantage comes with a requirement that is often the most significant practical consideration in the transaction: the senior lender must agree to the mezzanine loan through an intercreditor agreement.

The intercreditor agreement (ICA) governs the relationship between the senior lender and the mezzanine lender. It establishes standstill periods during which the mezzanine lender cannot exercise remedies, cure rights that allow the mezzanine lender to cure defaults on the senior loan, and buyout provisions that give the mezzanine lender the option to purchase the senior loan at par. Negotiating the ICA is often the most time-consuming and expensive part of adding mezzanine debt to a capital stack, frequently requiring four to eight weeks and $25,000 to $75,000 in legal fees for all parties.

Preferred Equity: Structure and Mechanics

Preferred equity is not a loan. It is an equity investment in the property-owning entity that comes with preferential rights to distributions and, in many cases, certain control provisions that activate upon specified trigger events. The preferred equity investor becomes a member or partner in the entity, with contractual priority over common equity holders for both current distributions and return of capital.

In a standard preferred equity structure, the investor contributes capital to the entity in exchange for a preferred return, typically ranging from 12% to 18% annually. This preferred return must be paid (or accrued) before any distributions flow to common equity. The preferred return may be structured as current pay (distributed monthly or quarterly from property cash flow), accrued (compounding and paid upon a capital event such as refinance or sale), or a combination where a portion is paid currently and the remainder accrues.

Beyond the preferred return, many preferred equity arrangements include an equity kicker or profit participation component. This means the preferred equity investor receives not only the stated preferred return but also a share of the property's appreciation or operating profits above certain hurdle rates. For example, the preferred equity provider might receive a 14% preferred return plus 20% of profits above a 20% internal rate of return to the common equity. This upside participation is one of the primary reasons preferred equity costs more than mezzanine debt on a total-return basis.

The waterfall structure in a preferred equity deal determines the order in which cash flow and capital event proceeds are distributed. A typical waterfall might work as follows: first, operating cash flow pays senior debt service. Second, the preferred equity investor receives its current-pay preferred return. Third, any remaining cash flow is distributed to common equity. Upon a sale or refinance, capital is returned in a similar priority: senior debt repayment first, then preferred equity capital plus any accrued and unpaid return, then common equity. If the proceeds are insufficient to repay all layers, preferred equity absorbs losses before the senior lender but after common equity.

Instead of foreclosure rights, preferred equity investors protect their position through control provisions embedded in the operating agreement of the property-owning entity. These provisions typically include the right to remove and replace the managing member upon certain trigger events (such as failure to pay the preferred return for a specified period, a default on the senior loan, or a material breach of the operating agreement), approval rights over major decisions (refinancing, additional indebtedness, capital expenditures above a threshold, leases to affiliates), the right to block distributions to common equity until all preferred returns are current, and in some cases, the right to force a sale of the property if defaults are not cured within a specified period.

Cost Comparison and Total Return Analysis

On a stated-rate basis, mezzanine debt is almost always cheaper than preferred equity. A mezzanine loan at 12% interest provides the capital provider with a fixed, predictable return, and the borrower knows exactly what the cost of that capital will be over the loan term. Preferred equity at a 14% preferred return already starts at a higher nominal cost, and when upside participation is factored in, the total cost to the sponsor can climb significantly higher.

Consider a $2 million capital raise on a $10 million acquisition. A mezzanine loan at 12% interest over three years costs the sponsor $240,000 per year in interest, or $720,000 over the full term (assuming current-pay interest). If the property appreciates by 30% and is sold for $13 million, the mezzanine lender receives its $2 million principal plus accrued interest and nothing more. The sponsor keeps all of the appreciation.

Now consider the same $2 million raised as preferred equity at a 14% preferred return plus 20% of profits above a 15% IRR to the sponsor. The annual preferred return is $280,000 ($840,000 over three years), already $120,000 more than the mezzanine cost. Additionally, if the sponsor's IRR exceeds 15%, the preferred equity investor receives 20% of the excess profits. On a deal that generates $3 million in total profit, the preferred equity provider might receive an additional $200,000 to $400,000 in profit participation, bringing the total cost of that capital to $1,040,000 to $1,240,000 over three years. The effective annual cost of the preferred equity in this scenario ranges from roughly 17% to 21%, far exceeding the 12% mezzanine rate.

The tax treatment further widens the gap. Because mezzanine interest is deductible, a sponsor in a 30% effective tax bracket reduces the after-tax cost of 12% mezzanine debt to approximately 8.4%. Preferred equity distributions are not deductible, so the 14% preferred return costs the full 14% on an after-tax basis. This difference alone can represent tens of thousands of dollars per year on a multi-million dollar capital raise.

Despite these cost advantages, mezzanine debt is not always available. When the senior lender refuses to approve subordinate debt, or when the intercreditor negotiation would delay the closing past a critical deadline, preferred equity may be the only practical path forward. In those situations, the higher cost is the price of getting the deal done.

Intercreditor and Senior Lender Considerations

The intercreditor agreement is one of the most important practical differences between mezzanine debt and preferred equity. Because mezzanine debt is legally classified as a loan, it triggers the subordinate debt restrictions found in virtually every senior loan document. The senior lender has legitimate concerns about a junior lender taking actions (such as foreclosing on the ownership interests) that could disrupt the senior loan or interfere with the senior lender's collateral.

The intercreditor agreement addresses these concerns by establishing ground rules. Key provisions typically include standstill periods (often 60 to 120 days) during which the mezzanine lender cannot exercise remedies after the borrower defaults, giving the senior lender time to assess the situation and pursue its own remedies. The ICA also grants the mezzanine lender cure rights, allowing it to step in and cure defaults on the senior loan to prevent a senior foreclosure that would wipe out the mezzanine position. Buyout provisions give the mezzanine lender the option to purchase the senior loan at par (the outstanding principal balance plus accrued interest and fees), allowing it to take control of the entire debt stack. Notice requirements ensure both lenders are informed of defaults and proposed actions by the other party.

Negotiating these provisions is complex and can take weeks. Each lender has its own form of intercreditor agreement, and the terms are heavily negotiated. Legal costs for all parties (senior lender, mezzanine lender, and borrower) typically run $25,000 to $75,000 in aggregate. Some senior lenders, particularly CMBS servicers, are notoriously difficult to negotiate intercreditor agreements with, and some refuse to allow mezzanine debt altogether.

Preferred equity avoids this entire process. Because preferred equity is structured as an equity investment in the borrowing entity rather than a loan, it does not trigger the subordinate debt restrictions in the senior loan documents. The senior lender's loan agreement typically restricts "additional indebtedness" and "subordinate liens," but a properly structured preferred equity investment is neither. The preferred equity investor joins the ownership structure of the entity, which the senior lender generally views as an internal ownership matter rather than an additional debt obligation. This distinction saves time, reduces closing costs, and eliminates the risk that the senior lender will refuse to cooperate.

However, sponsors should be aware that some sophisticated senior lenders have begun including provisions in their loan documents that require consent for preferred equity investments as well, particularly when the preferred equity has debt-like features (such as a fixed maturity, mandatory redemption, or foreclosure-like remedies). The line between mezzanine debt and preferred equity is not always bright, and lenders are increasingly scrutinizing subordinate capital structures regardless of their label.

Foreclosure and Enforcement Differences

The enforcement mechanisms available to mezzanine lenders and preferred equity investors are fundamentally different, and these differences have significant practical implications for both borrowers and capital providers.

A mezzanine lender's primary remedy upon default is foreclosure on the pledged ownership interests through UCC Article 9. This process involves sending a notice of default, allowing a cure period (typically 30 days, though the intercreditor agreement may impose additional waiting periods), and then conducting a commercially reasonable sale of the pledged interests. The sale can be either public or private, and in many cases the mezzanine lender credit-bids its outstanding loan balance to acquire the interests itself. The entire process, from notice to completed sale, can take as little as 60 to 90 days in most jurisdictions. Once the mezzanine lender acquires the ownership interests, it controls the entity that owns the property and can install new management, refinance the senior loan, or sell the property.

This speed and certainty of enforcement is one of the reasons mezzanine debt commands a lower interest rate than preferred equity. Capital providers willing to accept the mezzanine position know that their recovery mechanism is well-established, legally tested, and relatively swift. The borrower, in turn, faces a clear and credible threat in the event of default, which serves as a strong incentive to keep the mezzanine loan current.

Preferred equity investors do not have foreclosure rights. Their remedies are contractual rather than statutory, meaning they are only as strong as the provisions negotiated in the operating agreement. Typical control provisions include the right to remove the managing member and appoint a replacement (effectively taking over day-to-day management of the property), the right to block all distributions to common equity until preferred returns are current, approval or veto rights over major decisions such as refinancing, new leases, capital expenditures, or asset sales, and the right to force a sale of the property if specified conditions are met (such as a prolonged payment default or breach of financial covenants).

While these provisions can be powerful in practice, they are subject to greater legal uncertainty than UCC foreclosure. Disputes over whether trigger events have occurred, whether the preferred equity investor followed proper procedures, or whether the removal of the managing member was commercially reasonable can lead to litigation. The enforcement process is less standardized and less predictable than a UCC sale, which introduces additional risk for the capital provider and, in some cases, additional leverage for the borrower to negotiate a workout rather than face a hostile takeover of management.

Tax Implications for Borrowers

The tax treatment of mezzanine debt versus preferred equity is a significant factor in the total cost analysis and can influence which structure a sponsor selects, particularly on larger transactions where the dollar impact is substantial.

Mezzanine interest payments are treated as interest expense for federal income tax purposes. Subject to the limitations imposed by Internal Revenue Code Section 163(j), which caps the deduction for business interest expense at 30% of adjusted taxable income for certain businesses, mezzanine interest is generally deductible by the borrowing entity. This deduction reduces the entity's taxable income and, by extension, the tax liability of the partners or members who receive pass-through income from the entity. The practical effect is that the after-tax cost of mezzanine debt is lower than the stated interest rate. For a borrower in a combined 30% federal and state tax bracket, a 12% mezzanine interest rate has an effective after-tax cost of approximately 8.4%.

Preferred equity distributions are treated as allocations of profit or returns of capital to an equity investor. They are not interest expense and are not deductible by the entity. The borrowing entity pays the preferred return out of its after-tax income (or from cash flow that would otherwise be distributed to common equity holders). For the same borrower in a 30% tax bracket, a 12% preferred equity return costs the full 12% after tax, compared to 8.4% for an equivalent mezzanine rate. On a $2 million capital raise, this difference amounts to roughly $72,000 per year in additional after-tax cost for preferred equity.

There are additional tax nuances to consider. Mezzanine debt does not create a tax basis allocation issue because it is treated as debt of the entity, and the interest deduction flows through to all partners or members in proportion to their ownership. Preferred equity, by contrast, creates a new equity interest in the entity, which requires careful allocation of income, losses, and tax credits among the preferred and common equity holders according to the partnership or operating agreement. This allocation can be complex and requires experienced tax counsel to structure properly, adding to the transaction's professional costs.

Borrowers should also be aware that the IRS and state taxing authorities may recharacterize preferred equity as debt (or mezzanine debt as equity) if the economic substance of the arrangement does not match its legal form. Preferred equity with a fixed maturity, mandatory redemption, and no real equity risk may be treated as disguised debt for tax purposes, which would change the tax treatment for both the borrower and the capital provider. Similarly, mezzanine debt that participates heavily in profits and has no fixed repayment obligation might be recharacterized as equity. Proper structuring and documentation are essential to achieve the intended tax treatment.

Which Is Better for Different Scenarios

The choice between mezzanine debt and preferred equity depends on the specific characteristics of the deal, the sponsor, and the senior lender. Here are several common scenarios and the structure that typically fits best in each.

Large institutional deal with cooperative senior lender. On a $50 million or $100 million acquisition with a senior lender that regularly works with mezzanine providers, mezzanine debt is usually the preferred choice. The intercreditor agreement process, while time-consuming, is well-understood by institutional participants. The lower cost of mezzanine capital (both pre-tax and after-tax) provides a meaningful advantage at scale, and the senior lender's willingness to cooperate eliminates the primary obstacle to using mezz. Institutional mezzanine lenders can also provide larger commitments, often $5 million to $25 million or more, with standardized terms that facilitate quick execution.

Smaller sponsor with a CMBS or agency senior loan. CMBS loan documents and Freddie Mac or Fannie Mae loan agreements often contain strict prohibitions on subordinate debt and require lengthy approval processes for any additional financing. In these situations, preferred equity is frequently the only viable option. The preferred equity investment does not trigger the subordinate debt restrictions, and the sponsor avoids a potentially months-long negotiation with a CMBS special servicer that may ultimately refuse the request. The higher cost of preferred equity is offset by the certainty of execution and the ability to close the deal on schedule.

Construction or heavy value-add project. For ground-up construction or major renovation projects, preferred equity is often a natural fit because the risk profile of the deal is inherently equity-like. The property may not generate any cash flow during the construction period, making regular interest payments on mezzanine debt difficult without a funded interest reserve. Preferred equity can be structured with a fully accruing return during the construction phase, converting to current-pay once the property stabilizes. Additionally, preferred equity providers on construction deals often want upside participation to compensate for the higher risk, and the equity structure accommodates this naturally.

Sponsor seeking maximum leverage. When the sponsor needs to minimize equity contribution, the combined use of senior debt plus mezzanine can sometimes achieve higher total leverage than senior debt plus preferred equity. Because mezzanine is treated as debt, some structures allow combined loan-to-cost ratios of 85% to 90% (for example, 70% senior debt plus 15% to 20% mezzanine). Preferred equity, while providing similar capital, may be limited in its contribution relative to total capitalization by the senior lender's requirements around minimum sponsor equity. The distinction is subtle but can mean the difference between a 10% and a 15% equity requirement for the sponsor.

Time-sensitive acquisition. When the sponsor faces a hard closing deadline and cannot afford the four to eight weeks required to negotiate an intercreditor agreement, preferred equity offers a faster path to closing. Preferred equity documentation involves negotiating the operating agreement amendments between the sponsor and the capital provider, without requiring the senior lender's involvement. This streamlined process can shave weeks off the closing timeline.

Combined LTV/LTC Impact

Adding either mezzanine debt or preferred equity to the capital stack increases the sponsor's total leverage, reducing the amount of common equity required and amplifying both potential returns and potential losses. Understanding how each option affects total leverage ratios is critical for structuring a deal that meets the sponsor's return targets while staying within acceptable risk parameters.

In a standard capital stack with only senior debt and common equity, the loan-to-cost (LTC) ratio reflects the senior lender's contribution. For a $10 million acquisition with a 70% LTC senior loan, the sponsor needs $3 million in equity. Adding $1.5 million in mezzanine debt raises the total debt (senior plus mezz) to $8.5 million, or 85% LTC. The sponsor's equity requirement drops to $1.5 million. Adding $1.5 million in preferred equity achieves a similar total capitalization split, but the combined "leverage" is classified differently: 70% debt and 15% preferred equity, with 15% common equity.

This classification distinction matters because senior lenders evaluate leverage differently depending on whether the subordinate capital is debt or equity. Some senior lenders set maximum combined LTV or LTC limits that include mezzanine debt. For example, a senior lender might cap combined LTV (senior plus mezz) at 80%, limiting how much mezzanine the sponsor can layer on top of a 70% LTV senior loan to an additional 10% of value. Preferred equity, because it is classified as equity, may not count against this combined leverage limit, potentially allowing the sponsor to achieve higher total capitalization with less common equity.

Typical combined ratios in the current market are as follows. For senior debt plus mezzanine, combined LTC ratios of 75% to 85% are common, with some aggressive structures reaching 90%. For senior debt plus preferred equity, total capitalization ratios (debt plus pref equity) of 80% to 90% are achievable, because the preferred equity does not trigger the senior lender's debt-related leverage caps. In both cases, the common equity requirement for the sponsor typically falls in the 10% to 25% range, depending on the property type, business plan, and overall risk profile of the transaction.

The leverage amplification works both ways. Higher leverage increases the sponsor's return on equity when the deal performs well, but it also increases the risk of loss. A property that declines in value by 15% wipes out the common equity entirely in an 85% LTC structure, and the mezzanine or preferred equity capital begins to erode. Sponsors should model downside scenarios carefully when adding subordinate capital, ensuring that the property's cash flow can support all layers of the capital stack even under stress conditions such as increased vacancy, lower rental rates, or rising interest expenses.
