HUD 223(f) vs 221(d)(4): Which FHA Multifamily Loan Program Do You Need?

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • 223(f) purpose: Acquisition and refinance of existing multifamily
  • 221(d)(4) purpose: New construction and substantial rehabilitation
  • 223(f) max LTV: Up to 85% (market rate); 90% (affordable)
  • 221(d)(4) max LTV: Up to 83.3% (market rate); 87% (affordable)
  • 223(f) term: Up to 35 years
  • 221(d)(4) term: Up to 40 years plus construction period

Overview of HUD Multifamily Programs

The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) and the Federal Housing Administration (FHA) offer several loan insurance programs designed specifically for multifamily housing. These programs are delivered through a network of approved MAP (Multifamily Accelerated Processing) lenders who originate, underwrite, and service the loans on HUD's behalf. Among all the HUD multifamily programs, two stand out as the most widely used and most important for apartment investors and developers: Section 223(f) for existing properties and Section 221(d)(4) for new construction and substantial rehabilitation.

Both programs share a set of core features that make them uniquely attractive in commercial real estate. HUD-insured loans are fully amortizing (no balloon payment at maturity), non-recourse to the borrower, and carry fixed interest rates for the entire loan term. The loan terms themselves are the longest available in commercial real estate lending: up to 35 years for 223(f) and up to 40 years plus the construction period for 221(d)(4). No conventional lender, bank, or CMBS conduit offers anything close to these terms.

The mechanism that enables these favorable terms is FHA mortgage insurance. The borrower pays a Mortgage Insurance Premium (MIP) to HUD, and in return, FHA insures the loan against default. This government guarantee allows HUD-approved lenders to offer lower interest rates, higher leverage, and longer terms than they could on an uninsured basis. The loans are then securitized through Ginnie Mae mortgage-backed securities, giving lenders access to the deep liquidity of the government securities market.

While both programs share this foundational structure, they serve fundamentally different purposes. Understanding which program applies to your project is the first and most critical decision in the HUD lending process. The wrong choice can cost you months of wasted effort and significant application expenses.

What Is HUD 223(f)?

Section 223(f) of the National Housing Act provides FHA-insured mortgages for the acquisition or refinance of existing multifamily properties with five or more residential units. This is the workhorse program of HUD multifamily lending, accounting for the majority of FHA-insured multifamily loans originated each year. It is the program most apartment investors encounter when they explore HUD financing for the first time.

To qualify for 223(f), the property must be at least three years old and must not require substantial rehabilitation. HUD draws a clear line between limited repairs (allowed under 223(f)) and substantial rehabilitation (which requires the 221(d)(4) program). Under 223(f), the borrower may include repairs in the loan amount up to the greater of $6,500 per unit or 15% of the property's replacement cost, adjusted periodically for inflation. This threshold covers a wide range of improvements: cosmetic unit upgrades, new appliances, roof replacement, HVAC system updates, parking lot resurfacing, exterior painting, and similar deferred maintenance items. It does not cover gut renovations, structural changes, or the addition of new units.

The loan terms under 223(f) are exceptionally favorable. The maximum term is 35 years or 75% of the property's remaining economic life, whichever is less. The interest rate is fixed for the entire term, so the borrower locks in debt service certainty for up to three and a half decades. All 223(f) loans are fully amortizing, meaning there is no balloon payment at maturity. The loan is non-recourse, so the borrower's personal assets are not at risk beyond the collateral property (subject to standard "bad boy" carve-outs for fraud and other misconduct).

Maximum leverage under 223(f) is up to 85% loan-to-value for market-rate properties and up to 90% for affordable housing properties. The minimum debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) is 1.176x for market-rate deals and 1.11x for affordable projects. The annual MIP currently ranges from 0.25% to 0.65% of the outstanding loan balance, depending on the property type, whether the property is affordable housing, and whether it holds green building certification.

The typical 223(f) timeline from application to closing is three to six months, though actual timelines vary based on the HUD regional office workload and the completeness of the application package. This makes 223(f) the faster of the two major HUD programs, and one of the most efficient paths to long-term, fixed-rate multifamily financing available in the market.

What Is HUD 221(d)(4)?

Section 221(d)(4) of the National Housing Act provides FHA-insured mortgages for the new construction and substantial rehabilitation of multifamily properties. This is the only government-insured construction-to-permanent loan program available in the commercial real estate market, and it occupies a unique position in multifamily finance. No other lending program combines construction financing and permanent financing into a single, government-insured package with a 40-year term.

The 221(d)(4) program finances the full development lifecycle. During the construction phase (typically 18 to 24 months), the lender advances funds as construction milestones are completed, with HUD inspectors verifying progress. Once construction is complete and the project receives its final endorsement from HUD, the loan converts automatically to a permanent, fully amortizing mortgage. There is no need to refinance out of the construction loan into a separate permanent loan, which eliminates interest rate risk and refinance uncertainty during a vulnerable period in the project's life.

The maximum loan term is 40 years from the date of final endorsement, plus the construction period. This means the total loan life can exceed 42 years, making it the longest-term financing available for any commercial real estate asset class. The interest rate is fixed before construction begins and remains locked for the entire term, including both the construction and permanent phases. During the permanent phase, the loan is non-recourse.

Maximum leverage under 221(d)(4) is up to 83.3% of replacement cost for market-rate properties and up to 87% for affordable housing projects. The minimum DSCR requirements mirror those of 223(f): 1.176x for market-rate and 1.11x for affordable. Annual MIP ranges from 0.25% to 0.65% during the permanent phase, with a higher rate applied during the construction period to reflect the additional risk.

One significant requirement of the 221(d)(4) program is compliance with Davis-Bacon prevailing wage standards. All construction labor on a 221(d)(4) project must be paid at or above the prevailing wage rates established by the U.S. Department of Labor for the project's geographic area. This adds to construction costs (typically 10% to 20% above non-Davis-Bacon labor costs) and requires ongoing compliance documentation and monitoring.

The 221(d)(4) processing timeline is substantially longer than 223(f). From pre-application to initial endorsement (the closing of the construction loan) typically takes 6 to 12 months or more, depending on the complexity of the project, the HUD office workload, and the quality of the application. The construction period adds another 18 to 24 months. Final endorsement occurs after construction completion, lease-up, and cost certification. The total timeline from project inception to permanent financing is roughly two to three years. Despite this extended timeline, the end result is financing that cannot be replicated by any conventional lender.

Eligibility Requirements

Both 223(f) and 221(d)(4) share certain baseline eligibility criteria, but they diverge in important ways based on the nature of the project.

Shared requirements. Both programs require the property to be multifamily housing with five or more residential units. The borrower must work with an FHA-approved MAP lender. The borrower is required to create a single-asset entity (typically an LLC or limited partnership) to hold the property and the mortgage. A Phase I Environmental Site Assessment is required for all projects. Both programs require a third-party appraisal and market study prepared by HUD-approved professionals.

223(f) specific requirements. The property must be at least three years old with no substantial rehabilitation planned. It should be stabilized or near-stabilized, with occupancy typically at 85% or above at the time of application. Repairs included in the loan must fall within the program's limits (the greater of $6,500 per unit or 15% of replacement cost). A Physical Needs Assessment (PNA) is required to evaluate the property's condition and estimate remaining useful life of major systems. The PNA helps HUD determine the appropriate loan term relative to the property's economic life.

221(d)(4) specific requirements. The project must involve new ground-up construction or substantial rehabilitation of an existing structure. For new construction, the borrower must have site control, zoning approvals, and completed architectural plans and specifications. For substantial rehabilitation, the scope of work must exceed the 223(f) repair thresholds. A detailed construction budget, timeline, and bonding plan are required. The general contractor must be approved by HUD and is typically required to post a performance bond and payment bond. All construction labor must comply with Davis-Bacon prevailing wage standards, which requires certified payroll submissions throughout the construction period.

Cost and Timeline Comparison

The cost and timeline differences between 223(f) and 221(d)(4) are significant and often drive the choice between programs as much as the project type itself.

223(f) costs and timeline. The 223(f) program is the faster and less expensive path to HUD-insured financing. A typical 223(f) transaction closes in three to six months from the date of application. Upfront costs include the MIP initial deposit (typically 0.50% to 1.00% of the loan amount), lender application and origination fees (usually 1% to 2% of the loan amount), and third-party report costs (appraisal, market study, PNA, Phase I environmental, and title/survey). Total closing costs on a 223(f) loan typically run 3% to 5% of the loan amount. The ongoing cost is the annual MIP, which is paid monthly as part of the debt service.

221(d)(4) costs and timeline. The 221(d)(4) program involves a longer and more expensive process. The timeline from pre-application to initial endorsement (construction loan closing) is typically 6 to 12 months or more. The construction period adds another 18 to 24 months before the project reaches final endorsement and converts to permanent financing. Total elapsed time from project inception to stabilized permanent debt is typically two to three years.

Upfront costs are higher for 221(d)(4) due to the additional complexity. Beyond the standard MIP deposit and lender fees, borrowers face costs for detailed architectural plans and specifications, construction bonding, Davis-Bacon compliance monitoring, HUD construction-phase inspections, and a more extensive application package. The Davis-Bacon wage requirement alone can add 10% to 20% to construction labor costs compared to a non-HUD project. Total soft costs and closing costs on a 221(d)(4) deal can run 5% to 8% of the total project cost.

Despite these higher upfront costs and longer timelines, the 221(d)(4) program's long-term economics are compelling. The 40-year, fixed-rate, non-recourse, fully amortizing permanent loan that results from the process is unmatched in commercial real estate. For developers who can plan around the timeline and absorb the upfront costs, the 221(d)(4) program often produces the lowest total cost of capital over the life of the project.

MIP (Mortgage Insurance Premium) Differences

Both 223(f) and 221(d)(4) loans require the borrower to pay MIP to FHA as the cost of the government mortgage insurance. MIP is calculated as a percentage of the outstanding loan balance and is paid monthly along with principal and interest. The MIP rates for both programs are set by HUD and vary based on several factors.

For 223(f) loans, the annual MIP typically ranges from 0.25% to 0.65%. The exact rate depends on the property type (market rate vs. affordable housing), whether the property has received green building certification (such as ENERGY STAR or a comparable standard), and the specific program endorsement. Affordable housing properties and green-certified buildings qualify for the lowest MIP rates, sometimes as low as 0.25% per year.

For 221(d)(4) loans, the permanent-phase MIP rates are similar, ranging from 0.25% to 0.65% based on the same factors. However, during the construction period, a higher MIP rate applies to reflect the additional risk HUD assumes on a project that has not yet been built and stabilized. The construction-phase MIP is typically higher than the permanent-phase rate and is charged on the cumulative amount of loan funds that have been disbursed.

On a practical level, MIP adds a meaningful but manageable cost to HUD financing. On a $10 million loan with a 0.45% annual MIP rate, the cost is $45,000 per year, or approximately $3,750 per month, added to the borrower's debt service. While this is an additional expense that conventional loans do not carry, it is the direct cost of the FHA insurance that enables the low fixed rates, high leverage, long terms, and non-recourse structure that make HUD loans so attractive. For most borrowers, the MIP is more than offset by the interest rate savings compared to conventional alternatives.

Green building certification deserves special attention in the MIP calculation. HUD offers reduced MIP rates for properties that meet certain energy efficiency and sustainability standards. For a large multifamily project, the MIP reduction can save tens of thousands of dollars annually over the life of the loan. Borrowers who are planning renovations (under 223(f)) or new construction (under 221(d)(4)) should consider incorporating green building features to capture these savings.

The Substantial Rehabilitation Threshold

The single most important distinction between 223(f) and 221(d)(4) is the level of renovation involved in the project. HUD draws a bright line between limited repairs (eligible for 223(f)) and substantial rehabilitation (requiring 221(d)(4)), and understanding exactly where that line falls is essential for choosing the right program.

Under 223(f), the borrower may include repairs in the loan amount up to the greater of $6,500 per unit or 15% of the property's estimated replacement cost. These figures are adjusted periodically by HUD, so borrowers should confirm the current thresholds with their MAP lender. For a 100-unit property with a replacement cost of $15 million, the 15% threshold would be $2.25 million (or $22,500 per unit), which is well above the per-unit floor. For a smaller property with lower replacement cost, the $6,500 per-unit floor may be the controlling limit.

Renovations that fall within these limits can include a broad range of improvements: kitchen and bathroom upgrades, flooring replacement, appliance installation, roof repairs or replacement, HVAC system updates, exterior painting and siding, parking lot and landscaping improvements, window replacement, and other deferred maintenance items. The key constraint is that the work must not involve structural changes, the addition of new units, or a scope that requires tenants to vacate the property for an extended period.

If the renovation scope exceeds the 223(f) repair limits, the project crosses into substantial rehabilitation territory and must be financed under the 221(d)(4) program. Examples of substantial rehabilitation include gut renovations of existing buildings, conversion of non-residential buildings (such as hotels, offices, or warehouses) to residential apartments, adding new floors or wings to an existing structure, replacing major structural systems (foundations, load-bearing walls), and any renovation that requires the property to be partially or fully vacant during the construction period.

This threshold is not discretionary. If your renovation budget exceeds the limits, you cannot use 223(f) regardless of other circumstances. Conversely, if your repairs fall within the limits, you should not use 221(d)(4), as its longer timeline, higher costs, and Davis-Bacon requirements would add unnecessary expense and complexity. Getting this classification right at the outset is critical, and it is one of the first questions any experienced MAP lender will help you answer.

When to Use Each Program

Choosing between 223(f) and 221(d)(4) ultimately comes down to matching the program to the project. Here is a clear breakdown of when each program is the right fit.

Use HUD 223(f) when:

  • Acquiring an existing stabilized apartment complex. If you are purchasing a multifamily property that is at least three years old, has stable occupancy, and does not need major renovation, 223(f) provides the best permanent financing available. The combination of a 35-year term, fixed rate, full amortization, and non-recourse structure is unmatched for acquisition financing.
  • Refinancing an existing multifamily loan. If you currently hold a multifamily property with a shorter-term loan (such as a 5-year bank loan, bridge loan, or CMBS loan approaching maturity), 223(f) allows you to refinance into long-term, fixed-rate HUD debt. This is especially attractive for borrowers facing the 2026 maturity wall who want to lock in permanent financing and eliminate refinance risk.
  • Light renovation projects under the repair caps. If your property needs cosmetic upgrades, deferred maintenance, or unit refreshes that fall within the 223(f) repair limits, you can finance those improvements as part of the 223(f) loan. This allows you to improve the property without the added time and expense of the 221(d)(4) process.

Use HUD 221(d)(4) when:

  • Building a new apartment community from the ground up. If you are developing a new multifamily project on a vacant or cleared site, 221(d)(4) is the only government-insured construction-to-permanent loan available. The ability to lock in a fixed rate before construction begins and convert seamlessly to a 40-year permanent loan eliminates the refinance risk that exists with conventional construction lending.
  • Converting a non-residential building to apartments. Adaptive reuse projects (converting a hotel, office building, warehouse, or other commercial structure to multifamily residential) are classified as substantial rehabilitation and require the 221(d)(4) program.
  • Gut renovation that exceeds 223(f) repair limits. If your planned renovation scope exceeds the greater of $6,500 per unit or 15% of replacement cost, the project must go through 221(d)(4). This includes major structural work, full interior gut renovations, adding units, and any work requiring extended vacancy.
