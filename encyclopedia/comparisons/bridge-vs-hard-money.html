Bridge Loan vs Hard Money Loan: Key Differences for Commercial Borrowers

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Bridge rates: 7.5% to 10% (institutional)
  • Hard money rates: 10% to 15%+
  • Bridge LTV: Up to 75% to 80%
  • Hard money LTV: Up to 65% to 70%
  • Bridge lenders: Debt funds, institutional platforms
  • Hard money lenders: Private individuals, small lending companies

What Is a Commercial Bridge Loan?

A commercial bridge loan is a short-term financing tool designed for transitional commercial real estate properties. These are properties that do not yet qualify for permanent, long-term debt because they are being acquired, renovated, leased up, or repositioned. Bridge loans "bridge" the gap between the current state of the property and the point at which it can secure stable, lower-cost permanent financing.

Bridge loans are funded by institutional capital sources: debt funds, banks, insurance company affiliates, and institutional lending platforms. Because these lenders manage pooled capital from large investors, they operate under regulatory frameworks and internal risk guidelines that benefit borrowers through lower rates, higher leverage, and longer terms compared to private capital alternatives.

Underwriting for a bridge loan considers both the property's current income and its projected stabilized value. The lender evaluates the borrower's business plan in detail, including planned renovations, target rents, lease-up timeline, and projected net operating income (NOI) at stabilization. This dual focus allows bridge lenders to extend financing on properties that would not qualify for permanent debt based on current cash flow alone.

The typical bridge loan borrower is an experienced operator with a clear, executable business plan. Institutional bridge lenders want a track record of similar projects, sufficient liquidity reserves, and a credit profile that demonstrates financial responsibility (minimum 650 credit score, with many lenders preferring 680+). Bridge loan terms range from 12 to 36 months, rates fall between 7.5% and 10%, and origination fees range from 1 to 2 points. Most are structured as interest-only to preserve cash flow during the improvement period.

What Is a Hard Money Loan?

A hard money loan is a short-term, asset-based loan funded by private capital sources. The term "hard money" refers to the hard asset (the real property) that serves as the primary basis for the lending decision. Unlike institutional bridge loans, hard money underwriting focuses primarily on the collateral value of the real estate rather than evaluating the borrower, the business plan, and the property in combination.

Hard money lenders include private individuals, family offices, small lending companies, and private mortgage funds. These lenders use their own capital rather than institutional pools, which gives them significantly more flexibility. A hard money lender can say yes to a deal that an institutional platform would decline because the decision-making process involves fewer layers of approval and fewer regulatory constraints.

The primary advantages of hard money are speed and flexibility. Hard money loans can close in as few as 5 to 14 business days because underwriting is simpler and asset-focused. Borrower income verification, tax return analysis, and detailed business plan review are either minimal or absent. This makes hard money the go-to option when time is the most critical factor in a deal.

The trade-off is cost. Hard money interest rates typically range from 10% to 15% or higher, with origination fees of 2 to 5 points. Terms are shorter (6 to 18 months), maximum leverage is lower (65% to 70% LTV), and loans are almost always full recourse. The higher cost reflects the higher risk tolerance of the lenders and the speed and certainty they provide. For borrowers who understand the cost structure and use hard money strategically, it is a powerful tool rather than a last resort.

Underwriting Differences: Income-Based vs. Asset-Based

The most fundamental difference between bridge loans and hard money loans is the underwriting approach. This single distinction drives nearly every other difference in qualification requirements, pricing, and borrower experience.

Bridge lenders use an income-based (or income-plus-asset) underwriting model. They analyze projected NOI at stabilization, the feasibility of the borrower's renovation or lease-up plan, the borrower's track record with similar projects, and overall financial strength. A typical bridge loan application requires rent rolls, trailing 12-month operating statements, a detailed renovation budget, a lease-up pro forma, borrower financial statements, and a personal credit report. The lender's underwriting team models cash flows, stress-tests assumptions, and presents the deal to a credit committee for approval. This process takes 2 to 4 weeks but produces better pricing because the lender has higher confidence in the deal's outcome.

Hard money lenders use an asset-based underwriting model. Their primary question is simple: if the borrower defaults, can we recover our capital by selling the property? The lender determines the property's as-is market value and lends at a conservative LTV (typically 65% to 70%). The equity cushion is the lender's primary protection against loss. Many hard money lenders do not require tax returns, bank statements, or detailed financial disclosures, and some do not pull credit at all. This stripped-down process allows them to move quickly, but the higher risk is reflected in higher rates and fees.

This fundamental underwriting difference determines who qualifies for each product. Borrowers with strong track records, good credit, and well-documented business plans are well served by institutional bridge lending. Borrowers who lack experience, have credit challenges, or simply need to close faster than institutional timelines allow will find hard money a viable path forward, provided they can absorb the higher cost.

When to Use a Bridge Loan

Bridge loans are the right tool when your deal matches the institutional lending profile and you have the time and documentation to go through a more thorough underwriting process. The lower cost makes bridge financing the preferred option whenever it is available to you.

Value-add acquisitions with a clear business plan. If you are acquiring a commercial property with a defined renovation scope, realistic rent projections, and a timeline to stabilization, institutional bridge lenders will compete for your deal. The combination of a solid plan and an experienced borrower is exactly what bridge lenders are designed to finance.

Lease-up scenarios. Properties that are newly constructed or recently renovated but not yet fully leased often need 12 to 24 months of additional time before they can qualify for permanent financing. A bridge loan provides that runway at a reasonable cost while the property reaches stabilized occupancy.

Borrowers with strong track records. If you have completed multiple similar projects and can demonstrate consistent execution, bridge lenders view you as a lower-risk borrower. Your experience directly translates into better pricing and higher leverage. First-time borrowers or those without relevant experience will find it more difficult to access institutional bridge capital.

Deals that need more time than hard money allows. Hard money terms are typically 6 to 18 months. If your business plan requires 24 to 36 months to execute (a larger renovation, a phased lease-up, or a market repositioning), bridge financing provides the longer timeline without the cost and disruption of refinancing midway through the project.

Larger deals ($2M+). Institutional bridge lenders generally have minimum loan amounts of $1 million to $2 million, and they are most competitive on deals of $5 million and above. If your deal size falls within the institutional range, bridge lending is almost always cheaper and more efficient than hard money.

When Hard Money Is the Right Choice

Hard money fills a critical gap in the capital markets. It serves borrowers and deals that do not fit neatly into institutional lending boxes, and it does so with speed and certainty that institutional platforms cannot match.

Time-critical deals. In competitive acquisition environments, the ability to close in 7 to 14 days can be the difference between winning and losing a deal. Sellers often prefer buyers who can demonstrate certainty of execution, and a hard money pre-approval letter or proof of funds carries weight. If the seller's timeline does not accommodate a 3- to 4-week institutional closing process, hard money is the tool that keeps you in the deal.

Borrowers with credit issues. Life events such as a prior bankruptcy, foreclosure, tax lien, or low credit score can disqualify a borrower from institutional bridge programs regardless of the property's quality. Hard money lenders look past credit history and focus on the equity in the deal. If you have 30% to 35% equity and a viable property, many hard money lenders will fund the loan.

Unusual properties that institutions will not touch. Institutional bridge lenders have property type preferences and restrictions. A vacant former church being converted to apartments, a rural industrial property, or a mixed-use building with a non-conforming use may not fit institutional guidelines. Hard money lenders evaluate these deals on their individual merits and the underlying real estate value.

Fix-and-flip scenarios. Short-term renovation projects on smaller commercial properties or residential investment properties are a core market for hard money lenders. The loan term aligns with the renovation timeline (6 to 12 months), and the exit strategy (sell the renovated property) is straightforward. Many hard money lenders specialize in this niche and have streamlined processes for repeat borrowers.

Situations where speed trumps cost. Sometimes the economics of a deal make the hard money premium irrelevant. If you can acquire a property at a 20% discount to market value because you can close in 10 days, the extra 3 to 5 points in origination fees and the higher interest rate are a small price to pay relative to the profit opportunity. Sophisticated investors calculate the total cost of hard money against the opportunity cost of missing the deal, and the math often favors speed.

Rate and Fee Comparison

Understanding the total cost difference requires looking beyond the interest rate to include origination fees, exit fees, and other closing costs.

Bridge loan costs. Rates of 7.5% to 10%, origination fees of 1 to 2 points, and minimal or no exit fees. Some bridge lenders charge a small extension fee (0.25 to 0.50 points) if the borrower needs additional time beyond the initial term.

Hard money costs. Rates of 10% to 15%+, origination fees of 2 to 5 points, and possible exit fees of 1 to 2 points. Closing costs may also be higher because some hard money lenders require the borrower to pay the lender's legal counsel.

That difference represents 6.5% of the loan amount and could be the difference between a profitable deal and a marginal one. For borrowers who qualify for institutional bridge financing, the cost savings are substantial. For borrowers who need the speed or flexibility of hard money, the premium must be factored into the deal analysis from the outset.

Borrower Qualification Differences

The qualification requirements for bridge loans and hard money loans reflect their different underwriting approaches. Understanding what each lender expects helps you determine which product to pursue and how to prepare your application.

Bridge loan qualifications. Institutional bridge lenders evaluate the borrower almost as carefully as the property. Standard requirements include a minimum credit score of 650 (many prefer 680+), documented experience with similar projects, a detailed business plan, personal financial statements showing adequate net worth, and liquidity reserves sufficient to cover 6 to 12 months of debt service plus renovation contingency funds.

Hard money qualifications. Hard money lenders focus on the deal rather than the borrower. Many do not set a minimum credit score, and some do not pull credit at all. Experience is helpful but rarely required. The primary qualification is equity: if you have 30% to 35% equity in the property, most hard money lenders will consider the deal. Documentation requirements are minimal compared to bridge lending.

This qualification gap creates a clear pathway. Borrowers early in their investing career or rebuilding after financial setbacks often start with hard money, build a track record, and then graduate to institutional bridge lending for larger, more cost-efficient financing.

Exit Strategy Requirements

Both bridge loans and hard money loans are short-term financing tools, and both require a clear exit strategy. No lender in either category wants to hold a matured loan with an uncertain repayment path. The exit plan is a core component of underwriting for both product types, though the level of scrutiny differs.

Bridge loan exit strategies. The most common exit for a bridge loan is refinancing into permanent debt. Once the property is stabilized, the borrower applies for a long-term mortgage from a bank, CMBS lender, life insurance company, or agency lender (Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac for multifamily). Bridge lenders underwrite the feasibility of this exit by projecting stabilized NOI and confirming the property will meet permanent lender requirements for DSCR, LTV, and occupancy. Sale is the secondary exit path.

Hard money exit strategies. Hard money borrowers have a broader range of exit paths: selling after renovation (fix-and-flip), refinancing into a bridge loan (moving up the capital stack), or refinancing directly into permanent debt. Hard money lenders are generally less prescriptive about the specific exit, provided the borrower can articulate a realistic plan and the property value supports repayment.

The "capital stack" progression is worth highlighting. A borrower acquires a distressed property with hard money for speed, completes initial renovations, refinances into a lower-cost bridge loan to complete stabilization, and then exits into permanent financing. Each step reduces the cost of capital as the property's risk profile improves. This approach demonstrates that bridge and hard money are not competing products but complementary tools used at different stages of a property's lifecycle.
