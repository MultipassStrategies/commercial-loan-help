CMBS vs Agency Loans: Comparing Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and CMBS for Multifamily

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • CMBS property types: All commercial real estate
  • Agency property types: Multifamily only (5+ units)
  • CMBS LTV: Up to 75%
  • Agency LTV: Up to 80%
  • CMBS prepayment: Defeasance or yield maintenance
  • Agency prepayment: Yield maintenance, defeasance, or step-down

What Are CMBS Loans?

CMBS stands for commercial mortgage-backed securities. These are commercial real estate loans originated by conduit lenders, pooled together into a trust, and then sold to bond investors on the secondary market. The securitization process allows lenders to recycle capital and originate new loans continuously, which keeps CMBS lending highly active across market cycles. For borrowers, CMBS loans offer non-recourse financing (with standard carveouts for fraud, misrepresentation, and other "bad boy" acts) on stabilized, income-producing commercial properties.

CMBS loans are available for virtually all commercial property types, including office buildings, retail centers, industrial warehouses, hotels, mixed-use properties, and multifamily apartment complexes. This broad eligibility is one of the defining characteristics that separates CMBS from agency lending. If you own or are acquiring any type of stabilized commercial property, CMBS is a potential financing option.

Underwriting for CMBS loans focuses primarily on the property's cash flow rather than the borrower's personal financial strength. The debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) is the central metric: lenders want to see that the property's net operating income (NOI) comfortably exceeds the annual debt service obligation, typically requiring a minimum DSCR of 1.25x. Loan-to-value ratios generally cap at 75%, and loan terms are most commonly 5, 7, or 10 years with 25 to 30 years of amortization. Interest rates are based on Treasury or swap rate spreads and are typically fixed for the full loan term.

Prepayment on CMBS loans is handled through defeasance or yield maintenance. Defeasance requires the borrower to purchase a portfolio of U.S. Treasury securities that replicate the remaining loan payment stream, effectively substituting government bonds for the real estate collateral. Yield maintenance compensates the lender for lost interest income based on the difference between the loan rate and current Treasury yields. Neither option is inexpensive, and CMBS loans do not offer step-down (declining percentage) prepayment schedules. If the borrower defaults, loan workout and modification decisions are handled by a special servicer rather than the original lender, which can make the process less predictable.

What Are Agency Loans?

Agency loans are multifamily mortgages originated through programs backed by two government-sponsored enterprises (GSEs): Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. Fannie Mae's multifamily program operates through its Delegated Underwriting and Servicing (DUS) model, where approved lender partners originate and underwrite loans on Fannie Mae's behalf. Freddie Mac's multifamily program, branded as Optigo, works similarly through a network of approved Seller/Servicers. Both programs provide liquidity to the multifamily housing market by purchasing loans from originators and either holding them in portfolio or securitizing them.

The most important distinction about agency loans is their property type restriction. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac multifamily programs are available exclusively for residential rental properties with five or more units. This includes conventional apartments, affordable housing, seniors housing, student housing, and manufactured housing communities. If you own office space, retail, industrial, or hotel properties, agency financing is not an option.

For qualifying multifamily properties, agency loans generally offer the lowest fixed interest rates available in commercial real estate lending. The GSE backing provides an implicit (and in certain structures, explicit) government guarantee that allows agency lenders to price loans more aggressively than CMBS conduits or balance sheet lenders. Rates are typically 25 to 75 basis points tighter than comparable CMBS execution, and this spread advantage has been consistent across most market conditions.

Agency loans are non-recourse with standard carveouts, similar to CMBS. Both Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac offer supplemental loan programs that allow borrowers to place additional debt behind the first mortgage (typically available 12 months after the original loan closes) without refinancing and without triggering prepayment penalties. This is a significant advantage for borrowers who want to access equity from property appreciation or improvements. Additionally, both agencies offer incentive programs for green and affordable housing: Fannie Mae's Green Rewards program and Freddie Mac's Green Up program provide interest rate reductions (typically 10 to 25 basis points) for properties that meet energy and water efficiency standards or commit to sustainability improvements.

Property Type Eligibility

Property type eligibility is the most fundamental difference between CMBS and agency loans, and it is the first question every borrower should answer when evaluating these two financing paths. Agency loans through Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are restricted to multifamily residential properties with five or more units. CMBS loans, by contrast, are available for the full spectrum of commercial real estate asset classes.

If you own or are acquiring an office building, retail shopping center, industrial warehouse, hotel, self-storage facility, or any other non-residential commercial property, CMBS is the securitized financing option available to you. Agency programs simply do not apply. There is no exception or workaround for this restriction; it is built into the GSEs' congressional charter and regulatory framework.

For multifamily property owners, however, the choice opens up. Both CMBS and agency lenders will finance apartment buildings, and this is where the comparison becomes meaningful. A 200-unit apartment complex in a major metro area could be financed through either channel, and the decision comes down to rate, terms, flexibility, and the borrower's specific business plan. In nearly all cases, the agency execution will offer more favorable terms for multifamily, but there are situations (discussed below) where CMBS may be preferable even for apartments.

Mixed-use properties present a nuanced case. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac will finance mixed-use buildings where the residential component represents the majority of the property's square footage and income (typically 80% or more residential). If the commercial component exceeds that threshold, the property falls outside agency eligibility and CMBS becomes the primary securitized option.

Rate and Term Differences

Agency loans typically offer the lowest fixed interest rates available in commercial real estate lending. This pricing advantage exists because of the government-sponsored enterprise backing behind Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. Bond investors purchasing agency-backed mortgage securities accept lower yields than they would demand for CMBS bonds, because the GSE guarantee reduces credit risk. That lower cost of capital flows through to borrowers in the form of tighter spreads and lower all-in rates.

In the current market, agency rates for well-qualified multifamily deals generally range from 5.25% to 6.75%, while CMBS rates on comparable multifamily properties run approximately 5.75% to 7.50%. The spread between the two varies based on deal quality, market conditions, and the specific lender, but a 25 to 75 basis point advantage for agency is typical. On a $10 million loan, a 50 basis point rate difference translates to $50,000 per year in interest savings, or $500,000 over a 10-year term. That is a significant cost differential that directly impacts cash flow and investor returns.

Both CMBS and agency loans offer standard terms of 5, 7, and 10 years. However, agency programs provide additional term options that CMBS does not. Fannie Mae DUS offers 12-year and 15-year terms, and Freddie Mac Optigo offers 15-year terms. For borrowers who want a longer rate lock and amortization period, these extended terms can be valuable. Certain Fannie Mae programs also offer terms exceeding 15 years, including options that extend to 30 years or more for specific property types such as affordable housing.

Rate lock timing also differs between the two programs. Agency lenders typically offer rate locks 30 to 180 days before closing, giving borrowers the ability to secure a rate well in advance and protect against market movement during the due diligence and closing process. CMBS rate locks, by contrast, generally occur at or very near closing, which exposes the borrower to rate volatility throughout the underwriting period. For transactions with extended timelines (such as new construction takeouts or complex acquisitions), the agency rate lock advantage can be worth tens of thousands of dollars in risk reduction.

Prepayment Structure Differences

Prepayment structure is one of the most practically important differences between CMBS and agency loans. The prepayment terms embedded in your loan agreement determine how much it will cost to sell, refinance, or pay off the mortgage before the maturity date. Getting this right at origination can save (or cost) hundreds of thousands of dollars down the road.

CMBS loans offer two prepayment options: defeasance and yield maintenance. Defeasance is the more common of the two in recent CMBS issuances. It requires the borrower to hire a defeasance consultant, purchase a portfolio of U.S. Treasury securities that matches the remaining loan payment schedule, and substitute those securities for the real estate collateral. The process is complex, takes 30 to 60 days, and involves legal, consulting, and transaction fees that typically range from $50,000 to $150,000 or more depending on loan size and remaining term. Yield maintenance is a mathematical formula that compensates the lender for the present value of lost interest income, calculated as the difference between the loan rate and the current Treasury yield multiplied by the remaining term. In a declining rate environment, yield maintenance can be extremely expensive. CMBS loans do not offer step-down prepayment schedules.

Agency loans provide more flexibility. Fannie Mae DUS loans are most commonly structured with yield maintenance for the majority of the term, followed by a brief open period (typically the last 90 days before maturity) during which no penalty applies. However, Fannie Mae also offers declining step-down prepayment schedules on certain products. Freddie Mac Optigo offers the widest range of prepayment options among the three programs: yield maintenance, defeasance, and declining step-down schedules (such as a 5-4-3-2-1 percentage penalty that declines each year). The step-down option is particularly valuable because it gives borrowers predictable, known prepayment costs that decrease over time, making it easier to model exit scenarios and disposition timelines.

For borrowers who anticipate selling or refinancing before maturity, the step-down prepayment option available through agency programs is a meaningful advantage. Rather than hiring consultants and purchasing Treasury portfolios (defeasance) or calculating complex present-value formulas (yield maintenance), the step-down borrower simply pays a known percentage of the outstanding balance. This certainty and simplicity has real value in transaction planning.

Flexibility During the Loan Term

Beyond the initial loan terms, agency and CMBS programs differ significantly in the flexibility they offer borrowers during the life of the loan. These mid-term options can have a major impact on your ability to execute your business plan, access equity, and manage your portfolio efficiently.

Supplemental loans. Both Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac offer supplemental loan programs that allow borrowers to place additional debt behind the existing first mortgage. Supplemental loans are typically available 12 months after the original loan closes, and they allow the borrower to tap into increased property value (from market appreciation, rent growth, or capital improvements) without refinancing the first mortgage and without triggering prepayment penalties. This is a powerful tool for multifamily investors who want to pull equity out of a performing asset while keeping their favorable first mortgage terms intact. CMBS loans rarely permit supplemental financing because the securitized trust structure and pooling and servicing agreement make it difficult to subordinate additional debt behind the first mortgage.

Loan assumptions. Both CMBS and agency loans are assumable, meaning a property buyer can take over the existing loan rather than obtaining new financing. This feature is particularly valuable in a rising rate environment, where assuming a below-market rate loan can be worth significantly more than securing a new loan at current rates. However, the assumption process differs considerably. Agency assumptions are relatively straightforward: the buyer applies to the servicer, undergoes credit review, and pays an assumption fee (typically 0.5% to 1.0% of the outstanding balance). The process usually takes 45 to 60 days. CMBS assumptions require approval from the special servicer, involve more extensive documentation, carry higher fees (often 1% or more), and can take 60 to 90 days or longer. While both are achievable, agency assumptions are simpler and more predictable.

Property releases. For borrowers with portfolio-level agency loans (multiple properties under a single loan), Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac offer property release provisions that allow individual assets to be sold and released from the collateral pool without triggering a full prepayment event. CMBS portfolio loans are less common, and releasing individual properties from a CMBS trust is significantly more complex, often requiring special servicer approval and potentially a partial defeasance.

Loan modifications. If market conditions change or the property's performance shifts, agency servicers generally have more authority and flexibility to work with borrowers on modifications, rate reductions, or term extensions. CMBS modifications must go through the special servicer, which operates under strict pooling and servicing agreement guidelines and may have different economic incentives than the borrower. This structural difference means that agency borrowers typically experience a more responsive and collaborative servicing relationship throughout the loan term.

When CMBS Wins vs. When Agency Wins

Understanding where each program has an advantage helps you match the right financing to your specific situation. Neither CMBS nor agency is universally superior; the best choice depends on property type, business plan, and borrower priorities.

CMBS wins when:

  • The property is not multifamily. If you own office, retail, industrial, hotel, self-storage, or any other non-residential commercial property, CMBS is the securitized financing option. Agency is not available.
  • Higher leverage is needed on certain property types. For non-multifamily assets, CMBS may offer higher leverage than bank or life company alternatives, with LTVs up to 75% on stabilized properties where balance sheet lenders may cap at 65% to 70%.
  • Less borrower scrutiny is preferred. CMBS underwriting is heavily property-focused. The conduit lender cares primarily about the property's cash flow, DSCR, and market position. Borrowers with less operating experience, lower net worth, or less conventional backgrounds may find CMBS more accessible than agency programs, which evaluate the borrower's track record and financial strength more closely.
  • The multifamily property has characteristics that challenge agency guidelines. Certain multifamily properties, such as those with significant commercial space, non-standard configurations, or borrower structures that do not align with GSE requirements, may be better suited for CMBS even though they technically contain residential units.

Agency wins when:

  • The property is a standard multifamily asset. For conventional apartment buildings, affordable housing, seniors housing, student housing, and manufactured housing communities, agency programs offer the best combination of rate, terms, and flexibility available in the market.
  • Lowest possible rate is the priority. Agency's 25 to 75 basis point rate advantage over CMBS translates to meaningful interest savings over the loan term, especially on larger loans.
  • Prepayment flexibility matters. The availability of step-down prepayment schedules, particularly through Freddie Mac Optigo, gives agency borrowers more options and lower costs when exiting a loan early.
  • Supplemental financing is part of the strategy. Borrowers who plan to access equity through supplemental loans (rather than refinancing) should strongly favor agency, where supplemental programs are well-established and widely used.
  • Green or affordable housing incentives apply. Fannie Mae Green Rewards and Freddie Mac Green Up programs offer rate reductions of 10 to 25 basis points for properties meeting sustainability standards. There is no CMBS equivalent to these incentive programs.
  • Early rate lock is valuable. Agency rate locks of 30 to 180 days before closing protect borrowers from market volatility during the transaction timeline, reducing execution risk compared to CMBS rate locks that occur at or near closing.

For multifamily borrowers who qualify for both programs, the decision often comes down to a simple calculus: agency is almost always the better execution on rate and flexibility, while CMBS serves as a strong alternative when agency guidelines are too restrictive for a particular deal's characteristics. Many experienced multifamily operators use agency financing as their primary tool and turn to CMBS only when a specific property or borrower situation falls outside agency parameters.
