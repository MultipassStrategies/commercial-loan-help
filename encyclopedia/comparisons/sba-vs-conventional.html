SBA Loan vs Conventional Commercial Mortgage: Which Is the Better Fit?

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Down payment: SBA: 10-15%; Conventional: 20-30%
  • Rate comparison: SBA 7(a): Prime + 0-2.75%; Conventional: Treasury + 1.5-3.0%
  • Term comparison: SBA: up to 25 years; Conventional: 5-10 year terms (25-30yr amortization)
  • Size limits: SBA 7(a): $5M max; Conventional: no cap
  • Eligibility: SBA: owner-occupied businesses only; Conventional: investors and owner-occupants
  • Time to close: SBA: 60-90 days; Conventional: 30-60 days

Overview of SBA vs Conventional Lending

SBA loans and conventional commercial mortgages represent two fundamentally different approaches to financing commercial real estate. One is government-backed, designed to help small businesses access capital they might not otherwise qualify for. The other is market-based, driven by private-sector underwriting standards and lender risk appetite. Understanding the core philosophy behind each program is the first step toward choosing the right financing for your deal.

The U.S. Small Business Administration does not lend money directly. Instead, it guarantees a portion of each loan (typically 75% to 85%) made by an approved lender, which dramatically reduces the lender's risk. This guarantee is what allows SBA lenders to offer lower down payments, longer repayment terms, and approval for borrowers who might not meet conventional underwriting thresholds. The trade-off is a more involved application process, stricter eligibility requirements, and longer timelines to close.

Conventional commercial mortgages, by contrast, are issued entirely at the lender's discretion with no government involvement. The bank or credit union holds the full risk of the loan on its balance sheet (or sells it into the secondary market), and the underwriting reflects that risk exposure. Conventional lenders require higher down payments and stronger borrower profiles, but they offer faster closings, fewer restrictions on property use, and the ability to finance deals of virtually any size.

The ideal borrower profile for each program is distinct. SBA financing tends to be the strongest fit for first-time business owners purchasing their own commercial space, small businesses with limited capital for a down payment, and entrepreneurs whose credit or operating history might not meet conventional thresholds. Conventional financing is better suited to experienced investors building a portfolio, borrowers who need speed and flexibility, and deals involving investment properties or larger loan amounts that exceed SBA limits.

SBA Advantages

The SBA loan programs offer several powerful benefits that make them the preferred option for qualifying small business borrowers. The most significant advantage is the lower down payment requirement. While conventional lenders typically require 20% to 30% equity, SBA 7(a) loans can close with as little as 10% to 15% down, and SBA 504 loans require just a 10% equity injection from the borrower. For a $1 million property, that is the difference between needing $100,000 and $250,000 in cash at closing. This lower barrier to entry is often the deciding factor for first-time buyers and businesses with limited working capital.

SBA loans also offer significantly longer repayment terms. Real estate loans under both the 7(a) and 504 programs can extend up to 25 years, and they are fully amortizing. This means there is no balloon payment at the end of the term. The borrower simply makes regular payments over the full 25 years until the loan is paid off. Conventional commercial mortgages, by contrast, typically mature in 5 to 10 years even though payments are calculated on a 25- or 30-year amortization schedule. When the conventional loan matures, the borrower must either pay off the remaining balance, refinance, or sell the property. This balloon risk is one of the most significant drawbacks of conventional financing, and SBA loans eliminate it entirely.

The SBA 504 program deserves special attention for its below-market fixed rates. Because the CDC debenture that funds the government-backed portion of the 504 loan is tied to Treasury yields with a thin spread, borrowers often secure rates in the 5.5% to 6.5% range in 2026. This is frequently the lowest fixed rate available in the entire commercial lending market, making the 504 program exceptionally attractive for owner-occupants who qualify.

Finally, the government guarantee (75% to 85% of the loan) reduces risk for the lender, which means SBA-approved lenders can approve borrowers who might not pass conventional underwriting. Businesses with shorter operating histories, lower credit scores, or thinner balance sheets may qualify for SBA financing when conventional lenders decline the deal. The SBA guarantee effectively extends the credit box, opening the door for borrowers who would otherwise be shut out of commercial real estate ownership.

Conventional Advantages

Conventional commercial mortgages offer their own set of advantages that make them the better choice for many borrowers, particularly those with strong financial profiles and specific deal structures that fall outside SBA parameters.

Speed is often the most important conventional advantage. Conventional commercial mortgages typically close in 30 to 60 days, with some bank portfolio loans closing in as little as three to four weeks for straightforward deals. SBA loans, by comparison, generally take 60 to 90 days, and 504 loans can stretch even longer. In competitive acquisition situations where the seller has multiple offers, the ability to close quickly can mean the difference between winning and losing the deal.

Conventional loans impose far fewer restrictions on borrowers and properties. There is no owner-occupancy requirement, which means investors can use conventional financing to purchase rental properties, multi-tenant office buildings, shopping centers, and other investment real estate. There are no SBA size standards to meet, no requirement that the business be for-profit, and no limitations on the borrower's industry. Any creditworthy borrower or entity can qualify for conventional financing.

Loan size flexibility is another major conventional advantage. SBA 7(a) loans are capped at $5 million, which excludes a large portion of commercial real estate transactions. Conventional lenders have no government-imposed size limits. Whether the deal is $500,000 or $50 million, conventional financing can accommodate it. For growing businesses and experienced investors, this scalability is essential.

The documentation burden for conventional loans is also lighter. While SBA applications require a detailed business plan, financial projections, three years of personal and business tax returns, personal financial statements, and extensive supporting documentation, conventional lenders focus primarily on the property's income, the borrower's financial strength, and the appraisal. The streamlined process saves time and reduces the administrative overhead for borrowers.

Conventional loans also offer more flexibility in property types. While SBA programs restrict financing to properties that serve specific business purposes and meet occupancy requirements, conventional lenders will finance a much broader range of commercial real estate, including investment properties, special-purpose facilities, and properties in industries that the SBA may restrict or exclude.

Eligibility Comparison

The eligibility requirements for SBA and conventional loans differ substantially, and understanding these differences early in the process can save significant time and effort.

SBA loans are reserved exclusively for for-profit small businesses that meet the SBA's size standards. These standards vary by industry and are defined by either annual revenue or number of employees. For most industries, the size standard is based on average annual receipts over the past five years or the number of employees. Businesses that exceed these thresholds are ineligible for SBA financing regardless of how strong their financials may be. Additionally, certain business types are categorically excluded from SBA lending, including real estate investment companies, lending institutions, and businesses primarily engaged in speculative activities.

The owner-occupancy requirement is perhaps the most significant eligibility distinction. For SBA 7(a) loans, the borrower's business must occupy at least 51% of the property. For SBA 504 loans, the same 51% occupancy threshold applies. This requirement effectively eliminates SBA financing for pure investment properties and limits its use to businesses that will physically operate from the purchased space.

Conventional commercial mortgages have no government-mandated eligibility criteria. Qualification is determined entirely by the lender's underwriting standards, which focus on the borrower's creditworthiness, the property's income and value, and the overall strength of the deal. Any individual, partnership, LLC, corporation, or trust can apply. There are no industry restrictions, no size standards, and no occupancy requirements. This open eligibility makes conventional financing the default option for investors, large businesses, and any borrower who does not fit the SBA mold.

Documentation Requirements

The paperwork required for each loan type reflects the different levels of government oversight and lender risk involved.

SBA loan applications are documentation-intensive. The standard package includes a completed SBA loan application (Form 1919 or 1920), a detailed business plan with market analysis and revenue projections, three years of personal tax returns for all owners with 20% or more stake, three years of business tax returns, personal financial statements (SBA Form 413), a schedule of business debts, year-to-date profit and loss statements, balance sheets, business licenses and registrations, articles of incorporation or organization, lease agreements (for existing properties), and purchase agreements and appraisals for the property being acquired. For SBA 504 loans, additional documentation related to the CDC application and project cost breakdown is required.

This extensive documentation serves a dual purpose. It satisfies the SBA's requirement that the borrower demonstrates both creditworthiness and the ability to repay, and it gives the lender confidence that the government guarantee will be honored if the loan defaults. The trade-off is a longer preparation and review period.

Conventional commercial mortgage applications focus more narrowly on the property and the borrower's financial strength. A typical package includes two to three years of personal and business tax returns, a personal financial statement, a current rent roll (for investment properties), property operating statements, a third-party appraisal, environmental reports (Phase I, sometimes Phase II), a purchase agreement, and entity documentation. While still substantial, the conventional package omits the business plan, projections, and SBA-specific forms that add considerable time to the SBA process. For experienced borrowers with organized financial records, a conventional application can be assembled in a matter of days.

Prepayment Penalty Differences

Prepayment penalties protect the lender against the loss of expected interest income when a borrower pays off a loan early. The structure of these penalties differs significantly between SBA and conventional loans, and the distinction can have a major financial impact on borrowers who sell, refinance, or pay down their loans ahead of schedule.

SBA 7(a) loans carry a prepayment penalty only during the first three years of the loan. The penalty applies only if the borrower prepays more than 25% of the outstanding balance in any single year during that initial period. The penalty is 5% in year one, 3% in year two, and 1% in year three. After year three, there is no prepayment penalty at all. This declining structure is relatively borrower-friendly and allows substantial flexibility for businesses that grow quickly or receive an acquisition offer.

SBA 504 loans have a longer prepayment restriction. The CDC debenture portion of the loan carries a declining prepayment penalty over 10 years, which reflects the longer-term nature of the government-backed funding. The penalty decreases each year and reaches zero after the tenth year. Borrowers who plan to hold their property for a decade or more will never encounter this penalty, but those with shorter timelines should factor it into their total cost analysis.

Conventional commercial mortgages typically use one of three prepayment structures, each designed to compensate the lender for the full cost of early repayment. Yield maintenance requires the borrower to pay a lump sum equal to the present value of the remaining interest payments, discounted at the current Treasury rate. This can be extremely expensive, especially in a falling rate environment. Defeasance requires the borrower to purchase a portfolio of Treasury securities that replicate the remaining loan cash flows, effectively substituting government-backed collateral for the property. Step-down penalties use a fixed declining percentage schedule (such as 5-4-3-2-1 over five years). Of these three, step-down penalties are the most predictable and typically the least costly, while yield maintenance and defeasance can add hundreds of thousands of dollars to the cost of an early exit.

Owner-Occupancy Requirements

The owner-occupancy requirement is one of the most consequential distinctions between SBA and conventional financing, and misunderstanding it can derail a deal.

Both SBA 7(a) and 504 loans require the borrower's business to occupy at least 51% of the property. This means the business must physically use the majority of the rentable square footage. For a single-tenant building, this is straightforward: the borrower's company operates from the premises. For multi-tenant or mixed-use properties, the calculation becomes more nuanced. The borrower's business must occupy 51% or more of the total usable space, and the remaining space can be leased to third-party tenants.

This requirement has several practical implications. First, it eliminates SBA financing for any property that is primarily an investment. A borrower who wants to buy a four-unit strip retail center and lease all four units to tenants cannot use SBA financing. Second, it limits future flexibility. If a business owner purchases a building with SBA financing, occupies 60% of the space, and later decides to downsize and lease out the majority of the building, that change could technically violate the loan terms. While lenders rarely call loans for minor occupancy shifts after closing, significant changes in use should be discussed with the lender.

Mixed-use properties present interesting opportunities under SBA rules. A business owner who buys a two-story building, operates their company from the ground floor (55% of the total space), and rents out the second floor (45%) to a third party would qualify for SBA financing while generating rental income that supports the debt service. This structure is common and can be highly advantageous for small businesses.

Conventional commercial mortgages have no owner-occupancy requirement whatsoever. The borrower can occupy 100% of the property, 0% of the property, or any percentage in between. This flexibility is why conventional financing is the standard choice for real estate investors who purchase properties solely for lease income.

Property Type Restrictions

SBA programs place meaningful restrictions on the types of properties that qualify for financing. The property must be used for business purposes, and certain property types and industries are excluded or restricted. Properties used for speculative investment, passive rental income (without owner-occupancy), or speculative land development are not eligible. Additionally, the SBA restricts financing for businesses involved in lending, gambling, political lobbying, and certain other industries.

Common property types financed through SBA programs include owner-occupied office buildings, retail storefronts, medical and dental offices, restaurants, auto repair shops, manufacturing facilities, warehouses, and mixed-use buildings where the business occupies the required percentage. Hotels and motels are also eligible under both 7(a) and 504 programs, making SBA financing popular in the hospitality sector for owner-operators.

Conventional commercial mortgages finance a much broader range of property types. In addition to all the property types eligible under SBA programs, conventional lenders will finance multi-tenant apartment buildings, shopping centers, industrial parks, self-storage facilities, mobile home parks, senior housing, and other investment-grade commercial real estate. There are no industry restrictions beyond what the individual lender's risk appetite dictates. Some conventional lenders specialize in specific property types and offer particularly competitive terms for their areas of expertise.

When SBA Wins vs When Conventional Wins

Choosing between SBA and conventional financing comes down to a handful of key variables. The following decision framework can help you identify which path is likely to produce the best outcome for your specific situation.

SBA is typically the better choice when:

  • You are a first-time business buyer with limited cash reserves and need the lowest possible down payment to preserve working capital.
  • You want a fully amortizing loan with no balloon payment, eliminating refinance risk at maturity.
  • Your business qualifies for the SBA 504 program, which offers below-market fixed rates that are difficult to match through conventional channels.
  • Your credit profile or business history is strong but not strong enough to meet conventional underwriting thresholds. The SBA guarantee gives lenders the confidence to approve deals they might otherwise decline.
  • You plan to occupy the property long-term and want the certainty of a 25-year repayment schedule.

Conventional financing is typically the better choice when:

  • You are an experienced real estate investor purchasing income-producing properties for lease to tenants.
  • The deal requires a loan amount above $5 million, exceeding SBA 7(a) limits.
  • Closing speed is critical, and you cannot afford the 60- to 90-day SBA timeline.
  • You have a strong cash position with 25% or more for a down payment and want to minimize paperwork and restrictions.
  • The property does not meet SBA occupancy or eligibility requirements.
  • You value flexibility in prepayment, property use, and future refinancing options.

Either option can work when: you are an owner-occupant with a strong financial profile and adequate capital. In this scenario, running both options in parallel and comparing total cost of capital (including fees, rate, term, and prepayment structure) will reveal which program offers the better deal for your specific situation.

Hybrid Strategy: Using Both Programs Over Time

Savvy business owners and investors do not limit themselves to one financing channel. A hybrid strategy that leverages both SBA and conventional financing at different stages of growth can optimize capital efficiency and accelerate portfolio expansion.

Start with SBA for your first purchase. When you are buying your first commercial property for business use, the SBA's low down payment requirement preserves your cash. Instead of putting 25% down on a $1 million building ($250,000), you put down 10% to 15% ($100,000 to $150,000), keeping $100,000 or more in reserve for working capital, tenant improvements, and unexpected expenses. The SBA's fully amortizing structure means you are building equity from day one without the pressure of a balloon payment looming five or seven years out.

Then use conventional for investment properties. Once your business is established and generating strong cash flow, you can begin acquiring investment properties using conventional financing. Because SBA loans require owner-occupancy, they cannot be used for rental properties. Conventional loans fill this gap, allowing you to purchase multi-tenant buildings, retail centers, and other income-producing assets. With the equity you have built in your SBA-financed property and the cash flow from your business, you are now in a much stronger position to meet conventional down payment and underwriting requirements.

Refinance from SBA to conventional when appropriate. There are situations where refinancing an existing SBA loan into a conventional mortgage makes sense. If your property has appreciated significantly and your business has a strong track record, you may be able to refinance at a lower rate, pull out equity for additional investments, or move to a loan structure that better fits your current needs. After the SBA 7(a) prepayment penalty expires (year three), you can refinance without cost. For SBA 504 loans, the longer prepayment window means waiting is usually the better approach unless the financial benefit of refinancing is substantial enough to absorb the penalty.

This staged approach allows business owners to start with the most accessible financing available, build a track record and equity position, and then expand into conventional financing as their portfolio and financial strength grow. Many of the most successful small business real estate owners followed exactly this path.
