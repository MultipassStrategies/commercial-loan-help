Fixed vs. Variable Rate Commercial Mortgages: Which Is Right for Your Deal?

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Fixed rate basis: Treasury spread
  • Variable rate basis: SOFR + spread
  • Fixed advantage: Payment predictability
  • Variable advantage: Lower initial rate, flexibility
  • Prepayment: Fixed has penalties; variable typically does not
  • Best for: Fixed: long hold; Variable: short hold or value-add

How Fixed Rates Work in Commercial Lending

A fixed-rate commercial mortgage locks your interest rate for the entire loan term (or for a defined period within the term), so your monthly debt service payment remains constant regardless of what happens in the broader economy. For commercial real estate borrowers, this predictability is the single most valuable feature of fixed-rate financing. You know exactly what you owe every month, which makes budgeting, cash flow projections, and long-term business planning significantly easier.

Fixed rates on commercial mortgages are benchmarked to U.S. Treasury yields. The most common reference is the 10-year Treasury note for loans with 10-year terms, though 5-year and 7-year Treasuries are used for shorter fixed-rate periods. The lender adds a spread on top of the Treasury yield to account for credit risk, loan servicing costs, and profit margin. For example, if the 10-year Treasury is yielding 4.25% and the lender's spread is 2.00%, the borrower's all-in fixed rate would be 6.25%.

The spread varies based on several factors: property type (multifamily properties generally command tighter spreads than retail or office), loan-to-value ratio, borrower creditworthiness, debt service coverage ratio, and whether the loan will be held on the lender's balance sheet or securitized (as with CMBS loans). Stabilized, institutional-quality assets with strong cash flow typically receive the most favorable spreads, while transitional or higher-risk properties pay more.

One critical characteristic of fixed-rate commercial loans is the prepayment structure. Because the lender has committed capital at a specific rate for a defined term, most fixed-rate loans carry some form of prepayment protection. Common structures include yield maintenance (which compensates the lender for lost interest income based on the difference between your rate and current Treasury yields), defeasance (which requires you to purchase Treasury securities that replicate the remaining loan cash flows), and step-down penalties (a declining percentage penalty, such as 5-4-3-2-1 over five years). These prepayment provisions are a major consideration for borrowers who may need to sell or refinance before the loan matures.

How Variable Rates Work: SOFR and Rate Caps

A variable-rate (also called floating-rate) commercial mortgage has an interest rate that adjusts periodically based on a benchmark index. Since the transition away from LIBOR, the standard benchmark for U.S. commercial real estate loans is the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, or SOFR. Your loan rate is expressed as SOFR plus a fixed spread. For instance, a loan priced at SOFR + 2.50% with 30-day average SOFR at 4.30% would carry a current all-in rate of 6.80%.

SOFR is published daily by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York and is based on actual overnight repurchase agreement (repo) transactions collateralized by U.S. Treasury securities. Because it reflects real market activity rather than estimated rates (as LIBOR did), SOFR is considered a more transparent and reliable benchmark. Most commercial floating-rate loans reference 30-day average SOFR, though some use the 90-day term SOFR published by CME Group. Rate resets typically occur monthly, meaning your payment adjusts each month as SOFR moves.

The spread on a variable-rate loan is negotiated at origination and remains constant for the loan term. Spreads on floating-rate commercial mortgages generally range from 1.75% to 4.00% over SOFR, depending on property type, leverage, borrower experience, and loan structure. Bridge loans and higher-leverage transitional deals command wider spreads, while lower-leverage loans on stabilized properties receive tighter pricing.

Variable-rate loans typically offer significantly more flexibility than their fixed-rate counterparts. Most floating-rate commercial mortgages have no prepayment penalty (or only a modest exit fee of 0.50% to 1.00% during the first year), making it easy to refinance, sell the property, or pay down the loan without incurring substantial costs. This flexibility is why variable rates are the standard choice for bridge loans, value-add strategies, and any business plan that involves a near-term exit or refinance.

The primary risk of a variable rate is obvious: if SOFR rises, your interest expense increases and your cash flow shrinks. To mitigate this risk, nearly all lenders require borrowers to purchase an interest rate cap, which functions as a ceiling on how high your effective rate can go. We will cover rate caps in detail below.

Rate Cap Requirements and Costs

If you take a variable-rate commercial mortgage, your lender will almost certainly require you to purchase an interest rate cap. A rate cap is a derivative contract that sets a maximum (or "strike") rate on the SOFR component of your loan. If SOFR rises above the strike rate, the cap provider (typically a major bank such as JPMorgan, Wells Fargo, or Bank of America) pays you the difference, effectively capping your interest expense.

For example, suppose your loan is priced at SOFR + 2.50% and you purchase a rate cap with a 5.00% SOFR strike. If SOFR rises to 5.75%, the cap provider pays you the 0.75% difference on the notional amount of the cap. Your effective rate stays at 7.50% (the 5.00% cap strike + your 2.50% spread) rather than rising to 8.25%.

The cost of a rate cap depends on four primary variables:

  • Notional amount: The loan balance the cap covers. A larger loan means a more expensive cap.
  • Strike rate: A lower strike (more protection) costs more than a higher strike. Lenders typically require a strike rate that ensures the property's DSCR stays above 1.0x to 1.25x even at the capped rate.
  • Term: Caps are purchased for a specific period, usually matching the initial loan term (commonly two or three years). Longer caps cost more.
  • Market volatility: When the market expects greater interest rate uncertainty, cap premiums increase. In periods of low expected volatility, caps are cheaper.

In early 2026, with SOFR in the mid-4% range and volatility relatively moderate following the Federal Reserve's rate adjustments, a two-year rate cap on a $5 million loan with a 4.50% SOFR strike might cost approximately $40,000 to $75,000. A three-year cap on the same terms would run higher. These premiums are paid upfront at closing and are typically a required closing cost on any floating-rate deal. When comparing fixed vs. variable total costs, the cap premium must be factored into the variable-rate side of the equation.

Hybrid Options: The Best of Both Worlds?

Not every commercial mortgage is purely fixed or purely variable. Hybrid structures blend elements of both, giving borrowers a middle path that may suit certain strategies better than either extreme.

Fixed-to-float. Some loans start with a fixed rate for an initial period (typically three to five years), then convert to a floating rate tied to SOFR for the remaining term. This structure gives borrowers initial certainty while providing flexibility in the later years. It can be attractive for borrowers who want stability during a lease-up or early holding period but expect to refinance or sell before the floating period begins.

Float-to-fixed. Less common, but available from some portfolio lenders and credit unions, these loans begin with a variable rate and convert to a fixed rate after a specified trigger (a date, a stabilization milestone, or a borrower election). This structure benefits borrowers who are executing a business plan that will improve the property's profile and qualify it for better fixed-rate terms in the near future.

Adjustable-rate mortgages (ARMs). Commercial ARMs reset at defined intervals (such as every five years) rather than monthly. A 5/5 ARM, for instance, locks the rate for five years, then resets based on prevailing market conditions for another five years. ARMs offer more predictability than a pure floating rate while still allowing some rate adjustment over the loan's life.

Interest rate swaps. Sophisticated borrowers can combine a floating-rate loan with a separate interest rate swap agreement, effectively converting the variable rate into a synthetic fixed rate. The borrower pays a fixed rate to the swap counterparty and receives the floating rate, netting out to a fixed cost. Swaps are common in large balance commercial and multifamily lending, and they can sometimes produce a lower effective fixed rate than a traditional fixed-rate loan because the underlying floating-rate loan may have more favorable terms and lower prepayment restrictions.

How the 2026 Rate Environment Affects Your Decision

The interest rate landscape in early 2026 reflects a period of transition. After several rate cuts by the Federal Reserve in late 2024 and through 2025, the federal funds rate has settled into a range that places 30-day average SOFR in the mid-4% area. The 10-year Treasury yield, which drives fixed commercial mortgage rates, has been trading in the 4.00% to 4.50% range as markets weigh persistent inflation concerns against slowing economic growth.

For commercial real estate borrowers, this environment creates a nuanced decision. Fixed rates on stabilized commercial properties are generally available in the 6.25% to 7.25% range, while variable rates start about 50 to 100 basis points lower. The gap between short-term and long-term rates (the yield curve) is relatively flat, which means the initial cost savings of a variable rate are smaller than they would be in a steep-curve environment.

Several factors should inform your decision in the current market:

  • Fed policy trajectory. If you believe the Federal Reserve will continue to cut rates or hold steady, a variable rate allows you to benefit from further declines. If you believe inflation may force the Fed to reverse course, locking in a fixed rate protects you from upward moves.
  • Yield curve shape. A flat or inverted yield curve narrows the advantage of floating-rate borrowing. When long-term rates are close to short-term rates, the cost of locking in certainty is relatively low, making fixed rates more attractive on a risk-adjusted basis.
  • Property business plan. The rate structure should match your execution timeline. A three-year value-add project should not be saddled with a 10-year fixed-rate loan and its associated prepayment penalties. Conversely, a stabilized asset you plan to hold for a decade benefits from the certainty of a fixed rate.
  • Rate cap costs. In the current environment, rate cap premiums are moderate. If cap costs spike due to renewed volatility, the all-in cost of a variable rate increases, potentially eroding the initial rate advantage.

When to Choose a Fixed Rate

A fixed-rate commercial mortgage is the stronger choice in several well-defined scenarios. Understanding when fixed-rate financing aligns with your strategy helps you avoid both overpaying for certainty you do not need and underprotecting yourself against rate risk you cannot afford.

Stabilized, cash-flowing properties. If you are acquiring or refinancing a property with stable occupancy, predictable rents, and no major capital expenditure plans, a fixed rate locks in your cost of debt and allows you to model returns with confidence. This is especially true for net-leased properties with long-term tenants, where the landlord's primary objective is preserving the spread between rental income and debt service.

Long hold periods. Borrowers who plan to own a property for seven years or more benefit from the certainty of a fixed rate. Over a long horizon, the probability of encountering a period of rising short-term rates is significant, and the cumulative impact of even modest rate increases on a variable loan can exceed the initial savings many times over.

Rate lock opportunities. When Treasury yields dip due to economic uncertainty or a flight to safety, locking in a fixed rate captures favorable pricing that may not be available later. Borrowers who are watching the market and can move quickly during these windows often secure rates well below the trailing average.

Conservative underwriting and risk tolerance. If your deal has thin margins, limited reserves, or a DSCR close to the lender's minimum, a fixed rate removes one of the largest variables from your cash flow model. For borrowers who prioritize sleep-at-night certainty over marginal savings, the fixed-rate premium is well worth paying.

When to Choose a Variable Rate

Variable-rate financing is not simply the "riskier" option. In many situations, it is the smarter and more cost-effective structure. The key is matching the loan to the business plan.

Bridge and transitional loans. If you are purchasing a property that needs renovation, repositioning, or lease-up before it qualifies for permanent financing, a floating-rate bridge loan is the standard tool. Bridge lenders expect short hold periods and structure their loans accordingly, with interest-only payments, flexible draw schedules for capital improvements, and no prepayment penalties. A fixed rate would be both more expensive and operationally restrictive for this type of deal.

Value-add strategies. Borrowers executing a value-add business plan (renovating units, improving management, increasing rents) typically plan to refinance into permanent debt once the property is stabilized. A variable-rate loan with a two- to four-year term aligns perfectly with this timeline. The lower initial rate preserves cash flow during the improvement period, and the lack of prepayment penalties allows a clean exit into fixed-rate permanent financing when the property's improved performance supports better terms.

Expected refinance or sale within three to five years. If your investment thesis includes a near-term capital event (sale, refinance, or recapitalization), a fixed-rate loan's prepayment penalties can cost hundreds of thousands of dollars and significantly erode returns. A variable rate avoids this problem entirely.

Declining rate environment. When the Federal Reserve is actively cutting rates or markets are pricing in future cuts, a variable rate allows you to benefit from each reduction without refinancing. If SOFR drops 1.00% during your hold period, your interest expense declines dollar-for-dollar, directly improving cash flow and returns.

In this scenario, the variable-rate loan saves approximately $246,000 over three years, driven by both the lower interest rate and the absence of a prepayment penalty. The rate cap limits worst-case exposure, but the borrower still faces more cost uncertainty than with the fixed option.

The takeaway is clear: for a borrower planning a three-year hold with a defined exit, the variable rate is significantly cheaper under most rate scenarios. For a borrower planning to hold for ten years, the fixed rate's certainty becomes far more valuable as the range of possible SOFR outcomes widens with time.

Interest Rate Hedging Options

Beyond the simple fixed-or-variable choice, commercial borrowers have access to several hedging tools that can fine-tune interest rate exposure. These instruments are most commonly used by borrowers with loans above $3 million, where the cost of the hedge is justified by the magnitude of the rate risk.

Interest rate caps. As discussed above, caps set a maximum on the floating-rate index. They are the most common hedging tool in commercial real estate and are required by most floating-rate lenders. Caps provide asymmetric protection: you benefit if rates fall but are protected if they rise above the strike. The cost is the upfront premium.

Interest rate swaps. A swap converts a floating rate into a synthetic fixed rate. The borrower enters into a separate agreement with a counterparty (usually the lender or a major bank) to exchange floating-rate payments for fixed-rate payments on a specified notional amount. Swaps do not require an upfront premium, but they create a two-way obligation: if rates fall, the borrower continues to pay the higher fixed swap rate. Breaking a swap early can result in a termination fee that functions much like a prepayment penalty. Swaps are best suited for borrowers who want fixed-rate certainty but prefer the operational flexibility and terms of a floating-rate loan.

Interest rate collars. A collar combines a rate cap (purchased by the borrower) with a rate floor (sold by the borrower to the counterparty). The premium received from selling the floor partially or fully offsets the cost of purchasing the cap, creating a "zero-cost" or "low-cost" collar. The trade-off is that the borrower gives up the benefit of rate decreases below the floor. If SOFR drops below the floor rate, the borrower effectively pays the floor rate rather than the lower market rate. Collars are useful when rate cap premiums are expensive and the borrower wants to reduce upfront hedging costs while still limiting upside exposure.

Forward rate locks. Borrowers who plan to close a fixed-rate loan in the future (for example, upon completion of construction or stabilization) can lock in today's Treasury rate through a forward rate lock agreement with their lender. This eliminates the risk that rates will rise before the loan closes. Forward locks typically involve a deposit or fee, and the borrower is obligated to close the loan at the locked rate. If rates decline, the borrower does not benefit from the drop. Forward locks are commonly used in construction-to-permanent loan programs.

Each of these tools involves trade-offs between cost, flexibility, and risk exposure. The right hedge depends on your loan size, hold period, risk tolerance, and view of where rates are headed. For deals above $5 million, consulting with a capital markets advisor or your lender's derivatives desk can help identify the most efficient hedging structure.

SOFR stands for the Secured Overnight Financing Rate. It is the benchmark that replaced LIBOR for most commercial real estate floating-rate loans in the United States. SOFR is based on actual overnight repurchase agreement (repo) transactions collateralized by U.S. Treasury securities, making it a transparent and reliable measure of short-term borrowing costs.

Your variable commercial mortgage rate is typically quoted as SOFR plus a spread (for example, SOFR + 2.50%). As SOFR moves up or down based on Federal Reserve policy and money market conditions, your interest rate and monthly payment adjust accordingly. Most commercial loans reference 30-day average SOFR and reset monthly, though some use 90-day terms.

Understanding SOFR trends is essential for forecasting your debt service costs on any floating-rate loan. You can track daily SOFR rates on the Federal Reserve Bank of New York's website. Because SOFR closely follows the federal funds rate, Federal Reserve policy announcements are the most important driver of where your variable rate is headed.

A rate cap is a financial instrument that limits how high your variable interest rate can go during the loan term. It functions like an insurance policy: you pay an upfront premium to a counterparty (usually a major bank), and if SOFR rises above the cap strike rate, the counterparty pays you the difference. This effectively places a ceiling on your maximum interest expense.

Most lenders require borrowers to purchase a rate cap on any floating-rate commercial mortgage. The requirement protects both the borrower and the lender from extreme rate increases that could push debt service above what the property's cash flow can support. Even when not strictly required, a rate cap is a prudent risk management tool.

The cost of a rate cap depends on the notional loan amount, the cap strike rate, the term, and current market volatility. In early 2026, a two-year cap on a $5 million loan with a reasonable strike rate might cost $40,000 to $75,000. Your lender will specify the minimum cap requirements (strike rate and term) as a condition of closing.

In most cases, you cannot convert a variable-rate commercial mortgage to a fixed rate within the same loan agreement. Unlike some residential mortgage products that offer a built-in conversion feature, commercial loans are generally structured as either fixed or variable from the start.

To move from a variable rate to a fixed rate, you would typically need to refinance into a new fixed-rate loan. This involves standard closing costs, a new appraisal, and potentially a new lender relationship. The good news is that most floating-rate commercial loans carry little or no prepayment penalty, so the refinance itself is usually straightforward from a cost perspective.

Some lenders do offer hybrid structures that start with a variable rate and convert to a fixed rate after a specified period or milestone. If the ability to convert is important to your strategy, ask about these options during the origination process rather than assuming you can switch later. You can also achieve a synthetic conversion by entering into an interest rate swap at any point during your loan term, though swaps involve their own costs and obligations.

The spread between fixed and variable rates fluctuates based on the shape of the Treasury yield curve and Federal Reserve policy. Historically, fixed rates on commercial mortgages run 0.50% to 1.50% higher than comparable variable rates at the time of origination.

In early 2026, the gap is at the narrower end of that range. Ten-year fixed rates for stabilized properties are typically quoted around 6.25% to 7.25%, while variable rates on similar deals start around 5.75% to 6.75% (SOFR + spread). The relatively flat yield curve in 2026 means the premium for locking in a fixed rate is smaller than it has been in some past cycles.

On a $3 million loan, a 0.75% rate difference translates to roughly $22,500 per year in interest expense. Over a five-year hold, that is $112,500 in additional interest for the fixed-rate option, assuming the variable rate stays constant. Of course, the variable rate will not stay constant, which is precisely why the comparison requires careful analysis of your rate outlook, hold period, and tolerance for payment variability.
