Bridge Loan vs Permanent Loan: Which Is Right for Your Commercial Property?

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Bridge term: 6 to 36 months
  • Permanent term: 5 to 30 years
  • Bridge rates: 7.5% to 12%+ (SOFR + spread)
  • Permanent rates: 5.5% to 7.5% (fixed or floating)
  • Bridge LTV: Up to 75% to 80%
  • Permanent LTV: Up to 65% to 80% (varies by program)

What Is a Bridge Loan?

A bridge loan is a short-term financing tool designed for commercial properties that are in transition. Whether you are acquiring an underperforming asset, funding a renovation, leasing up a vacant building, or repositioning a property for a higher use, a bridge loan provides the capital to execute your business plan when traditional long-term lenders are not an option.

Bridge loans typically carry terms of 6 to 36 months, with 12 to 24 months being the most common initial term. Many bridge lenders offer one or two six-month extension options, giving borrowers additional runway if their stabilization plan takes longer than expected. The loan is structured as interest-only for the entire term, which keeps monthly payments low during the period when the property may not be generating its full income potential.

The defining characteristic of bridge lending is the underwriting approach. Unlike permanent lenders who focus heavily on current income, debt service coverage, and historical operating performance, bridge lenders underwrite primarily to the property's future value. This is often expressed as the "as-stabilized" or "as-completed" value, which reflects what the property will be worth once renovations are finished, vacancies are filled, and rents are brought to market levels. A bridge lender is essentially making a bet on the borrower's ability to execute a business plan, not just on what the property looks like today.

Bridge loan rates are higher than permanent loan rates, typically ranging from 7.5% to 12% or more depending on the deal's risk profile. Rates are usually floating, priced as SOFR plus a spread of 3.00% to 7.00% or higher. The spread reflects the lender's assessment of execution risk, property condition, borrower experience, leverage level, and market fundamentals. Origination fees on bridge loans generally run 1% to 3% of the loan amount, which is higher than permanent loan origination costs.

Speed is one of the primary advantages of bridge financing. While permanent loans can take 30 to 90 days (or longer) to close, bridge lenders routinely close in 2 to 4 weeks. Some private lenders and debt funds can close in as little as 7 to 10 business days for deals they are familiar with. This speed gives bridge borrowers a significant competitive advantage when pursuing time-sensitive acquisitions or distressed opportunities where sellers want certainty and a fast closing.

What Is a Permanent Loan?

A permanent loan is long-term financing for commercial properties that are already stabilized, meaning they have consistent occupancy, established rental income, and predictable operating expenses. Permanent loans are the foundation of commercial real estate finance; they provide the low-cost, long-duration capital that allows investors to hold properties for years or decades while generating steady cash flow.

Permanent loan terms range from 5 to 30 years. The most common structures in commercial real estate are 5-year, 7-year, and 10-year terms with 25- to 30-year amortization schedules. This means the borrower makes monthly principal and interest payments based on a 25- or 30-year repayment schedule, but the remaining balance comes due as a balloon payment at the end of the shorter loan term. Some programs, particularly those from Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac for multifamily properties, offer fully amortizing terms of up to 30 years with no balloon payment.

Permanent loan rates are significantly lower than bridge rates, typically ranging from 5.5% to 7.5% in the current market. Rates can be fixed for the entire term (benchmarked to U.S. Treasury yields plus a spread) or floating (tied to SOFR plus a spread). Fixed-rate permanent loans are the most common choice for borrowers seeking predictable debt service, while floating-rate permanent loans appeal to borrowers who expect rates to decline or who plan to sell or refinance within a few years.

Underwriting for permanent loans centers on the property's current financial performance. Lenders evaluate the debt service coverage ratio (DSCR), which measures the property's net operating income relative to annual debt service. Most permanent lenders require a minimum DSCR of 1.20x to 1.35x, meaning the property must generate 20% to 35% more income than needed to cover the loan payments. Borrower creditworthiness, property condition, market fundamentals, and tenant quality also factor into the underwriting decision.

Permanent loans are available from a wide range of lender types, each with different programs, rate structures, and requirements. Banks and credit unions offer portfolio loans with flexible terms. CMBS (commercial mortgage-backed securities) lenders provide non-recourse financing for stabilized assets of all property types. Agency lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac) offer some of the best terms available, but only for multifamily properties. Life insurance companies provide competitive fixed-rate financing for high-quality, low-leverage deals on institutional-grade assets.

When a Bridge Loan Makes More Sense

Bridge loans are not simply "expensive short-term debt." They are purpose-built financing tools for situations where permanent lending is either unavailable or would produce inferior results. Here are the scenarios where a bridge loan is the right choice.

Value-add acquisitions. If you are acquiring a property with below-market rents, deferred maintenance, or outdated units that you plan to renovate and reposition, a bridge loan funds both the acquisition and the improvement costs. Permanent lenders will not finance a renovation budget, and they will underwrite only to the property's current (lower) income, resulting in less proceeds and worse terms. A bridge lender will underwrite to the as-stabilized value, giving you higher leverage and the capital to execute your business plan.

Lease-up properties. Buildings with high vacancy do not generate enough income to meet permanent loan DSCR requirements. A bridge loan allows you to acquire or refinance the property, fund tenant improvements and leasing commissions, and carry the asset through the lease-up period until occupancy and income are sufficient for permanent financing. This is common with newly constructed properties, recently renovated buildings, or assets that lost tenants during an economic downturn.

Time-sensitive acquisitions. In competitive markets, the ability to close quickly can make or break a deal. Sellers often prefer buyers who can close in two to three weeks over those requiring 60 to 90 days for permanent loan processing. A bridge loan lets you move at the speed of a cash buyer while still using leverage. Once you own the property, you can refinance into permanent debt at your own pace.

Properties that do not qualify for permanent financing yet. Some properties have issues that disqualify them from permanent lending programs: environmental concerns that need remediation, deferred capital expenditures, zoning or permitting issues being resolved, or tenants with below-investment-grade credit. A bridge loan provides the time and capital to address these issues so the property can qualify for permanent financing later.

Distressed acquisitions. Buying a property out of foreclosure, from a distressed seller, or through a note purchase often requires speed and flexibility that permanent lenders cannot provide. Bridge lenders are accustomed to these situations and can structure loans around the unique complexities of distressed deals, including title issues, deferred maintenance, and uncertain income streams.

When a Permanent Loan Makes More Sense

Permanent financing is the right choice when the property is already performing well and your goal is to hold it for the long term at the lowest possible cost of capital.

Stabilized properties with strong occupancy. If your property has occupancy above 85% to 90%, established rental income, and a track record of consistent cash flow, you qualify for permanent financing at rates that are 200 to 500 basis points lower than bridge rates. There is no reason to pay bridge-level pricing for a property that already meets permanent loan underwriting standards.

Long-term hold strategy. Borrowers who plan to own a property for five years or more benefit from the certainty and low cost of permanent debt. A fixed-rate permanent loan locks in your debt service for the entire term, allowing you to model returns with confidence. Over a long hold period, the savings from a lower permanent rate compound significantly compared to what you would pay on a bridge loan or even a floating-rate permanent loan.

Lowest cost of capital. Permanent loans offer the most competitive pricing in commercial real estate lending. Agency loans for multifamily properties, in particular, provide some of the lowest rates available, along with high leverage, long terms, and non-recourse structures. Life insurance company loans offer similarly attractive terms for high-quality assets. If minimizing your cost of debt is the priority, permanent financing is the answer.

Predictable debt service. For properties where cash flow margins are tight or where the borrower values certainty over flexibility, a fixed-rate permanent loan eliminates interest rate risk entirely. You know exactly what you owe every month for the full loan term, which simplifies budgeting, cash flow management, and investor reporting.

The Bridge-to-Permanent Strategy

One of the most common strategies in commercial real estate is using a bridge loan as a stepping stone to permanent financing. Rather than choosing one or the other, many borrowers use both in sequence: a bridge loan to acquire and improve the property, followed by a permanent loan to hold it long-term at better terms.

The bridge-to-permanent strategy works in a straightforward sequence. First, the borrower secures a bridge loan to acquire the property, often at a higher leverage point based on the as-stabilized value. The bridge loan funds both the purchase price and the capital improvement budget through a holdback or draw structure. Over the next 12 to 24 months, the borrower executes the business plan: completing renovations, leasing up vacant space, increasing rents, improving property management, and building a track record of income.

Once the property is stabilized (typically defined as occupancy above 85% to 90% with at least 3 to 6 months of operating history at the new income level), the borrower refinances the bridge loan into permanent debt. Because the property is now performing at a higher level, the permanent loan is underwritten to the improved income, resulting in better terms: a lower interest rate, potentially higher proceeds, non-recourse structure, and a longer term.

The timing of the refinance is critical. Bridge loan terms are short, and extension options usually require additional fees and may have performance benchmarks (such as minimum occupancy levels) that must be met. Borrowers should begin the permanent loan application process 3 to 6 months before their bridge loan maturity date to ensure a smooth transition. Starting too late risks a gap between the bridge payoff and the permanent loan closing, which can result in default on the bridge loan or the need for a costly short-term extension.

Exit planning should begin before the bridge loan even closes. Experienced borrowers identify their target permanent lender and loan program during the acquisition phase, so they know exactly what stabilization benchmarks they need to hit. This alignment between the bridge loan business plan and the permanent loan requirements is what separates successful bridge-to-permanent executions from deals that get stuck in expensive bridge debt longer than planned.

Cost Comparison: Bridge vs. Permanent Over Time

To illustrate how the costs of bridge and permanent financing compare, consider the following scenario for a $5,000,000 commercial property acquisition.

Scenario: $5M Value-Add Acquisition

An investor acquires a multifamily property for $5,000,000. The property needs $500,000 in renovations and is currently 70% occupied. After renovations and lease-up, the property is expected to reach 95% occupancy with a stabilized value of $6,500,000. The investor plans to hold the property long-term after stabilization.

Option A: Bridge Loan First, Then Permanent

Cost Component Amount
Bridge loan amount $4,000,000 (80% of purchase price)
Bridge rate 9.5% (SOFR + 5.00%)
Bridge term 18 months (interest-only)
Bridge interest paid (18 months) $570,000
Bridge origination fee (2%) $80,000
Bridge exit fee $0
Total bridge period cost $650,000

After 18 months, the property is stabilized and the investor refinances into a permanent loan at 6.25% fixed with a 25-year amortization. The permanent loan is sized at $4,875,000 (75% of the $6,500,000 stabilized value), which pays off the bridge loan, reimburses the renovation costs, and returns capital to the borrower.

Option B: Permanent Loan from Day One

Cost Component Amount
Permanent loan amount $3,250,000 (65% of as-is value, lower LTV due to low occupancy)
Permanent rate 6.75% (higher rate due to risk profile)
Permanent term 10 years, 25-year amortization
Year 1 interest paid $215,000
Year 1.5 interest paid (18-month comparison) $322,500
Origination fee (1%) $32,500
Total 18-month cost $355,000

The Full Picture

At first glance, Option B appears cheaper over the initial 18 months ($355,000 vs. $650,000). However, the permanent loan in Option B provides only $3,250,000 in proceeds compared to $4,000,000 from the bridge loan. The borrower must bring an additional $750,000 in equity to close. The permanent loan also does not fund the $500,000 renovation budget, requiring the borrower to fund improvements entirely out of pocket.

More importantly, after stabilization, the borrower in Option A refinances into a permanent loan at 6.25% on $4,875,000, while the borrower in Option B is locked into a 6.75% rate on $3,250,000 with prepayment penalties that make refinancing expensive. Over the remaining years of the hold period, the lower rate and higher leverage in Option A produce better returns despite the higher upfront cost of the bridge period. The bridge-to-permanent path also allows the borrower to execute the full renovation, which drives the property's value and income to a higher level.

Lender Types for Each

Bridge loans and permanent loans come from different corners of the lending market. Understanding which lender types specialize in each product helps you target the right capital source for your deal.

Bridge Loan Lenders

Debt funds. Private debt funds are the largest source of bridge capital in commercial real estate. These are institutional investors (often backed by pension funds, endowments, and high-net-worth individuals) that raise pools of capital specifically to originate bridge loans. Debt funds offer competitive rates, high leverage, and the ability to close quickly. They are the go-to source for bridge loans in the $1 million to $50 million range and often specialize in specific property types or markets.

Private lenders and hard money lenders. For smaller deals or situations requiring maximum speed and flexibility, private lenders (sometimes called hard money lenders) provide bridge financing with minimal bureaucracy. Rates are higher (often 10% to 14%), but these lenders can close in days rather than weeks and are willing to finance deals that institutional lenders pass on, including distressed properties, land, and special-use assets.

Bridge lending platforms. A growing number of online platforms and fintech lenders now offer bridge loans through streamlined, technology-driven processes. These platforms typically focus on smaller balance loans ($500,000 to $10 million) and can provide term sheets within hours and closings within two to three weeks. They fill the gap between institutional debt funds and local hard money lenders.

Permanent Loan Lenders

Banks and credit unions. Local and regional banks are the most accessible source of permanent commercial mortgages. They offer portfolio loans with flexible terms, competitive rates, and the ability to build a relationship that benefits future deals. Banks typically lend on all property types and offer both fixed and variable rate options. Credit unions provide similar products, often with slightly lower rates and fees due to their nonprofit structure.

CMBS conduit lenders. CMBS (commercial mortgage-backed securities) lenders originate permanent loans that are pooled, securitized, and sold to bond investors. CMBS loans offer non-recourse financing with competitive rates, higher leverage (up to 75% LTV), and terms of 5 to 10 years. They are available for all stabilized property types and are a strong option for borrowers who want non-recourse terms without meeting agency multifamily requirements.

Agency lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac). For multifamily properties, agency lenders provide some of the best terms in the market: low rates, high leverage (up to 80% LTV), long terms (up to 30 years), non-recourse, and interest-only options. Agency loans are the gold standard for stabilized apartment properties and are the first call for most multifamily borrowers seeking permanent financing.

Life insurance companies. Life companies provide permanent financing for institutional-quality commercial real estate at very competitive fixed rates. They prefer low-leverage deals (typically 55% to 65% LTV) on high-quality assets in strong markets. In exchange for lower leverage, borrowers receive some of the lowest rates available, long terms, and favorable prepayment structures. Life company loans are best suited for well-capitalized borrowers with premium assets.
