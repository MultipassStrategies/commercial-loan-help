HUD 223(f) Loans: The Lowest Fixed Rates for Multifamily Acquisition and Refinance

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Max LTV: 85% (acquisition), 85% (refinance)
  • Min DSCR: 1.18x
  • Loan term: Up to 35 years
  • Rate type: Fixed, FHA-insured
  • Amortization: Fully amortizing (no balloon)
  • MIP: 0.25% to 0.65% annually

What Is the HUD 223(f) Program?

The HUD 223(f) program is a Federal Housing Administration (FHA) mortgage insurance program that enables the acquisition or refinancing of existing multifamily properties with five or more residential units. Administered by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) through its Office of Multifamily Housing Programs, the 223(f) program provides long-term, fixed-rate, fully amortizing, non-recourse financing at interest rates that are consistently lower than any other source of permanent multifamily capital. The program accomplishes this by insuring the mortgage against default, which eliminates credit risk for the lender and allows them to pass that benefit through to borrowers in the form of lower rates and better terms.

Section 223(f) of the National Housing Act was created to address a gap in the FHA's multifamily lending toolkit. While the 221(d)(4) program had long served the new construction and substantial rehabilitation market, there was no dedicated FHA program for purchasing or refinancing stabilized, existing apartment properties. The 223(f) program filled that gap by offering the same government-backed insurance and favorable terms for properties that were already built, occupied, and generating income. Over the decades since its introduction, the program has financed billions of dollars in multifamily transactions across the country, from small apartment buildings in rural communities to large urban complexes with hundreds of units.

The fundamental appeal of the 223(f) program is straightforward: no other source of permanent multifamily debt offers the same combination of low fixed rates, high leverage, full amortization, and non-recourse protection. For borrowers willing to accept a longer closing timeline and ongoing regulatory oversight, the 223(f) loan delivers financing economics that conventional lenders, Fannie Mae, and Freddie Mac simply cannot match.

Why 223(f) Offers the Lowest Fixed Rates

The reason HUD 223(f) loans carry the lowest fixed interest rates in the multifamily market comes down to one factor: FHA mortgage insurance. When a lender originates a 223(f) loan, the FHA insures the full mortgage amount against borrower default. If the borrower stops making payments, the lender files a claim with FHA and receives the outstanding principal balance. The lender bears essentially zero credit risk on the loan. Because the risk is removed, lenders do not need to price in a credit spread, a risk premium, or a loss reserve. They can fund the loan at a rate that closely tracks the yield on long-term U.S. government securities, adding only a thin servicing margin.

This stands in sharp contrast to how conventional lenders, Fannie Mae, and Freddie Mac price their loans. Those lenders retain some exposure to default risk (or, in the case of the GSEs, charge guarantee fees to offset that risk). Their rates must account for potential losses, which adds anywhere from 30 to 100+ basis points to the borrower's interest rate compared to a HUD loan. The gap widens further during periods of economic uncertainty, when conventional lenders and the GSEs increase their risk premiums while HUD rates remain anchored to the government yield curve.

Additionally, 223(f) loans are securitized through the Government National Mortgage Association (Ginnie Mae), which issues mortgage-backed securities carrying the full faith and credit of the United States. Investors treat Ginnie Mae securities as virtually equivalent to U.S. Treasury bonds, which means they accept very low yields. That investor appetite for Ginnie Mae paper is the engine that keeps HUD loan rates consistently below every other multifamily lending source.

Eligible Properties

The HUD 223(f) program covers a broad range of existing residential properties, but there are specific eligibility requirements that borrowers must understand before pursuing this financing.

  • Unit count: The property must contain five or more residential units. There is no maximum unit count; the program has been used for properties ranging from small 10-unit apartment buildings to large complexes with 500+ units.
  • Property age: The property must be at least three years old, measured from the date of the certificate of occupancy or the completion of construction. This requirement ensures that the building has an established operating history and that any construction defects have had time to surface.
  • Market-rate apartments: Standard market-rate apartment properties are the most common use of the 223(f) program. These include garden-style apartments, mid-rise and high-rise buildings, and townhouse-style rental communities.
  • Affordable and subsidized housing: Properties with Section 8 Housing Assistance Payment (HAP) contracts, Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC) allocations, or other affordability restrictions are eligible and often receive favorable MIP treatment.
  • Assisted living and board and care facilities: Licensed assisted living facilities and board and care homes that provide residential accommodations with support services qualify under the 223(f) program, provided they meet HUD's licensing and operational requirements.
  • Cooperative housing: Existing cooperative apartment buildings can also be financed through the 223(f) program.

Properties must not require substantial rehabilitation at the time of financing. HUD defines this threshold as non-critical, non-structural repairs that do not exceed the greater of $15,000 per unit or 15% of the estimated replacement cost. Repairs must be completable within 12 months and cannot require the displacement of residents. If the property needs work beyond these limits, the 221(d)(4) substantial rehabilitation program is the appropriate alternative.

Certain property types are not eligible for 223(f) financing. These include mobile home parks, properties used primarily for transient or hotel purposes, continuing care retirement communities (CCRCs), and properties with significant commercial space (generally more than 25% of net rentable area or 25% of effective gross income from commercial sources).

LTV and DSCR Requirements

HUD 223(f) loans offer some of the highest leverage available in multifamily lending, but the specific loan-to-value ratio and debt service coverage ratio requirements depend on the property type and whether the transaction is an acquisition or refinance.

Loan-to-Value Limits

  • Market-rate properties: Up to 85% LTV for both acquisition and refinance transactions.
  • Affordable housing (90% or more of units restricted): Up to 87% LTV, reflecting HUD's mission to encourage preservation and financing of affordable rental housing.
  • Properties with rental assistance: Up to 90% LTV for properties where 90% or more of the units receive project-based Section 8 or other rental subsidies, recognizing the stability and predictability of government-backed rental income.

These LTV limits are calculated based on the lesser of the appraised value (for refinances) or the acquisition cost (for purchases). HUD uses its own appraisal standards and requires the appraisal to be completed by an FHA-approved appraiser, which ensures conservative and consistent valuation across all deals.

Debt Service Coverage Requirements

  • Market-rate properties: Minimum DSCR of 1.18x, meaning the property's net operating income must be at least 118% of the annual debt service.
  • Affordable and subsidized properties: Minimum DSCR of 1.11x for properties where 90% or more of units are subject to affordability restrictions or receive project-based rental assistance. The lower threshold reflects the stability of restricted income streams.

HUD calculates DSCR using underwritten net operating income, which may differ from the borrower's trailing-12-month actuals. The HUD underwriter applies its own vacancy and expense assumptions, which can be more conservative than what the market suggests. Borrowers should expect HUD to stress-test the property's income and expenses independently rather than simply accepting the borrower's pro forma.

Rate Structure and Mortgage Insurance Premium

Understanding the all-in cost of a HUD 223(f) loan requires looking at both the base interest rate and the Mortgage Insurance Premium (MIP). The base rate is the interest rate charged by the originating lender (the MAP lender), and the MIP is the annual fee paid to FHA for providing the mortgage insurance that makes the low rate possible.

MIP Tiers

FHA sets MIP rates based on the loan characteristics and the property's mission alignment. The current MIP schedule includes several tiers:

  • 0.25% annual MIP: Available for properties that qualify as "green" (meeting energy efficiency or water conservation standards such as ENERGY STAR or comparable certifications) and for properties with significant affordable housing components. This is the lowest MIP tier and represents HUD's incentive to promote environmentally sustainable and affordable housing.
  • 0.45% annual MIP: The standard rate for refinance transactions on existing HUD-insured loans where the borrower is refinancing out of a previous FHA mortgage.
  • 0.65% annual MIP: The standard rate for new acquisitions and for refinance transactions where the property does not currently carry FHA insurance. This tier applies to most first-time HUD borrowers entering the program.

How the All-In Rate Is Calculated

The borrower's effective interest rate is the sum of the base note rate and the applicable MIP. For example, if the MAP lender quotes a base rate of 4.50% and the property falls into the 0.65% MIP tier (a new acquisition), the all-in rate to the borrower is 5.15%. For a green or affordable property qualifying for the 0.25% MIP, the same base rate would produce an all-in cost of just 4.75%.

Even at the highest MIP tier, the all-in rate on a HUD 223(f) loan is almost always below what Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, or life insurance companies offer for comparable properties. The MIP cost is further offset by the loan's full amortization (eliminating refinance risk and balloon payment exposure) and non-recourse structure (eliminating personal liability). When evaluated on a total-cost-of-capital basis over the full loan term, HUD 223(f) consistently delivers the best economics in multifamily lending.

The Non-Recourse, Fully Amortizing Advantage

Two structural features of the HUD 223(f) loan set it apart from virtually every other source of multifamily financing: it is fully amortizing and entirely non-recourse. Together, these features eliminate the two largest risks that multifamily borrowers face with conventional debt.

Fully Amortizing: No Balloon Risk

A HUD 223(f) loan amortizes fully over its term, which can extend up to 35 years (or the remaining economic life of the property, whichever is less). This means the borrower makes level monthly payments of principal and interest, and the loan balance reaches zero at the end of the term. There is no balloon payment. There is no maturity date requiring the borrower to refinance or sell.

This is a significant departure from conventional multifamily loans, Fannie Mae DUS loans, and Freddie Mac Optigo loans, all of which typically feature terms of 5, 7, 10, or 12 years with balloon payments at maturity. With those products, the borrower must refinance or pay off the remaining balance when the term expires, creating exposure to whatever interest rate environment exists at that future date. If rates have risen substantially, the borrower may face a cash flow crunch, reduced property value, or an inability to refinance on acceptable terms. The 2026 maturity wall facing billions of dollars in multifamily debt is a direct consequence of this balloon structure.

With a HUD 223(f) loan, this risk simply does not exist. The borrower locks in a fixed rate and a fixed payment for up to 35 years, with no refinancing events, no rate resets, and no principal due at maturity. For borrowers with a long-term hold strategy, this level of certainty is unmatched.

Non-Recourse: No Personal Guarantee

HUD 223(f) loans are structured as non-recourse obligations, meaning the borrower has no personal liability for the mortgage debt. If the property defaults, the lender's remedy is limited to the collateral (the property itself). The borrower's personal assets, other real estate holdings, and business interests are not at risk.

Standard carve-outs apply for fraud, material misrepresentation, environmental contamination, and misapplication of property income or insurance proceeds. These "bad boy" carve-outs are narrow and apply only in cases of borrower misconduct, not ordinary business losses. For borrowers who operate the property honestly and maintain it in good condition, the non-recourse protection is genuine and meaningful.

Limitations and Considerations

The HUD 223(f) program offers exceptional financing terms, but those terms come with trade-offs that borrowers must weigh carefully before committing to this path.

  • Timeline: The most frequently cited drawback of HUD financing is the time required to close. A typical 223(f) loan takes 6 to 12 months from initial engagement to final endorsement. This is significantly longer than the 45- to 90-day timelines available through Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, or conventional lenders. For time-sensitive acquisitions, this timeline may be a dealbreaker unless the seller is willing to accommodate a longer closing period or the borrower uses bridge financing to close quickly and then refinances into the HUD loan.
  • Davis-Bacon wage requirements: If the 223(f) loan includes repairs exceeding a certain cost threshold (generally the greater of $15,000 per unit or an amount set by HUD), all repair work must be performed by contractors paying Davis-Bacon prevailing wages. These wage requirements can increase repair costs by 10% to 30% compared to standard market labor rates, which affects the borrower's renovation budget and overall deal economics.
  • Regulatory Agreement: Every HUD-insured borrower must sign a Regulatory Agreement that grants HUD ongoing oversight of the property. This agreement requires the borrower to maintain the property in good condition, submit annual audited financial statements prepared by an independent CPA, obtain HUD approval for any transfer of physical assets or changes in ownership, comply with HUD's reserve and escrow requirements, and follow specific rules regarding distributions, secondary financing, and operating procedures. The Regulatory Agreement remains in effect for the entire life of the loan.
  • Replacement reserves: HUD requires borrowers to fund a replacement reserve account with monthly deposits, typically ranging from $250 to $300 per unit per year. These funds are held in escrow and can only be drawn with HUD approval for capital expenditures. While the reserves protect the property's long-term condition, they represent a cash flow reduction that conventional lenders do not require.
  • Annual financial reporting: Borrowers must provide audited financial statements to HUD each year, prepared by an independent certified public accountant. This annual audit is an ongoing expense (typically $5,000 to $15,000 depending on property size) that adds to the cost of maintaining the loan.
  • Prepayment restrictions: HUD 223(f) loans typically include a lockout period (usually 2 years) during which no prepayment is permitted, followed by a declining prepayment penalty or defeasance requirement. Borrowers who may need to sell or refinance within the first several years should carefully review the prepayment terms, as they can be more restrictive than those on agency or conventional loans.

The MAP Lender Process

All HUD 223(f) loans are originated through MAP (Multifamily Accelerated Processing) lenders, which are financial institutions approved by HUD to underwrite and process FHA-insured multifamily loans under delegated authority. Understanding the MAP lender's role is essential because the lender you choose will directly affect the speed, quality, and outcome of your HUD financing experience.

What Is a MAP Lender?

A MAP lender is a HUD-approved mortgage bank or financial institution that has been granted delegated processing authority for FHA multifamily loans. This means the MAP lender performs the full underwriting of the loan, including the financial analysis, property evaluation, market study review, and risk assessment, and then submits the completed package to HUD for review and approval. Under the MAP process (introduced in 2000 to replace the slower Traditional Application Processing method), HUD reviews the MAP lender's underwriting rather than starting from scratch, which significantly reduces processing time.

There are approximately 50 to 70 active MAP lenders operating in the United States. Some are large national mortgage banks that originate hundreds of HUD loans per year, while others are smaller regional firms with focused expertise. The quality and experience of the MAP lender matters enormously: a seasoned lender with deep HUD relationships and a strong track record will navigate the process far more efficiently than a lender with limited HUD experience.

The Processing Steps

  1. Pre-application. The borrower engages a MAP lender and provides preliminary information about the property, including rent rolls, operating statements, property condition, and the proposed transaction. The MAP lender evaluates the deal's feasibility and provides a preliminary term sheet outlining the expected loan amount, rate range, and timeline. At this stage, the lender identifies any potential red flags (environmental concerns, market issues, physical deficiencies) that could complicate the application.
  2. Firm application. Once the borrower decides to proceed, the MAP lender assembles the full application package. This includes an FHA appraisal, a Phase I environmental site assessment (and Phase II if warranted), a property condition assessment (also called a Project Capital Needs Assessment, or PCNA), a market study, title and survey reports, organizational and legal documents, three years of audited or certified operating statements, and current rent rolls. The MAP lender's underwriting team analyzes all of these documents and prepares the underwriting narrative and financial projections that will be submitted to HUD.
  3. HUD review and firm commitment. The completed application is submitted to the local HUD Multifamily Hub or Satellite office for review. HUD's staff reviews the MAP lender's underwriting, verifies compliance with all program requirements, and may request clarifications or additional documentation. If the application is approved, HUD issues a Firm Commitment letter, which is a binding offer to insure the mortgage subject to specified conditions.
  4. Closing. After the Firm Commitment is issued, the MAP lender and the borrower work to satisfy all conditions and prepare for closing. This includes finalizing legal documents, establishing escrow accounts, completing any required pre-closing repairs, and coordinating with the title company. The loan closes and funds are disbursed.
  5. Initial endorsement and final endorsement. At closing, HUD issues an Initial Endorsement of the FHA insurance. If the loan includes a repair escrow, HUD will issue Final Endorsement after all repairs are completed and inspected. If no repairs are involved, Initial and Final Endorsement may occur simultaneously. Final Endorsement marks the point at which the loan is fully insured and the Ginnie Mae security can be issued.

Timeline: What to Expect

A realistic timeline for a HUD 223(f) loan from initial engagement to final endorsement is 6 to 12 months. Here is a breakdown of each stage and the typical duration.

Stage Duration Key Activities
Pre-application and lender selection 2 to 4 weeks Identify MAP lender, provide preliminary financials, receive term sheet
Third-party reports 4 to 6 weeks Appraisal, Phase I environmental, PCNA, market study, survey, title
Firm application preparation 4 to 8 weeks MAP lender underwrites deal, assembles full application package
HUD review 8 to 16 weeks HUD reviews application, requests clarifications, issues Firm Commitment
Closing and endorsement 4 to 8 weeks Satisfy commitment conditions, close loan, receive Initial Endorsement

The HUD review stage is the most variable. Processing times depend on the workload at the local HUD office, the complexity of the deal, and the quality of the application package. A clean, well-prepared submission from an experienced MAP lender will move through HUD review faster than a package with gaps, inconsistencies, or unusual issues that require additional scrutiny. Borrowers should also factor in time for any back-and-forth with HUD staff, which is common even on straightforward transactions.

For acquisitions where the seller requires a defined closing date, many borrowers use a bridge loan to close the purchase on the seller's timeline and then refinance into the HUD 223(f) loan once the FHA processing is complete. This strategy allows the borrower to capture the acquisition opportunity without being constrained by HUD's longer timeline.

When HUD 223(f) Is the Right Choice

The HUD 223(f) program is not the right fit for every multifamily transaction. Its longer timeline, regulatory requirements, and upfront costs make it better suited for certain borrower profiles and investment strategies. Here are the scenarios where 223(f) delivers its greatest advantages.

  • Long-term hold strategy. If you plan to own the property for 10 years or more, the 223(f) loan's 35-year fully amortizing structure and low fixed rate generate significant savings over the hold period. The absence of a balloon payment means you never need to refinance, eliminating future rate risk entirely. The longer you hold, the more the HUD loan's economics outperform shorter-term agency and conventional alternatives.
  • Rate sensitivity. For borrowers focused on minimizing their cost of capital, no other permanent multifamily product delivers a lower fixed rate than HUD 223(f). The difference can be 30 to 100+ basis points compared to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, which translates to tens of thousands of dollars in annual debt service savings on larger loans. Over a 35-year term, the cumulative savings can be substantial.
  • Affordable housing preservation. Properties with Section 8 contracts, LIHTC restrictions, or other affordable housing commitments benefit from the 223(f) program's higher LTV limits (up to 90%) and lower MIP rates (as low as 0.25%). HUD's mission alignment with affordable housing means these borrowers receive the most favorable terms available anywhere in the market.
  • Cash flow maximization. The combination of high leverage (85%+ LTV), low rates, full amortization, and no balloon creates the highest possible cash-on-cash return for multifamily investors who prioritize steady, predictable income. The non-recourse structure further enhances the return profile by eliminating personal risk.
  • Refinancing out of maturing debt. Borrowers facing balloon maturities on existing agency or conventional loans can use the 223(f) program to refinance into permanent, fully amortizing debt and eliminate future maturity risk. This strategy is especially valuable in rising rate environments where refinancing short-term debt repeatedly becomes increasingly expensive.

Conversely, borrowers who need to close quickly (within 60 to 90 days), who plan to sell or refinance within a few years, or who are uncomfortable with HUD's regulatory oversight may find that Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, or a conventional lender is a better fit for their situation. The right choice depends on your investment horizon, risk tolerance, and willingness to accept HUD's process in exchange for its unmatched financing terms.

The Mortgage Insurance Premium (MIP) is an annual fee paid to FHA for insuring the loan against default. The MIP is what enables HUD 223(f) loans to carry such low interest rates: because FHA absorbs the credit risk, lenders can offer rates that track government bond yields rather than reflecting borrower-specific risk. The MIP is calculated as a percentage of the outstanding loan balance and is paid monthly as part of the regular mortgage payment.

MIP rates vary by transaction type and property characteristics. New acquisition loans and refinances of non-HUD debt carry a 0.65% annual MIP. Refinances of existing HUD-insured mortgages carry a 0.45% annual MIP. Properties that qualify as green (meeting energy efficiency standards such as ENERGY STAR certification) or that serve predominantly affordable housing populations may qualify for a reduced MIP of 0.25% per year.

While the MIP adds to the borrower's effective interest rate, the combined cost (base rate plus MIP) on a 223(f) loan remains lower than the all-in rates offered by Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and most conventional lenders. The MIP is also offset by the loan's structural advantages: full amortization eliminates balloon risk, and non-recourse protects the borrower from personal liability.

The HUD 223(f) program allows for repairs, but it is not designed for major rehabilitation or gut renovations. Eligible repairs must be non-structural and cannot exceed the greater of $15,000 per unit or 15% of the property's replacement cost (with potential adjustments in high-cost areas). All repairs must be completable within 12 months of loan closing, and residents cannot be required to vacate their units during the work.

Common repairs that fit within the 223(f) framework include roof replacement, HVAC system upgrades, plumbing and electrical improvements, kitchen and bathroom updates, flooring and paint, parking lot resurfacing, landscaping, and common area renovations. The cost of eligible repairs is included in the mortgage amount and held in an escrow account, with funds released as work is completed and inspected.

If your property requires structural work, additions, extensive reconfiguration, or repairs that exceed the 223(f) cost thresholds, the HUD 221(d)(4) program is the appropriate path. The 221(d)(4) program is specifically designed for substantial rehabilitation (and new construction) and does not impose the same repair caps. Your MAP lender can help you determine which program fits your planned scope of work.

Choosing the right MAP lender is one of the most consequential decisions in the HUD financing process. When evaluating MAP lenders, ask about their annual volume of HUD closings, their experience with your property type and geographic market, and the specific team members who will handle your deal.
