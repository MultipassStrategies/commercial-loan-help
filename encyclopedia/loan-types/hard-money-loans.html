Hard Money Commercial Loans: When Traditional Lending Will Not Work

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Interest rates: 10% to 15%+
  • Max LTV: 65% to 70%
  • Term: 6 to 24 months
  • Origination fee: 2 to 5 points
  • Closing speed: 3 to 14 days
  • Underwriting: Asset-based

What Is a Hard Money Loan?

A hard money loan is a short-term, asset-based financing instrument secured by real estate. Unlike conventional bank loans, which rely heavily on the borrower's creditworthiness, income history, and financial documentation, hard money loans are underwritten primarily on the value of the collateral property. The lender's central question is simple: if the borrower defaults, can the property be sold quickly enough and at a high enough price to recover the loan balance plus costs?

Hard money lenders are typically private individuals, small investment groups, or specialized funds rather than banks or institutional lenders. This private capital structure is what gives hard money its defining characteristics: speed, flexibility, and a willingness to finance deals that fall outside the narrow parameters of conventional lending. Because these lenders are not bound by the same regulatory frameworks that govern banks, they can make lending decisions in days rather than weeks or months.

The trade-off for this speed and flexibility is cost. Hard money loans carry significantly higher interest rates (typically 10% to 15% or more) and origination fees (2 to 5 points) compared to bank financing. They are also short-term by nature, with most terms ranging from 6 to 24 months. Hard money is not designed to be permanent financing. It is a bridge tool, meant to get a borrower from one point to another: from acquisition to stabilization, from purchase to renovation, or from an immediate opportunity to a position where conventional financing becomes available.

Hard Money vs. Bridge Loans

The terms "hard money" and "bridge loan" are sometimes used interchangeably, but they refer to different segments of the short-term lending market. Understanding the distinction helps borrowers choose the right financing for their situation.

Feature Hard Money Bridge Loan
Lender type Private individuals, small funds Institutional lenders, debt funds, banks

Origination fee 2 to 5 points 1 to 2 points
Closing speed 3 to 14 days 14 to 30 days

Bridge loans are a broader category of short-term financing that includes institutional lenders with dedicated bridge lending programs. These lenders offer somewhat lower rates and longer terms, but they require more documentation, more borrower scrutiny, and more time to close. Hard money sits at the far end of the speed-and-flexibility spectrum: the fastest close, the least documentation, and the highest cost. Many experienced investors use hard money to acquire a property quickly and then refinance into a lower-cost bridge loan or permanent financing once the deal is stabilized.

When Hard Money Makes Sense

Hard money is not the right tool for every deal, but there are specific scenarios where it is the best (or only) viable option. Understanding these situations helps borrowers deploy hard money strategically rather than as a last resort.

  • Credit challenges. Borrowers with low credit scores, recent foreclosures, or other blemishes that disqualify them from bank financing can still access hard money because the underwriting focuses on the property, not the borrower's credit history.
  • Speed is critical. When a deal must close in days rather than weeks (auction purchases, time-sensitive acquisitions, competitive bidding situations), hard money is often the only financing that can move fast enough.
  • Unusual properties. Banks tend to avoid properties that do not fit neatly into their underwriting boxes: mixed-use buildings with non-conforming layouts, properties with environmental issues, vacant buildings with no income history, or raw land. Hard money lenders evaluate the collateral on its own merits.
  • Construction with no track record. First-time developers or borrowers without a verifiable construction history often cannot qualify for bank construction loans. Hard money lenders are more willing to fund these projects if the numbers work and the borrower has sufficient equity.
  • Fix-and-flip projects. Short-term renovation projects that aim to buy, improve, and sell a property within 6 to 12 months are a natural fit for hard money's short-term structure. The loan term aligns with the project timeline, and the profit from the sale covers the higher financing cost.
  • Auction purchases. Real estate auctions often require proof of funds or rapid closing (sometimes within 24 to 72 hours). Hard money lenders can provide the proof of funds letter or the actual capital needed to complete the transaction on that timeline.
  • Gap financing. When a borrower has permanent financing approved but needs a short-term capital injection to close the gap (for example, covering renovation costs before a take-out loan funds), hard money can serve as interim financing.

Rate and Fee Structure

Hard money is expensive relative to bank financing, but the cost structure is straightforward once you understand the components. Borrowers should evaluate hard money on an all-in cost basis rather than fixating on any single fee.

  • Interest rate: 10% to 15%+ annually. Some lenders quote rates monthly (for example, 1% per month equals 12% annually). Rates vary based on the property type, loan-to-value ratio, borrower experience, and the lender's cost of capital.
  • Origination fee: 2 to 5 points, charged at closing. One "point" equals 1% of the loan amount. On a $500,000 loan, 3 points equals $15,000.
  • Processing/underwriting fee: $500 to $2,500, covering the lender's administrative costs for evaluating the deal.
  • Appraisal or BPO fee: $500 to $3,000, depending on the property type and whether a full appraisal or broker price opinion is required.
  • Legal/document preparation fee: $500 to $1,500 for loan document drafting and review.
  • Exit fee: Some lenders charge 0.5% to 1% of the outstanding loan balance at payoff. Not all lenders charge this, so it is worth negotiating.
  • Extension fee: If the borrower needs additional time beyond the original term, most lenders charge 0.5% to 1% per month for extensions. Having a clear exit strategy helps avoid these costs.

All-In Cost Example

Consider a $500,000 hard money loan at 12% interest with 3 points origination, held for 9 months:

  • Interest: $500,000 x 12% x (9/12) = $45,000
  • Origination: $500,000 x 3% = $15,000
  • Processing, appraisal, legal: approximately $3,500
  • Total cost: approximately $63,500
  • Annualized cost of capital: roughly 17% when all fees are included

This all-in calculation is the number that matters when comparing hard money to other financing options or evaluating whether a deal's profit margin justifies the cost.

Qualification: It Is About the Asset

The defining characteristic of hard money underwriting is its focus on the collateral rather than the borrower. While banks spend weeks analyzing tax returns, profit-and-loss statements, and personal financial histories, hard money lenders concentrate on answering one fundamental question: what is the property worth, and how does it compare to the loan amount?

  • Loan-to-value (LTV) is the primary metric. Most hard money lenders cap their exposure at 65% to 70% of the property's current value (or the purchase price, whichever is lower). For renovation projects, some lenders will lend based on the after-repair value (ARV), but at a lower LTV ratio, typically 60% to 65% of ARV.
  • Property valuation methods. Hard money lenders use appraisals, broker price opinions (BPOs), or internal valuations to determine property value. BPOs are faster and less expensive than full appraisals, which is one reason hard money can close so quickly.
  • No minimum credit score. Most hard money lenders do not impose a minimum FICO requirement. Some will pull credit to assess overall risk, but a low score alone will not disqualify a deal if the property and equity position are strong.
  • Borrower experience is helpful but not required. Lenders may offer better terms (lower rates, higher leverage) to experienced investors with a track record of successful projects. First-time borrowers can still qualify, but they should expect slightly more conservative terms.
  • Skin in the game. With a maximum LTV of 65% to 70%, borrowers must bring 30% to 35% equity to the table. This significant equity cushion is the lender's primary protection against loss and is non-negotiable for most hard money deals.

The asset-based underwriting model is both the greatest advantage and the defining limitation of hard money. It allows borrowers who cannot qualify for bank financing to access capital, but it also means that the property itself must be strong enough to support the loan on its own merits.

Common CRE Uses for Hard Money

Hard money serves a variety of purposes in commercial real estate, particularly for investors and developers who need speed, flexibility, or financing that falls outside conventional lending parameters.

  • Fix-and-flip. Purchasing a commercial property below market value, renovating it to improve its condition and functionality, and selling it at a profit. Hard money's short-term structure aligns naturally with the 6-to-12-month timeline typical of most flip projects.
  • Land loans. Banks are notoriously reluctant to finance raw land due to the lack of income-producing capacity and the difficulty of establishing reliable collateral values. Hard money lenders will fund land acquisitions at conservative LTVs (often 50% or less) for borrowers who have a clear development plan and timeline.
  • Construction financing. For borrowers who cannot qualify for bank construction loans (due to limited experience, credit issues, or unconventional project types), hard money provides an alternative path to fund ground-up construction or major renovations.
  • Distressed acquisitions. Properties in foreclosure, short sale, or bankruptcy often require rapid closing and cash-equivalent terms. Hard money enables investors to move quickly on these opportunities, which are often priced below market value precisely because they require speed and flexibility that most buyers cannot provide.
  • Bridge to stabilization. Acquiring a vacant or underperforming commercial property, leasing it up or repositioning it, and then refinancing into permanent financing once the property meets conventional lending standards.
  • Entitlement plays. Purchasing a property with the intention of securing zoning changes, permits, or approvals that increase its value, then selling or refinancing based on the improved entitlements.
  • Note purchases. Investors who buy distressed debt instruments (non-performing mortgage notes) sometimes use hard money to finance the acquisition, with the underlying real estate serving as collateral.

Risks and Downsides

Hard money solves real problems, but borrowers must enter these transactions with a clear understanding of the risks involved. The cost of getting a hard money deal wrong can be severe.

  • High cost of capital. With rates of 10% to 15%+ and origination fees of 2 to 5 points, hard money is among the most expensive forms of real estate financing available. If a project takes longer than expected or generates less profit than projected, the carrying costs can erode or eliminate the return.
  • Short timeline pressure. Most hard money loans mature in 6 to 24 months. If the borrower cannot execute their business plan (renovation, lease-up, sale, or refinance) within that window, they face extension fees, potential default, or the need to scramble for alternative financing under pressure.
  • Default risk. Because hard money lenders lend against collateral, they are prepared to foreclose if the borrower defaults. The foreclosure process can be swift, especially if the loan documents include a deed of trust with a power-of-sale clause. Borrowers who fall behind may lose the property and their equity investment.
  • Predatory lenders. The hard money market is largely unregulated compared to bank lending. Some lenders engage in predatory practices, including hidden fees, bait-and-switch rate structures, or terms designed to make default more likely so the lender can seize the property. Due diligence on the lender is just as important as due diligence on the deal.
  • No long-term hold option. Hard money is not designed for buy-and-hold investors. If the exit strategy fails and the borrower cannot sell or refinance before the loan matures, there is no option to simply extend the loan indefinitely. The lender will either charge significant extension fees or begin foreclosure proceedings.
  • Personal guarantee often required. Despite the asset-based underwriting model, many hard money lenders require a personal guarantee from the borrower, meaning personal assets beyond the collateral property are at risk in the event of a shortfall after liquidation.

Evaluating Hard Money Lenders: Red Flags

Not all hard money lenders operate with integrity. Because this segment of the lending market has less regulatory oversight than bank lending, borrowers must be their own advocates when evaluating potential lenders.

Red Flags to Watch For

  • Upfront fees before commitment. Legitimate hard money lenders do not require large upfront fees before issuing a formal loan commitment. If a lender asks for thousands of dollars in "processing fees" or "due diligence deposits" before you have a term sheet, walk away. Small application fees ($500 to $1,000 for appraisal or credit check) are reasonable; large non-refundable deposits are not.
  • No physical office or verifiable presence. A lender with no physical address, no website with real team profiles, and no verifiable track record is a significant risk. Legitimate hard money lenders are willing to meet in person, provide references, and show evidence of past transactions.
  • Unclear or shifting terms. If the terms of the loan keep changing between initial discussion and closing, or if the lender is vague about fees, prepayment structures, or default provisions, treat these as warning signs. All terms should be clearly documented in a written term sheet before you spend money on appraisals or legal fees.
  • Bait-and-switch rates. Some lenders advertise low rates to attract borrowers, then increase the rate at the last minute citing "market conditions" or "additional risk factors." A reputable lender stands behind their quoted terms unless material new information emerges during due diligence.
  • No references or verifiable closings. Any established hard money lender should be able to provide references from borrowers, title companies, or attorneys who have worked with them. If a lender refuses to provide references or cannot document recent closings, consider it a disqualifying factor.

What to Look For in a Reliable Lender

  • Transparent fee structure. All costs are disclosed upfront in a written term sheet, with no hidden fees or surprise charges at closing.
  • Proven track record. The lender can demonstrate a history of completed transactions, ideally with references you can contact.
  • Fast, responsive communication. Hard money is a speed product. If a lender takes days to return calls or respond to emails during the pre-approval phase, their performance during the closing process is unlikely to be better.
  • Willingness to show proof of funds. Reputable lenders are willing to demonstrate that they have the capital available to fund your loan. This is particularly important with smaller private lenders who may be dependent on raising capital from investors for each deal.

Exit Strategy Is Everything

If there is one principle that defines successful hard money borrowing, it is this: the exit strategy must be clear, realistic, and in place before the loan closes. Hard money lenders know this, which is why the exit plan is often the single most important factor in their lending decision after property value.

There are three primary exit strategies for hard money loans:

  1. Refinance to permanent financing. The most common exit. The borrower uses the hard money loan to acquire and improve a property, then refinances into a conventional mortgage, CMBS loan, or agency financing once the property is stabilized and meets bank underwriting standards. This strategy requires that the borrower have a realistic path to stabilization within the loan term and that market conditions support the anticipated permanent financing terms.
  2. Sell the asset. For fix-and-flip projects and other short-term plays, the exit is a property sale. The sale proceeds pay off the hard money loan, and the borrower keeps the profit. This strategy depends on accurate projections of the after-repair value, a realistic renovation timeline, and sufficient demand in the target market to support a timely sale at the projected price.
  3. Bring in a partner or additional capital. Some borrowers use hard money to acquire a property quickly, then bring in an equity partner or additional investor capital to pay off the hard money loan and reposition the deal for long-term ownership. This approach works well when the borrower has deal sourcing and management skills but needs additional capital to move beyond the short-term financing phase.

The worst position a hard money borrower can be in is approaching the loan maturity date without a viable exit. At that point, the borrower faces extension fees (if the lender agrees to extend), a forced sale at a disadvantageous price, or foreclosure. Every hard money deal should include a primary exit strategy, a backup plan, and a realistic timeline that accounts for potential delays in renovation, leasing, or the sale process.

Each financing type serves a different purpose in the capital stack. Experienced commercial real estate investors often use all three at different stages of a project: hard money for initial acquisition when speed is paramount, a bridge loan during the stabilization phase, and a permanent bank loan for the long-term hold. The key is matching the financing to the phase of the project and ensuring that each transition point (from hard money to bridge, or from bridge to bank) is planned and achievable within the loan term.

Frequently Asked Questions

Bridge loans are a broader category that includes institutional lenders offering short-term financing at moderate rates (typically 7% to 10%) with more documentation requirements. Hard money is a subset of bridge lending that refers specifically to private, individual, or small-fund lenders who prioritize speed and collateral value above all else. Hard money rates are typically higher (10% to 15%+) and terms are shorter, but the trade-off is significantly faster closing (sometimes within days) and minimal borrower qualification requirements.

Many deals start with hard money for immediate funding and then refinance into a lower-cost bridge or permanent loan once the property is stabilized. The choice between hard money and a traditional bridge loan depends on how quickly you need to close, how much documentation you can provide, and whether your borrower profile meets institutional lender requirements. If speed is the top priority and you are willing to pay a premium for it, hard money is the right tool. If you have more time and can satisfy moderate documentation requirements, a bridge loan will likely save you money.

Most hard money lenders do not have a minimum credit score requirement because their underwriting is based primarily on the value of the collateral (the property) rather than the borrower's personal financial profile. That said, some lenders will review your credit report to assess overall risk, and borrowers with recent foreclosures or bankruptcies may face higher rates or lower leverage.

The key factors for hard money approval are the property's value and condition, the strength of the exit strategy, and the borrower's equity in the deal (typically 30% to 35% or more). If your credit is a major issue, focus your application on the strength of the property, the clarity of your business plan, and your ability to bring sufficient equity to the table. These factors carry far more weight in hard money underwriting than your FICO score.

Hard money loans can close in as few as 3 to 5 business days for straightforward deals where the lender has existing familiarity with the property type and market. More typical timelines range from 7 to 14 days. The speed advantage comes from simplified underwriting: hard money lenders rely on a property valuation (often a broker price opinion rather than a full appraisal) and a basic title search, skipping the extensive documentation, committee reviews, and regulatory checks that slow down bank loans.

To achieve the fastest closing, have your entity documents, proof of funds for the down payment, and a clear scope of work (if renovation is involved) ready before you apply. Working with a title company that can expedite the title search and close on short notice also helps. Some borrowers maintain ongoing relationships with hard money lenders specifically so they can move quickly when opportunities arise, since a lender who already knows your track record can skip parts of the vetting process and fund even faster.

Hard money fees typically include an origination fee of 2 to 5 points (2% to 5% of the loan amount), which is charged at closing. Beyond the origination fee, many lenders charge a processing or underwriting fee ($500 to $2,500), an appraisal or BPO fee ($500 to $3,000 depending on property type), and legal document preparation fees. Some lenders also charge exit fees (0.5% to 1% of the loan balance at payoff) or extension fees (0.5% to 1% per month if you need additional time beyond the original term).

When comparing lenders, always calculate the total all-in cost including all fees and interest over your expected hold period, not just the headline rate. A lender offering 11% with 4 points may be more expensive than one offering 13% with 2 points if your hold period is short. Ask each lender for a complete fee schedule in writing before committing, and pay close attention to exit fees and extension fees, which can add significant cost if your project timeline shifts.

It depends entirely on the deal economics. Hard money is expensive, but the relevant question is whether the profit opportunity justifies the cost. For example, if you are acquiring a distressed commercial property for $500,000 with a clear path to a $750,000 after-repair value, paying 12% interest and 3 points on a 12-month hard money loan costs roughly $48,000 in interest plus $15,000 in points. If your renovation costs $100,000, your total investment is $665,000 against a $750,000 value, yielding $85,000 in equity creation. In this scenario, the hard money cost is a reasonable price for the speed and flexibility that made the deal possible.

Hard money makes less sense for stable, cash-flowing properties where cheaper financing is available, or for projects where the profit margin is too thin to absorb the higher carrying costs. Before committing to hard money, build a detailed pro forma that accounts for all financing costs (interest, points, fees, potential extension fees) alongside renovation expenses, holding costs, and a realistic sale or refinance timeline. If the numbers still work with a buffer for unexpected delays or cost overruns, hard money can be a powerful tool. If the deal only pencils with everything going perfectly, the risk may not be worth the reward.
