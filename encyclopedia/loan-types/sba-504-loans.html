SBA 504 Loans: Fixed Rates, Low Down Payment, and How to Apply

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 15, 2026
Key Facts
  • Typical loan amount: $500K to $5M (CDC portion)
  • Down payment: 10%
  • Rate type: Fixed, below market
  • Max term: 10 or 20 years
  • Job creation requirement: 1 per $65K
  • Structure: 50/40/10 split

What Is the SBA 504 Loan Program?

The SBA 504 loan program is a federally backed financing vehicle designed to help small businesses acquire major fixed assets, primarily commercial real estate and heavy equipment. Administered by the U.S. Small Business Administration in partnership with Certified Development Companies (CDCs), the program provides long-term, fixed-rate financing at below-market interest rates. The central purpose of the 504 program is to promote economic development and job creation by making it easier for small businesses to invest in the physical infrastructure they need to grow.

Congress created the 504 program in 1980 as part of the Small Business Investment Act, building on the SBA's existing mission to support American small businesses. The program was designed to fill a gap in the conventional lending market: many small business owners could not secure affordable long-term financing for real estate and equipment because banks viewed these loans as too risky or required down payments that were out of reach. By introducing a government-backed second lien and reducing the borrower's equity requirement to just 10%, the 504 program opened the door for thousands of businesses that would otherwise have been shut out of commercial real estate ownership.

Since its inception, the 504 program has facilitated tens of billions of dollars in small business lending. It remains one of the SBA's most popular programs because it directly addresses the two biggest barriers to commercial property ownership: high down payments and unpredictable interest rates. Because the CDC portion of the loan carries a fixed rate tied to U.S. Treasury bond yields, borrowers gain long-term certainty over a significant portion of their debt service, which is especially valuable during periods of rising interest rates.

The Unique 50/40/10 Structure Explained

The defining feature of the SBA 504 loan is its three-layer financing structure, commonly referred to as the 50/40/10 split. This structure divides the total project cost among three parties: a conventional lender, a Certified Development Company, and the borrower. Each layer serves a distinct role, and understanding how they interact is essential to grasping why the 504 program is so advantageous for small businesses.

First Mortgage: The Bank's 50%

The first layer is a conventional first-mortgage loan from a bank or credit union, covering approximately 50% of the total project cost. This lender holds the senior lien position, meaning it has first claim on the property in the event of a default. Because the bank's exposure is limited to half the project value (rather than the 70% to 80% typical of a conventional commercial loan), the lender faces significantly reduced risk. This lower risk profile often translates into more favorable terms for the borrower on the bank's portion, including competitive interest rates and longer amortization periods. The first-mortgage rate may be fixed or variable, depending on the bank's lending policies and the borrower's preference.

CDC Debenture: The SBA-Backed 40%

The second layer is a debenture issued by a Certified Development Company, covering up to 40% of the total project cost. This is the portion backed by the SBA's guarantee, and it is what makes the 504 program unique. The CDC debenture is a fully amortizing, fixed-rate loan with a term of 10, 20, or 25 years. The interest rate is set at the time the debenture is pooled and sold to investors, and it is pegged to the prevailing yield on U.S. Treasury bonds plus a small spread. Because these debentures carry the full faith and credit of the U.S. government, investors accept very low yields, which translates directly into below-market fixed rates for borrowers. The CDC debenture sits in a second-lien position behind the bank's first mortgage.

Borrower Equity: The 10% Down Payment

The third layer is the borrower's equity injection, which is just 10% of the total project cost in most cases. This is dramatically lower than the 20% to 30% down payment that conventional commercial lenders typically require. The reduced equity requirement allows business owners to preserve working capital for operations, hiring, and other growth investments. In certain situations the down payment increases to 15% (for startups operating fewer than two years, or for special-purpose properties) or 20% (for startups purchasing special-purpose properties). Even at the higher thresholds, the 504 program remains one of the most affordable entry points into commercial real estate ownership.

Who Qualifies for an SBA 504 Loan?

The SBA 504 program targets small, for-profit businesses operating within the United States. Eligibility is determined by several financial and operational criteria, all of which are designed to ensure the program serves its intended purpose of supporting genuinely small enterprises.

  • Net worth limit: The business must have a tangible net worth of less than $15 million.
  • Net income limit: Average net income must be under $5 million for the two full fiscal years prior to the application.
  • For-profit status: The business must be organized as a for-profit entity. Nonprofits and government entities are not eligible.
  • U.S. operations: The business must operate within the United States or its territories.
  • Owner-occupancy requirement: For existing buildings, the borrower must occupy at least 51% of the total usable square footage. For new construction, the threshold is 60%. This requirement ensures the program supports operational businesses rather than passive investors.
  • Management expertise: The borrower should demonstrate relevant management experience and a sound business plan.
  • Good character: All owners with 20% or more equity must pass an SBA background check with no recent bankruptcies, criminal history, or delinquent federal debt.

Certain industries are excluded from 504 eligibility, including lending and investment firms, speculative real estate developers, gambling enterprises, and businesses engaged in activities the SBA considers inconsistent with public policy. Passive real estate holding companies also do not qualify; the borrower's operating business must be the primary occupant of the property.

Eligible Property Types and Uses

SBA 504 loan proceeds may be used for a specific set of fixed-asset purposes. The program is intentionally focused on long-term capital investments that promote business stability and growth.

  • Purchase of land and existing buildings: Acquiring commercial real estate for business operations is the most common use of 504 financing.
  • New construction: Building a new facility from the ground up, including site preparation, grading, and utility installation.
  • Renovation and modernization: Improving or converting existing buildings to meet the needs of the borrower's business, including major renovations, expansions, and retrofits.
  • Long-life equipment: Purchasing machinery and equipment with a useful life of at least 10 years. This provision is particularly valuable for manufacturers, printers, and other capital-intensive businesses.
  • Soft costs: Eligible soft costs include professional fees (appraisals, environmental studies, architectural plans), title insurance, and closing costs directly related to the 504 project.

There are important restrictions on what 504 funds cannot be used for. The program does not cover working capital, inventory purchases, debt consolidation (outside the dedicated refinance program), or short-lived assets. Rental properties where the borrower is not the primary occupant are also excluded. The intent is to channel 504 dollars exclusively into long-term assets that will anchor a business in its community for years to come.

Current Rates and How They Are Set

One of the greatest advantages of the SBA 504 program is the way interest rates are determined on the CDC debenture portion. Unlike conventional commercial loans, where rates are set by individual banks and influenced by risk premiums, the CDC debenture rate is derived from the yield on U.S. Treasury bonds. This mechanism consistently produces below-market fixed rates, which is why savvy business owners seek out 504 financing even when other options are available.

The rate-setting process works as follows. CDCs across the country originate 504 debentures on an ongoing basis. These individual debentures are then pooled together and sold as government-backed securities to institutional investors. The rate on each pool is fixed at the time of sale, which occurs monthly. The effective rate to the borrower includes the Treasury bond yield plus a spread that covers the CDC's servicing fee (typically around 0.625%), an SBA guarantee fee, and a small funding fee. Even with these additions, the all-in rate on the CDC portion is nearly always lower than what a borrower would pay on a conventional fixed-rate commercial mortgage.

The 20-year term is the most popular choice for real estate acquisitions because it combines a long amortization period with favorable fixed pricing. The 25-year option, introduced in 2018, further reduces monthly payments and is increasingly popular for larger projects where cash flow optimization is a priority. The 10-year term is typically reserved for equipment purchases or situations where a shorter repayment period is advantageous. Because rates fluctuate monthly with Treasury yields, the exact rate a borrower receives depends on when the debenture is pooled, not when the loan application is submitted.

The CDC Role Explained

Certified Development Companies are the backbone of the SBA 504 program. A CDC is a nonprofit corporation certified by the SBA to promote economic development within a defined geographic area. CDCs serve as the bridge between borrowers, conventional lenders, and the SBA itself, handling much of the application processing, underwriting, and loan servicing for the 504 debenture portion.

There are approximately 230 CDCs operating throughout the United States. Some serve a single state or metropolitan area, while others have been authorized to operate nationwide. Each CDC must meet strict SBA certification requirements, including maintaining a professional staff with lending expertise, demonstrating a track record of community development activity, and submitting to regular SBA audits. CDCs earn revenue primarily through servicing fees on the debentures they originate, which aligns their incentives with long-term loan performance rather than volume alone.

In the 504 process, the CDC's responsibilities include evaluating the borrower's eligibility, preparing the loan application for SBA review, coordinating with the first-mortgage lender, ordering appraisals and environmental reports, and ensuring compliance with all SBA regulations. After closing, the CDC continues to service the debenture for its full term, collecting payments and monitoring the loan. For borrowers, working with a knowledgeable CDC can make the difference between a smooth process and a frustrating one. You can locate a CDC through the SBA's lender match tool at sba.gov, by contacting your local SBA district office, or by using a matching service like Commercial Loan Help to connect with CDCs experienced in your property type and market.

Application Process: Step by Step

Applying for an SBA 504 loan involves more documentation and coordination than a conventional commercial mortgage, but the payoff in terms of rate and down payment savings is substantial. Here is a step-by-step overview of what to expect from initial inquiry through closing.

  1. Initial inquiry and prequalification. The borrower contacts a CDC or a lending marketplace to discuss the project. The CDC performs a preliminary review of the borrower's financials, the proposed property, and the overall feasibility of the deal. This stage helps identify any obvious disqualifiers before significant time and money are invested.
  2. Conventional lender engagement. Because the 504 structure requires a first-mortgage lender, the borrower (often with the CDC's help) secures a commitment from a bank or credit union willing to provide the 50% first lien. Some CDCs have established relationships with banks that regularly participate in 504 deals, which can streamline this step considerably.
  3. Full application package. The borrower submits a detailed application to the CDC, including three years of business and personal tax returns, interim financial statements, a business plan or projection, personal financial statements for all owners with 20% or more equity, a resume or background summary for key principals, and information about the property (purchase agreement, lease details, or construction plans).
  4. Third-party reports. The CDC orders an independent appraisal, an environmental site assessment (Phase I, and Phase II if warranted), and a title search. These reports protect all parties and are required by SBA regulations.
  5. CDC underwriting and committee review. The CDC's loan officer underwrites the deal, analyzing cash flow, collateral, creditworthiness, and compliance with SBA eligibility rules. The completed package is then presented to the CDC's internal loan committee for approval.
  6. SBA authorization. Once the CDC approves the loan, it submits the application to the SBA's loan processing center for final review and authorization. The SBA evaluates the deal independently and, if approved, issues an authorization letter detailing the terms and conditions of the debenture.
  7. Closing and disbursement. After SBA authorization, the closing process begins. The first-mortgage lender typically funds first, followed by the borrower's equity injection. The CDC debenture is funded on an interim basis (often through a bridge loan) until the debenture is pooled and sold, at which point the permanent fixed rate is locked in.
  8. Debenture pooling and permanent funding. Debentures are pooled and sold to investors on a monthly cycle. Once the pool sale is complete, the interim financing is retired and the borrower's permanent fixed rate takes effect. From this point forward, the borrower makes monthly payments to the CDC at the locked-in rate for the full term of the debenture.

The entire process typically takes 60 to 90 days from complete application to closing, though timelines can vary depending on the complexity of the project, the responsiveness of the borrower, and the workloads of the CDC and SBA processing centers. Experienced CDCs and lenders can often accelerate the timeline for straightforward deals.

The 504 Refinance Program

In addition to new acquisitions and construction, the SBA 504 program includes a dedicated refinance option that allows business owners to replace existing commercial debt with long-term, fixed-rate 504 financing. This refinance program was made permanent by Congress in 2016 after several years of temporary authorizations, giving borrowers a reliable pathway to improve their debt terms.

To qualify for a 504 refinance, the existing debt must have been used for a 504-eligible purpose (such as purchasing or improving a commercial property), and the loan must have been current for the 12 months preceding the application. The borrower must still meet all standard 504 eligibility criteria, including the owner-occupancy requirement and the net worth and income limits. The refinance can include up to 20% of the appraised property value for eligible business expenses, providing a limited cash-out component that many borrowers use to fund renovations, equipment upgrades, or other capital needs.

The refinance program is especially attractive in environments where businesses originally financed properties with variable-rate loans or short-term balloon notes. By converting to a 504 fixed-rate debenture, borrowers eliminate interest rate risk and gain predictable monthly payments for up to 25 years. For businesses facing loan maturities with unfavorable renewal terms, the 504 refinance can be a powerful tool to stabilize cash flow and reduce overall borrowing costs.

Advantages Over Conventional Financing

The SBA 504 program offers several distinct advantages that set it apart from conventional commercial mortgages and other financing options.

  • Lower down payment. At just 10% equity (compared to 20% to 30% for conventional loans), the 504 program preserves working capital and makes commercial real estate ownership accessible to businesses that might otherwise be unable to invest in property.
  • Below-market fixed rates. Because the CDC debenture is backed by the U.S. government and priced off Treasury yields, borrowers consistently receive fixed rates lower than those available from banks on conventional commercial mortgages.
  • Long-term stability. With fully amortizing terms of 10, 20, or 25 years and no balloon payments on the CDC portion, borrowers gain certainty over their largest fixed cost. This eliminates the refinancing risk that comes with conventional loans that typically mature in 5 to 10 years.
  • Lower monthly payments. The combination of a low fixed rate and a long amortization period produces monthly payments that are often significantly lower than comparable conventional financing, freeing up cash flow for business operations and growth.
  • Preservation of banking relationships. Because the bank's first mortgage covers only 50% of the project (rather than 70% to 80%), the lender's risk is lower, and the borrower's existing credit lines and banking relationships are less likely to be strained.
  • No future rate surprises. The fixed rate on the CDC debenture is locked for the full loan term, protecting the borrower from rising interest rates over the life of the loan.

Limitations and Considerations

While the SBA 504 program offers significant benefits, it is not the right fit for every situation. Borrowers should weigh several limitations before proceeding.

  • Owner-occupancy requirement. The 51% (or 60% for new construction) occupancy requirement means the 504 program is not available for purely investment properties. Borrowers who plan to lease the majority of their space to tenants will need to look at other financing options.
  • Prepayment penalty on the CDC portion. The CDC debenture carries a declining prepayment penalty during the first half of the loan term. For a 20-year debenture, the penalty starts at approximately 10% of the outstanding balance and decreases by 1% each year for the first 10 years, after which no penalty applies. Borrowers who anticipate selling or refinancing within the first several years should factor this cost into their planning.
  • Processing time. The multi-party structure of the 504 loan (involving the borrower, a bank, a CDC, and the SBA) typically requires 60 to 90 days or more from application to closing. Borrowers with time-sensitive transactions may find this timeline challenging compared to conventional loans that can close in 30 to 45 days.
  • Job creation rules. The requirement to create or retain one job per $65,000 in debenture funding can be a concern for capital-intensive businesses that do not plan to hire aggressively. While there are alternative ways to satisfy this requirement (through community development or public policy goals), borrowers should confirm their ability to comply before applying.
  • Not for working capital or inventory. The 504 program is strictly limited to fixed assets. Businesses that need financing for day-to-day operations, inventory, or short-term needs must seek other products, such as an SBA 7(a) loan or a conventional line of credit.
  • Personal guarantee required. All owners with 20% or more equity in the business must provide a personal guarantee on the 504 debenture, meaning personal assets are at risk in the event of default.
  • SBA fees. The CDC portion includes an upfront guarantee fee (approximately 1.7% of the debenture amount) and ongoing servicing fees that are built into the interest rate. While these costs are generally offset by the below-market rate, they should be factored into the total cost comparison.

Your CDC can help you determine exactly how much equity will be required based on your specific business history and the type of property you are acquiring.

The SBA 504 program requires that each project create or retain one job for every $65,000 in SBA debenture funding provided. For small manufacturers (businesses with NAICS codes in the 31-33 range), the threshold is more generous at one job per $100,000. These jobs must generally be created or retained within two years of the loan being fully disbursed, and the CDC will track compliance as part of its ongoing servicing responsibilities.

While the job creation requirement may sound restrictive, the SBA provides several alternative ways to meet it. Borrowers can satisfy the requirement by demonstrating that the project achieves community development goals, such as improving the local tax base, expanding services in underserved or rural areas, reducing business costs in economically distressed regions, or contributing to neighborhood revitalization. Projects that advance public policy goals (such as energy efficiency improvements, renewable energy adoption, or the expansion of exports) can also qualify under these alternatives.

In practice, many businesses naturally meet the job creation threshold through normal operations and growth. The CDC will help you project and document your job creation plan as part of the application, and most borrowers find this requirement manageable. If your project is capital-intensive with limited hiring plans, discuss the community development and public policy alternatives with your CDC early in the process to confirm that you can satisfy the SBA's requirements through one of these alternative pathways.

Yes, SBA 504 loans can absolutely be used for mixed-use properties, and this is a common scenario for small businesses that want to own their building while generating rental income from additional tenants. The key requirement is that the borrower's own business must occupy at least 51% of the total usable square footage for existing buildings, or at least 60% for new construction projects. Mixed-use arrangements are popular among business owners who want to offset their mortgage costs with rental income. For example, a medical practice might purchase a two-story building, occupy the ground floor for its clinic, and lease the upper floor to another healthcare provider. Similarly, a retailer might buy a building with a storefront on the main level and apartment units above. As long as the borrower's business meets the occupancy threshold, the rental income from additional tenants can actually strengthen the loan application by improving the property's overall cash flow profile.

When evaluating a mixed-use property, the SBA and the CDC will carefully review floor plans, lease agreements, and occupancy calculations to confirm that the borrower's business genuinely occupies the required percentage of space. It is important to note that the borrower cannot simply be the landlord. The business must actively operate from the property as its primary place of business. If you are considering a mixed-use deal, work closely with your CDC to ensure the property layout and tenant mix satisfy all SBA requirements before moving forward with the purchase.
