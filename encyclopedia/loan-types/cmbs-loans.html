CMBS Loans: How Conduit Lending Works for Commercial Real Estate

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Typical loan size: $2M to $50M+
  • Max LTV: 75%
  • Min DSCR: 1.25x
  • Term options: 5, 7, or 10 years
  • Rate type: Fixed (Treasury spread)
  • Amortization: 25 to 30 years

What Is a CMBS Loan?

A CMBS loan is a commercial mortgage that is originated by a lender and then pooled with other commercial mortgages into a bond (a commercial mortgage-backed security) that is sold to investors on the secondary market. The acronym stands for Commercial Mortgage-Backed Securities, and the loans themselves are often called "conduit loans" because the originating lender acts as a conduit, packaging the debt and passing it through to the capital markets rather than holding it on its own balance sheet.

The concept behind CMBS is straightforward. A lender originates dozens or hundreds of individual commercial real estate loans. Those loans are transferred into a trust, and the trust issues bonds backed by the combined cash flows from all the underlying mortgages. Institutional investors (pension funds, insurance companies, mutual funds, hedge funds) purchase those bonds, and the proceeds flow back to the original lender, freeing up capital to originate more loans. This cycle creates a large, liquid source of commercial real estate debt that does not depend on any single bank's balance sheet or appetite for real estate lending.

For borrowers, the practical result is access to fixed-rate, non-recourse financing at competitive rates for income-producing commercial properties. CMBS lenders evaluate deals primarily on the strength of the property's cash flow and the quality of the real estate, placing less emphasis on the borrower's personal financial statement or corporate balance sheet than a traditional bank would. This makes CMBS an especially attractive option for investors, sponsors, and property owners who want to separate their personal liability from their real estate holdings.

How CMBS Securitization Works

Understanding the securitization process helps borrowers appreciate why CMBS loans are structured the way they are, and why certain restrictions (like prepayment lockouts and rigid loan documents) exist. The process involves four main stages.

Origination

A CMBS lender (also called a conduit lender or loan originator) underwrites and closes individual commercial mortgage loans. These lenders are typically large investment banks, commercial banks with capital markets divisions, or specialized conduit lending shops. The lender evaluates each property based on its net operating income, occupancy, location, condition, and market fundamentals. Once the loan closes, it sits on the originator's warehouse line until enough loans have accumulated to form a pool.

Pooling

When the originator has assembled a sufficient volume of loans (typically $500 million to $1.5 billion or more), the loans are transferred into a special-purpose entity, usually structured as a real estate mortgage investment conduit (REMIC) trust. This trust is a bankruptcy-remote vehicle, meaning it is legally separated from the originator. If the originator goes bankrupt, the trust and its loans are unaffected. The trust holds the mortgages and collects the monthly payments from all the borrowers in the pool.

Tranching

The trust issues bonds in a series of classes, or "tranches," each carrying a different level of risk and return. Senior tranches (rated AAA or AA) receive payments first and bear the least risk; they offer the lowest yields but are protected by the subordinate tranches beneath them. Mezzanine tranches (rated A through BBB-) sit in the middle and absorb losses after the most junior classes are wiped out. The lowest tranche (the "first-loss piece" or B-piece) takes the first hit if any loans in the pool default; in exchange, it earns the highest yield. This waterfall structure allows the securitization to attract a broad range of investors with different risk appetites, from conservative insurance companies buying AAA bonds to opportunistic hedge funds buying B-pieces.

Servicing

Once the bonds are sold, the day-to-day management of the loan pool is handled by two types of servicers. The master servicer collects monthly payments from borrowers, manages escrow accounts, and handles routine administrative tasks. If a loan becomes troubled (missed payments, default, imminent maturity default), it is transferred to a special servicer, a separate firm with expertise in workout and distressed-debt resolution. The special servicer's job is to maximize recovery for the bondholders, whether through loan modifications, discounted payoffs, foreclosure, or property sales. This two-tier servicing structure is a defining characteristic of CMBS and has important implications for borrowers, particularly when problems arise.

Who Qualifies for CMBS Financing

CMBS underwriting is fundamentally property-driven. While the borrower's experience and track record matter, the primary focus is on whether the property generates enough income to reliably service the debt. This property-centric approach opens the door for a wider range of borrowers than traditional bank lending, but it also means the property itself must meet specific financial benchmarks.

Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR)

The single most important metric in CMBS underwriting is the debt service coverage ratio, which measures the property's net operating income (NOI) relative to the annual debt service (principal and interest payments). Most CMBS lenders require a minimum DSCR of 1.25x, meaning the property must generate at least $1.25 in NOI for every $1.00 of debt service. Stronger properties with stable tenants and long lease terms may qualify at slightly lower DSCRs, while riskier property types (such as hospitality or single-tenant retail) may require 1.35x or higher.

Loan-to-Value Ratio (LTV)

CMBS lenders typically cap their loan-to-value ratio at 75%, meaning the borrower needs at least 25% equity in the property. LTV is calculated using the appraised value of the property as determined by a third-party MAI appraisal. Some lenders will push to 80% LTV for exceptionally strong assets, but 75% is the standard ceiling for conduit loans. The LTV and DSCR constraints work together: the loan amount is set at whichever metric produces the lower proceeds.

Borrower Requirements

While CMBS is less borrower-dependent than bank lending, originators still evaluate the sponsor's background. Key borrower considerations include:

  • Real estate experience: Lenders prefer borrowers with a demonstrable track record of owning and managing commercial properties similar to the subject asset.
  • Net worth and liquidity: The carve-out guarantor (typically the principal or sponsor) must have a net worth at least equal to the loan amount and liquid assets equal to roughly 10% of the loan balance, though these thresholds vary by lender.
  • Clean background: Borrowers must pass background checks with no recent bankruptcies, foreclosures, or criminal history.
  • Single-purpose entity: The borrowing entity must be a newly formed, single-purpose entity (SPE) that owns no assets other than the subject property. This requirement protects the trust by preventing creditors of the borrower's other businesses from making claims against the collateral property.

Eligible Property Types

CMBS financing is available for most categories of income-producing commercial real estate. The key requirement is that the property must generate rental income or operating revenue sufficient to cover debt service. Properties that are owner-occupied or primarily dependent on the business operations of a single owner-operator (rather than tenant leases or guest revenue) are generally not suitable for CMBS.

  • Office: Suburban and urban office buildings with diversified tenant rosters are a core CMBS property type. Single-tenant office assets can qualify if the tenant is creditworthy and the lease term extends well beyond the loan maturity.
  • Retail: Anchored shopping centers, strip malls, and standalone retail properties with established tenants are commonly financed through CMBS. Grocery-anchored centers are particularly favored.
  • Multifamily: Apartment complexes with 50 or more units are eligible, though borrowers should compare CMBS terms against agency (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac) options, which often offer higher leverage and lower rates for qualifying multifamily assets.
  • Industrial: Warehouses, distribution centers, and flex industrial properties have become increasingly popular with CMBS lenders as e-commerce demand has driven strong fundamentals in the sector.
  • Hospitality: Hotels and resorts can be financed through CMBS, though underwriting is more conservative due to the higher revenue volatility inherent in hospitality. DSCR requirements are typically higher (1.35x to 1.50x), and lenders may require larger reserves.
  • Self-storage: Climate-controlled and drive-up self-storage facilities with stabilized occupancy are an accepted CMBS collateral type, often underwritten favorably due to the sector's strong historical performance.
  • Mixed-use: Properties combining two or more of the above uses (such as ground-floor retail with upper-floor office or apartments) qualify provided the overall property cash flow meets DSCR and LTV thresholds.

CMBS Rate Structure

CMBS loans carry fixed interest rates for the entire loan term, which is one of their primary attractions. The rate is calculated as a spread over the yield on U.S. Treasury securities with a maturity matching the loan term. For a 10-year CMBS loan (the most common term), the benchmark is the 10-year Treasury note. For 5-year and 7-year loans, the corresponding Treasury yields are used.

How the Spread Is Determined

The spread (measured in basis points, where 100 basis points equals 1%) reflects the risk premium that bond investors demand above the risk-free Treasury rate. Several factors influence the spread on any given loan:

  • Property quality and location: A Class A office building in a major metro will receive a tighter spread than a Class C retail strip in a secondary market.
  • Leverage: Lower LTV loans command tighter spreads because the equity cushion protects bondholders from losses.
  • DSCR: Higher coverage ratios indicate stronger cash flow relative to debt, which reduces risk and tightens the spread.
  • Property type: Stable property types like industrial and multifamily generally receive tighter spreads than volatile types like hospitality.
  • Market conditions: During periods of strong investor demand for CMBS bonds, spreads compress. During market stress or uncertainty, spreads widen as investors require higher compensation for risk.

Current Rate Environment

As of early 2026, 10-year CMBS conduit spreads for stabilized, institutional-quality properties generally range from 150 to 250 basis points over the 10-year Treasury. With the 10-year Treasury trading in the 4.00% to 4.50% range, all-in fixed rates for well-qualified borrowers are landing between approximately 5.50% and 7.00%, depending on property type, leverage, and overall deal strength. Spreads have tightened modestly from their 2023-2024 peaks as issuance volume has recovered and investor confidence in the sector has improved, though they remain wider than the pre-2022 lows.

Advantages of CMBS Loans

CMBS financing offers several distinct benefits that make it a preferred choice for many commercial real estate investors and sponsors.

  • Non-recourse structure: CMBS loans are non-recourse (subject to standard bad-boy carve-outs), meaning the borrower's personal assets are generally protected if the property fails to perform. This is a major advantage over bank loans, which frequently require full personal guarantees.
  • High leverage: With LTVs up to 75% (and occasionally 80% for strong assets), CMBS offers competitive leverage that allows borrowers to maximize their return on equity while keeping a significant portion of capital available for other investments.
  • Fixed-rate certainty: The fully fixed rate eliminates interest rate risk for the entire loan term, giving borrowers predictable debt service payments for budgeting and cash flow planning.
  • Flexible borrower requirements: Because underwriting is property-focused, CMBS is accessible to a broader range of borrowers, including foreign nationals, entities with complex ownership structures, and sponsors whose personal financial profiles might not satisfy a bank's relationship lending criteria.
  • Assumability: CMBS loans are typically assumable, allowing the borrower to transfer the loan to a qualified buyer when selling the property. In a rising rate environment, an assumable below-market-rate loan can increase the property's sale price and make it more attractive to buyers.
  • Large loan capacity: CMBS lenders can originate very large loans (routinely $50 million and above, and single-asset single-borrower deals exceeding $100 million) without the concentration risk limitations that constrain individual banks.
  • No ongoing financial covenants: Unlike bank loans, which often include annual financial covenants that can trigger a default even when the borrower is current on payments, CMBS loans generally do not impose ongoing financial performance tests after closing.

Limitations and Risks

CMBS loans come with structural rigidities and risks that borrowers must understand before committing. These limitations are inherent to the securitization model and cannot be negotiated away once the loan is pooled.

  • Prepayment lockout: Most CMBS loans include an initial lockout period (typically two years) during which the borrower cannot prepay the loan at all. This means you cannot refinance or sell the property free and clear of the mortgage during the lockout window, regardless of circumstances.
  • Defeasance or yield maintenance: After the lockout period, prepayment is permitted only through defeasance (replacing the loan collateral with a portfolio of U.S. Treasury securities that replicate the remaining payment stream) or yield maintenance (paying a premium that compensates the lender for lost interest). Both methods can be extremely expensive, particularly in a declining rate environment. Defeasance involves purchasing a custom bond portfolio and hiring a specialized consultant, which can add significant transaction costs.
  • Special servicing risk: If the property encounters financial difficulty, the loan is transferred from the master servicer to a special servicer whose primary obligation is to the bondholders. The special servicer may impose additional fees, and the workout process can be slow and adversarial. Borrowers have limited negotiating leverage once a loan enters special servicing.
  • Inflexible loan documents: CMBS loan documents are highly standardized because they must conform to the requirements of the rating agencies and bond investors. There is very little room to negotiate custom provisions, carve-outs, or modifications. Changes that a bank might readily agree to (such as releasing a parcel, modifying a lease approval threshold, or adjusting reserve requirements) are often impossible in a CMBS structure.
  • Reserve requirements: CMBS lenders typically require the borrower to fund several reserve accounts at closing, including reserves for property taxes, insurance, tenant improvements, leasing commissions, capital expenditures, and sometimes seasonal operating shortfalls. These reserves tie up cash that would otherwise be available to the borrower.
  • Supplemental loan limitations: While some CMBS trusts allow supplemental (additional) financing if the property's value has increased, the availability and terms of supplemental loans are subject to strict rules defined in the trust documents. Borrowers cannot simply go back to the lender and request additional proceeds as they might with a bank relationship.

CMBS vs. Bank Loans

Choosing between CMBS and a traditional bank loan depends on the borrower's priorities, the property profile, and how much flexibility is needed during the loan term. The table below highlights the key differences.

In general, CMBS is the stronger choice for borrowers who prioritize non-recourse protection, fixed-rate certainty, and maximum leverage on a stabilized, cash-flowing property. Bank loans are the better fit when the borrower needs flexibility to modify the loan, prepay without penalty, or is executing a business plan that involves significant lease-up, renovation, or repositioning during the loan term.

The CMBS Loan Process Step by Step

The CMBS origination process is more structured and timeline-intensive than a typical bank loan. Here is what borrowers can expect from initial inquiry through closing.

  1. Initial screening and term sheet. The borrower submits a loan request package that includes a property summary, rent roll, trailing 12-month operating statements, and a description of the borrower and guarantor. The CMBS lender evaluates the deal and, if interested, issues a term sheet outlining the proposed loan amount, rate, term, amortization, reserves, and key structural provisions. Term sheets are non-binding but serve as the framework for the transaction.
  2. Application and deposit. Once the borrower accepts the term sheet, they execute a formal loan application and submit a good-faith deposit (typically $15,000 to $50,000) to cover third-party report costs. This deposit is generally non-refundable and covers the appraisal, environmental assessment, property condition report, and seismic study (if applicable).
  3. Third-party reports. The lender orders an MAI appraisal, a Phase I environmental site assessment, a property condition assessment (PCA), and, in seismic zones, a probable maximum loss (PML) study. These reports are conducted by independent firms and typically take three to four weeks to complete. The results directly influence the final loan sizing and terms.
  4. Underwriting. The lender's underwriting team performs a detailed analysis of the property's income and expenses, lease terms, market comparables, tenant creditworthiness, and capital needs. They stress-test the cash flow under various scenarios and determine the final loan amount based on DSCR and LTV constraints. The underwriter also reviews the borrower's and guarantor's financial statements, experience, and legal background.
  5. Loan approval and commitment. After underwriting is complete, the loan goes through the lender's internal credit committee for approval. If approved, the lender issues a commitment letter with the final loan terms and conditions. The commitment letter is binding on the lender (subject to satisfying specified conditions) and typically requires the borrower to sign and return it within a defined period.
  6. Legal documentation and closing. The lender's counsel prepares the loan documents, which are largely standardized for CMBS. The borrower's attorney reviews and negotiates limited provisions. Closing typically occurs 45 to 75 days after the initial term sheet, depending on the complexity of the deal and the speed of third-party report delivery. At closing, the borrower funds required reserves, the lender disburses the loan proceeds, and the mortgage is recorded.
  7. Securitization. After closing, the loan is placed on the originator's warehouse line until it is pooled with other recently originated CMBS loans. The pool is then rated by one or more rating agencies, bonds are issued, and the securities are sold to institutional investors. At this point, the loan is governed by the trust's pooling and servicing agreement (PSA), and ongoing management shifts to the master servicer.

Defeasance and Yield Maintenance Explained

Prepayment provisions are one of the most misunderstood aspects of CMBS lending. Unlike a residential mortgage, where you can typically pay off the loan early by writing a check, a CMBS borrower must navigate rigid prepayment mechanisms that exist to protect bondholders from reinvestment risk.

Defeasance

Defeasance is the process of substituting the real estate collateral with a portfolio of U.S. government securities (typically Treasury STRIPS) that generate cash flows identical to the remaining scheduled loan payments. Once the collateral is replaced, the borrower is released from the mortgage, and the property can be sold or refinanced free and clear. The trust continues to receive its expected payments from the Treasury portfolio, so bondholders are unaffected.

The mechanics of defeasance are complex. The borrower hires a defeasance consultant who works with a securities broker to assemble the replacement portfolio. The borrower must also retain legal counsel, an accountant to verify the cash flow match, and a successor borrower (a special-purpose entity that assumes the defeased loan and holds the Treasury portfolio). The total cost of defeasance includes the premium on the Treasury securities (which can be substantial if interest rates have fallen since origination), consultant fees ($25,000 to $75,000), legal fees, and rating agency fees. In a rising rate environment, defeasance can actually be done at a discount because the replacement Treasuries cost less than par. In a falling rate environment, the cost can exceed several percentage points of the loan balance.

Yield Maintenance

Yield maintenance is a simpler but often equally expensive prepayment mechanism. The borrower pays a premium calculated to make the lender "whole" for the interest income lost due to early repayment. The formula typically equals the present value of the difference between the loan's interest rate and the prevailing Treasury yield for the remaining loan term, applied to the outstanding principal balance. Like defeasance, yield maintenance is most expensive when rates have dropped and least expensive (or even negligible) when rates have risen above the loan's coupon.

Most CMBS loans follow a structure of two years of lockout followed by defeasance or yield maintenance for the remainder of the term, with an open prepayment window during the final three to six months before maturity. Borrowers should model prepayment costs carefully before committing to a CMBS loan, particularly if there is any possibility of selling or refinancing the property before the loan matures.

The 2026 CMBS Market

The CMBS market in 2026 is shaped by several converging forces that are creating both challenges and opportunities for borrowers.

The Maturity Wall

An estimated $90 billion to $120 billion in CMBS loans originated during the 2015-2017 issuance wave are reaching maturity in 2025 and 2026. This "maturity wall" is the largest concentration of CMBS loan maturities since the post-financial-crisis wave of 2015-2016. Many of these loans were originated at lower interest rates and higher property valuations than what the current market supports. Borrowers whose properties have experienced declining values or rising vacancies (particularly in the office sector) face refinancing gaps, where the new loan amount available at today's LTV and DSCR constraints is less than the maturing loan balance. This gap must be filled with new equity, mezzanine debt, or a negotiated modification with the special servicer.

Spread Trends

After widening significantly in 2022 and 2023 as the Federal Reserve raised interest rates and commercial real estate values came under pressure, CMBS bond spreads have gradually tightened through 2024 and into 2026. Investor appetite for CMBS bonds has recovered as the rate environment has stabilized and property fundamentals in most sectors (industrial, multifamily, self-storage) have held up well. Office-backed CMBS bonds remain the exception, with wider spreads reflecting ongoing uncertainty about remote work trends and tenant demand. For borrowers with strong assets in favored property types, the current spread environment is producing competitive fixed rates.

Issuance Volume

CMBS issuance volume recovered meaningfully in 2025 after a sharp pullback in 2023 and early 2024. The market is on pace for approximately $85 billion to $100 billion in total 2026 issuance (conduit, single-asset single-borrower, and CRE CLO combined), which would represent a return to near-normal activity levels. Conduit lenders are actively competing for loans, which is producing favorable terms for well-qualified borrowers with stabilized assets. The rebound in issuance is a positive signal for borrowers, as greater lender competition typically leads to tighter spreads, higher leverage, and more flexible structuring.

For borrowers navigating the current market, the key takeaway is that CMBS remains a deep, liquid, and competitive capital source for stabilized commercial real estate. The maturity wall is creating stress in certain segments (particularly office), but it is also motivating servicers and lenders to find workable solutions for performing loans that face temporary headwinds. Borrowers with strong properties and proactive refinancing strategies are well-positioned to secure favorable terms in the current environment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Most CMBS conduit lenders set a practical minimum loan size of $2 million, though some programs will consider loans as low as $1 million for strong properties in established markets. The minimum exists because the fixed costs of originating, underwriting, and securitizing a CMBS loan (including third-party reports, legal fees, and rating agency expenses) are roughly the same regardless of loan size. On a very small loan, those costs become disproportionate and erode the economics for both the lender and the borrower.

For loans under $2 million, borrowers will generally find better execution through a local bank, credit union, or SBA program where the overhead is lower and the terms can be more flexible. Some CMBS lenders have launched "small balance" programs targeting loans between $1 million and $5 million with streamlined underwriting and slightly lower closing costs, but these programs are less common and may carry modestly wider spreads than standard conduit execution.

There is no formal upper limit on CMBS loan size. Single loans of $100 million, $200 million, or more are regularly originated as single-asset single-borrower (SASB) transactions, where the entire securitization is backed by one large loan on a single trophy property or portfolio.

CMBS loans are structured as non-recourse, meaning the lender's primary remedy in a default is to seize the collateral property rather than pursue the borrower's personal assets. This is one of the most attractive features of CMBS financing for investors and sponsors who want to limit their personal exposure on any single deal.

However, every CMBS loan includes "bad boy" carve-out guarantees that can convert the loan to full recourse under specific circumstances. These triggers include fraud, intentional misrepresentation in the loan application, voluntary bankruptcy filing by the borrower, misappropriation of rents or insurance proceeds, unauthorized transfer of the property, and failure to maintain required insurance. The carve-outs are designed to prevent intentional misconduct, not to penalize borrowers for ordinary business downturns or property underperformance.

A creditworthy individual or entity must sign as the carve-out guarantor at closing. Lenders evaluate the guarantor's net worth (typically requiring at least 100% of the loan amount) and liquidity (typically 10% of the loan balance) to ensure the guarantee has real teeth. Borrowers should review the carve-out provisions carefully with legal counsel before closing, as the specific triggers and their scope can vary between lenders and transactions.

The first and most common approach is a loan assumption, where the buyer takes over the existing CMBS loan. CMBS loans are generally assumable, and this can be a significant selling point in a rising rate environment because the buyer inherits the original fixed rate. The buyer must go through a full underwriting review by the loan servicer, meeting the same net worth, liquidity, experience, and creditworthiness standards that the original borrower satisfied. There is typically an assumption fee of approximately 1% of the outstanding loan balance, plus legal and processing costs. The assumption process usually takes 30 to 60 days.

The second approach is to defease or prepay the loan using the applicable prepayment mechanism (defeasance or yield maintenance) described in the loan documents. This frees the property from the mortgage lien, allowing a clean sale. However, the cost can be significant, particularly if interest rates have fallen since the loan was originated. Borrowers should model the prepayment cost early in the sale process to ensure the transaction still pencils after accounting for the penalty.

During the initial lockout period (typically the first two years of the loan term), neither assumption nor defeasance is available, meaning the property effectively cannot be sold or transferred during that window.

CMBS loan rates are set as a spread over the corresponding U.S. Treasury yield. For a 10-year loan, the benchmark is the 10-year Treasury note; for a 5-year loan, it is the 5-year Treasury. The spread reflects the risk premium that CMBS bond investors demand above the risk-free rate, and it varies based on several deal-specific and market-wide factors.

Deal-specific factors that influence the spread include the property type (industrial and multifamily get tighter spreads; hospitality and office tend wider), the loan-to-value ratio (lower leverage equals tighter spread), the debt service coverage ratio (higher coverage signals lower risk), the property's location and physical condition, tenant quality and lease term, and the sponsor's track record. Market-wide factors include overall investor demand for CMBS bonds, recent default and loss experience in the CMBS universe, the pace of new issuance, and broader economic conditions.

In a healthy market, conduit spreads for stabilized, institutional-quality properties typically range from 150 to 250 basis points (1.50% to 2.50%) over the benchmark Treasury. So if the 10-year Treasury yields 4.25%, a borrower might expect an all-in fixed rate of roughly 5.75% to 6.75%. The rate is typically locked at or shortly before closing, though some lenders offer rate locks at application for an additional fee. Once locked, the rate is fixed for the full loan term.

A transfer to special servicing means the loan has encountered a problem that the master servicer cannot resolve through normal collection procedures. Common triggers include missed payments, a monetary or technical default, an imminent maturity default (where the borrower is unlikely to pay off or refinance the loan by its maturity date), or a significant decline in the property's occupancy or income that raises concerns about future performance.

Once transferred, the special servicer evaluates the situation and determines the resolution strategy that maximizes recovery for the bondholders. Options include a loan modification (adjusting the rate, extending the term, or splitting the loan), a forbearance agreement (giving the borrower additional time to cure the default), a discounted payoff (allowing the borrower or a third party to pay less than the full balance to resolve the loan), foreclosure and REO (real estate owned) disposition, or a note sale (selling the distressed loan to an investor at a discount).

Borrowers should be aware that special servicing introduces additional costs. The special servicer typically charges an annual fee of 0.25% of the outstanding loan balance, plus a workout fee (usually 1% of the loan balance) if a modification is reached, or a liquidation fee (also around 1%) if the property is foreclosed. These fees reduce the net proceeds available to the borrower and the trust. The special servicer's obligation is to the bondholders, not the borrower, which means the outcome may not align with what the borrower wants. Engaging early, providing transparent financial information, and presenting a realistic resolution plan are the best strategies for achieving a favorable workout.
