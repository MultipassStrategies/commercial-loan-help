Overview of the CRE Lending Landscape

Commercial real estate financing is not a single product. It is an entire ecosystem of lending options, each designed for different property types, borrower profiles, deal timelines, and risk levels. Whether you are purchasing a stabilized office building, constructing a ground-up multifamily development, or refinancing a retail center before your loan matures, the right loan type can save you hundreds of thousands of dollars over the life of the deal.

The CRE lending market includes traditional sources like banks and credit unions, government-backed programs through the SBA and USDA, capital markets products such as CMBS, institutional lenders like life insurance companies, and private capital options including hard money, mezzanine debt, and preferred equity. Each comes with distinct advantages, limitations, and costs. Understanding these differences is the first step toward structuring the best possible financing for your investment.

In 2026, borrowers face a dynamic lending environment shaped by interest rate shifts, tightening bank regulations, and evolving capital markets conditions. Some loan types that were widely available two years ago have become harder to access, while others have grown more competitive. This guide breaks down every major loan type so you can compare them clearly and make an informed decision.

Conventional Bank Loans

Conventional bank loans remain the backbone of commercial real estate lending. These are originated and held on the bank's balance sheet (known as "portfolio" loans), which gives the lender flexibility to negotiate terms based on the borrower's relationship and overall financial profile.

Rates on conventional CRE loans typically range from 6.0% to 8.0%, depending on the property type, leverage, and borrower strength. Banks generally lend up to 75% to 80% LTV on stabilized properties, though some will go as low as 65% on riskier asset classes. Terms run from 5 to 25 years, often with a fixed rate period of 5 to 10 years followed by an adjustment.

The main advantages of conventional bank loans include relationship-based underwriting (your overall banking relationship matters), flexibility in structuring, and the ability to negotiate prepayment terms. The primary drawbacks are longer closing times (45 to 90 days is common), stringent documentation requirements, and increasing regulatory pressure that has made some banks more conservative in their CRE lending since 2024.

CMBS and Conduit Loans

Commercial Mortgage-Backed Securities (CMBS) loans, also called conduit loans, are originated by lenders and then pooled together and sold to investors in the bond market. This securitization process allows for competitive rates but introduces rigid loan structures and limited flexibility after closing.

CMBS loans are ideal for stabilized, income-producing properties where the borrower does not anticipate needing to modify the loan during its term. Rates typically range from 6.0% to 7.5%, with LTVs of 65% to 75% and terms of 5 to 10 years. Minimum loan amounts usually start at $2 million, though some conduit programs go lower.

The biggest advantage of CMBS is that underwriting focuses heavily on the property's cash flow rather than the borrower's personal balance sheet. This makes CMBS accessible to borrowers who might not qualify for a bank loan. However, the trade-offs are significant: CMBS loans come with yield maintenance or defeasance prepayment penalties, the servicing is handled by a third-party special servicer, and any modifications to the loan require a complex and often costly process.

SBA 7(a) Loans

The SBA 7(a) program is the Small Business Administration's most popular lending vehicle. It provides government-guaranteed loans (up to 85% guarantee on loans under $150,000, and 75% on larger amounts) to small businesses for a variety of purposes, including purchasing commercial real estate.

SBA 7(a) loans allow up to 85% LTV, with terms up to 25 years for real estate purchases. Rates are typically variable, based on the prime rate plus a margin of 1.5% to 2.75%, putting current rates in the 7.5% to 10.0% range. The maximum loan amount is $5 million. Borrowers must be owner-occupants, meaning at least 51% of the property must be used by the borrower's business.

Qualification is more accessible than conventional lending because the SBA guarantee reduces lender risk. Borrowers need a credit score of at least 650 (ideally 680+), sufficient cash flow to cover debt service, and a reasonable amount of industry experience. The downside is that the process can be paperwork-intensive and can take 45 to 90 days to close. SBA guarantee fees also add to the overall cost.

SBA 504 Loans

The SBA 504 program is specifically designed for owner-occupied commercial real estate and major fixed-asset purchases. It uses a unique two-loan structure: a first mortgage from a conventional lender (typically 50% of the project cost), a second mortgage from a Certified Development Company, or CDC (up to 40%), and a 10% borrower down payment.

The CDC portion carries a below-market fixed rate tied to U.S. Treasury yields, making the blended rate on an SBA 504 deal extremely competitive, often in the 5.5% to 7.5% range. LTV can reach up to 90%, which is the highest leverage available in the conventional CRE lending market. Terms are 10, 20, or 25 years, and the fixed-rate CDC portion provides long-term payment stability.

The 504 program is best suited for owner-occupants who want low down payments and fixed rates. The main limitations include the owner-occupancy requirement (51% for existing buildings, 60% for new construction), the longer closing timeline (60 to 90 days), and geographic and job-creation requirements tied to the CDC component.

Bridge Loans and Hard Money Loans

Bridge loans and hard money loans occupy the short-term, higher-cost end of the CRE lending spectrum. While they share similarities, there are important distinctions between them.

Bridge loans are short-term financing (typically 6 to 36 months) used to "bridge" the gap between a current need and a longer-term solution. Common uses include acquiring a property before permanent financing is arranged, funding a value-add renovation, or covering a gap during lease-up. Bridge loan rates range from 8.0% to 12.0%, with LTVs of 65% to 80%. They can close in as little as two to four weeks.

Hard money loans are asset-based loans funded primarily by private investors or small lending firms. They are underwritten almost entirely on the value of the collateral, with minimal regard for the borrower's creditworthiness. Rates are the highest in the market (10.0% to 14%+), LTVs are typically conservative (50% to 70%), and terms are short (6 to 24 months). The primary advantage is speed: hard money loans can close in as little as one to two weeks. They are commonly used for distressed properties, auction purchases, and situations where time is more important than cost.

Both bridge and hard money loans are intended as temporary financing. The exit strategy (how you will repay the loan) is one of the most important factors lenders evaluate during underwriting. A clear plan to refinance into permanent financing or sell the property is essential.

Mezzanine Debt and Preferred Equity

Mezzanine debt and preferred equity are subordinate capital positions that sit between senior debt and common equity in the capital stack. Both serve the same basic purpose: they allow borrowers to increase leverage beyond what a senior lender will provide alone.

Mezzanine debt is a loan secured by a pledge of the borrower's ownership interest in the property-owning entity (rather than a mortgage on the property itself). Rates typically range from 10.0% to 14%+, and when combined with senior debt, total leverage can reach 75% to 90%. Mezzanine lenders require an intercreditor agreement with the senior lender. In a default scenario, the mezzanine lender's primary remedy is to take over the borrower's ownership interest.

Preferred equity is structured as an equity investment rather than debt, which means it does not appear on the balance sheet as a loan. Returns for preferred equity investors range from 12% to 18%+, reflecting the higher risk of being in a subordinate position. Preferred equity is often used in deals where the senior lender prohibits additional debt, as it technically falls outside the definition of a loan.

Both products are common in large-scale transactions ($5 million and above) and are frequently used in development projects, recapitalizations, and acquisitions where the sponsor wants to minimize the amount of common equity required.

Construction Loans

Construction loans are short-term facilities designed to fund the building of new properties or major renovations of existing ones. They differ from permanent loans in several key ways: funds are disbursed in stages (called "draws") as construction milestones are completed, interest is charged only on the amount drawn, and the loan typically converts to a permanent mortgage or is repaid upon project completion.

Rates on construction loans generally range from 7.5% to 10.0%, and lenders underwrite based on loan-to-cost (LTC) ratios of 60% to 75% rather than traditional LTV. Terms run from 12 to 36 months, depending on the scope of the project. Minimum loan amounts are usually $1 million or more, as the complexity of construction lending makes smaller loans uneconomical for many lenders.

Construction lenders require detailed project budgets, construction timelines, contractor qualifications, and often personal guarantees from the borrower. Many lenders also require evidence of permanent financing (a "take-out" commitment) before funding the construction loan. This is one of the more complex loan types to arrange, but it is essential for any ground-up development.

USDA Business and Industry Loans

The U.S. Department of Agriculture's Business and Industry (B&I) Guaranteed Loan Program provides government-guaranteed financing for businesses in rural areas. Properties located in towns with populations of 50,000 or fewer may qualify, making this program relevant for a surprisingly large portion of the country.

USDA B&I loans offer rates in the 6.0% to 8.0% range, with LTVs up to 80% and terms as long as 30 years. The maximum loan amount is $25 million. Like SBA loans, the government guarantee (up to 80% of the loan) reduces lender risk and enables more favorable terms than a conventional loan might offer.

The program is well suited for manufacturing facilities, agricultural businesses, hospitality properties, and other commercial ventures in rural and small-town markets. The approval process can take 60 to 90 days and involves both the lender and the USDA, but the competitive rates and long terms make it worth the wait for qualifying properties.

Life Insurance Company Loans

Life insurance companies are among the most conservative and lowest-cost lenders in the CRE market. They deploy funds from their large investment portfolios and target the highest-quality assets with the strongest cash flows. In exchange for this selectivity, they offer the best rates available: typically 5.5% to 6.75%.

Life company loans usually require low leverage (50% to 65% LTV), strong occupancy (typically 85%+ for multifamily, 90%+ for other property types), and institutional-quality properties. Minimum loan sizes are generally $5 million to $10 million, though some life companies will go lower for exceptional assets. Terms range from 7 to 30 years, and many offer full-term fixed rates.

The borrower profile for life company loans skews toward experienced investors and institutions with proven track records. The underwriting process is thorough and can take 45 to 90 days, but the result is often the lowest cost of capital in the entire CRE market. For borrowers who qualify, life insurance company loans should be the first option considered.

How to Choose the Right Loan Type: A Decision Framework

With so many options available, selecting the right CRE loan requires a structured approach. Consider these four key decision factors in order.

1. Property Status: Stabilized vs. Transitional

If your property is stabilized (fully leased, generating consistent income), you have access to the full range of permanent financing options: conventional bank loans, CMBS, life insurance company loans, and SBA programs. If the property is transitional (undergoing renovation, in lease-up, or distressed), your options narrow to bridge loans, hard money, construction loans, and mezzanine financing.

2. Timeline: How Fast Do You Need to Close?

Deals that must close within 30 days are best served by bridge or hard money lenders. If you have 45 to 90 days, the full range of conventional, CMBS, SBA, and life company options becomes available. Never pay bridge loan rates when you have the time to secure permanent financing.

3. Leverage: How Much Do You Need to Borrow?

If you need LTV above 75%, your main options are SBA 504 (up to 90%), SBA 7(a) (up to 85%), or a capital stack that combines senior debt with mezzanine or preferred equity. Life insurance companies rarely exceed 65% LTV, and CMBS typically caps at 75%.

4. Cost Sensitivity: Rate vs. Flexibility

If achieving the lowest possible rate is your top priority, pursue life insurance company or SBA 504 financing. If flexibility matters more (such as the ability to prepay without penalty or modify loan terms), a conventional bank portfolio loan may be the better choice despite a slightly higher rate.

When to Use Multiple Loan Types

Many experienced CRE investors use layered financing strategies to optimize their capital structure. Here are the most common scenarios for combining loan types.

SBA 504 plus conventional bank loan: The 504 structure itself is a combination, with the CDC second mortgage sitting behind a conventional first mortgage. This built-in combination allows for 90% LTV at a blended below-market rate.

Senior debt plus mezzanine: A CMBS or bank loan at 65% LTV combined with mezzanine debt can bring total leverage to 80% to 85%. This is common in large acquisitions where the sponsor wants to minimize equity while keeping the senior loan at a conservative level.

Bridge-to-permanent: Starting with a bridge loan to acquire and stabilize a property, then refinancing into a permanent loan (conventional, CMBS, or life company) once the asset is performing. This two-step approach is standard for value-add investment strategies.

Construction-to-permanent: Some lenders offer a single loan that converts from a construction facility to a permanent mortgage upon project completion. This eliminates the need to arrange separate financing and reduces closing costs.

The right combination depends on your specific deal economics, risk tolerance, and long-term hold strategy. In all cases, run detailed financial models comparing the total cost of capital across different structures before committing to a particular approach.
