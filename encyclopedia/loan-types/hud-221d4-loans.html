HUD 221(d)(4) Loans: Construction and Substantial Rehab Financing for Multifamily

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Max LTV: 85% to 90%
  • Min DSCR: 1.18x (1.11x for affordable)
  • Construction period: Up to 3 years
  • Permanent term: 40 years
  • Rate type: Fixed, FHA-insured
  • Amortization: Fully amortizing

What Is the HUD 221(d)(4) Program?

The HUD 221(d)(4) program is the federal government's primary tool for financing the construction and substantial rehabilitation of multifamily rental housing. Administered by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development through the Federal Housing Administration, the program provides FHA-insured mortgage financing for projects with five or more residential units. It is one of the most powerful and most underutilized financing vehicles in commercial real estate, offering developers a single loan that covers both the construction phase and the permanent mortgage in one closing.

Section 221(d)(4) of the National Housing Act authorizes HUD to insure mortgages on new construction and substantial rehabilitation projects for market-rate, affordable, and mixed-income multifamily housing. The FHA insurance removes default risk from the lender's balance sheet, which allows borrowers to access terms that are virtually impossible to replicate in the conventional market: fully amortizing 40-year fixed-rate loans, non-recourse financing, and leverage up to 90% of replacement cost for qualifying projects. These features make 221(d)(4) the gold standard for developers building large-scale apartment communities intended for long-term hold.

The program is processed through HUD's Multifamily Accelerated Processing (MAP) framework, which means that only MAP-approved lenders can originate 221(d)(4) loans. These lenders perform the initial underwriting and submit the application to HUD's regional office for review and approval. Because HUD insures the mortgage, the agency conducts its own independent review of the project's financial feasibility, architectural plans, market demand, and environmental compliance before issuing a firm commitment.

Why Developers Choose 221(d)(4)

The 221(d)(4) program addresses several of the biggest challenges in multifamily development financing. For developers willing to navigate the regulatory process, the benefits are substantial and difficult to match through any other lending source.

Non-recourse construction financing. Unlike virtually all conventional construction loans, a 221(d)(4) mortgage is non-recourse to the borrower. This means the developer's personal assets are not at risk if the project encounters financial difficulty. The FHA insurance covers the lender's exposure, and the developer's liability is limited to standard "bad boy" carve-outs (fraud, misrepresentation, environmental contamination, and similar willful acts). For developers with multiple active projects, non-recourse treatment is critical because it prevents a single troubled deal from jeopardizing their entire portfolio.

One closing for construction and permanent. Most conventional development deals require two separate financings: a short-term construction loan and a long-term permanent mortgage. This two-step process creates refinance risk, because the developer must qualify for the permanent loan at whatever market conditions prevail when construction is complete. With 221(d)(4), the construction loan and permanent mortgage are combined into a single closing. The fixed rate is locked before the first shovel hits the ground, and the loan automatically converts to a fully amortizing permanent mortgage once construction is complete and the project reaches initial occupancy. There is no second underwriting, no second appraisal, and no rate uncertainty at conversion.

40-year fully amortizing term. The permanent loan term under 221(d)(4) is 40 years, fully amortizing with no balloon payment. This produces exceptionally low debt service relative to the loan amount, which maximizes cash flow for the borrower and provides a level of payment stability that is unmatched in the conventional market. A 40-year amortization on a fixed-rate loan means the developer knows exactly what the mortgage payment will be for every single month of the loan's life.

High leverage. Depending on the project type and affordability designation, 221(d)(4) loans can reach up to 90% of replacement cost. This reduces the equity requirement to as little as 10% of total development cost, preserving the developer's capital for additional projects or contingency reserves.

Eligible Projects

The 221(d)(4) program is available for a defined set of multifamily project types. Understanding what qualifies is essential before investing the time and cost required for the HUD application process.

  • New construction: Ground-up development of multifamily residential properties with five or more units. This is the most common use of the program and encompasses garden-style apartments, mid-rise buildings, high-rise towers, townhome-style rental communities, and mixed-use projects where the residential component is the primary use.
  • Substantial rehabilitation: Major renovation of existing multifamily properties where the scope of work is extensive enough to be treated similarly to new construction. This includes gut renovations, structural modifications, full building system replacements (HVAC, plumbing, electrical, roofing), seismic upgrades, and significant reconfiguration of unit layouts. Minor cosmetic repairs do not qualify; the rehab must be substantial in both scope and cost.
  • Market-rate housing: Projects where rents are set at market levels with no income restrictions on tenants.
  • Affordable and subsidized housing: Projects with Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC) allocations, Section 8 rental assistance contracts, or other affordability restrictions. These projects receive more favorable underwriting terms, including higher LTV ratios and lower DSCR thresholds.
  • Mixed-income projects: Developments that combine market-rate and affordable units within the same property. HUD evaluates these projects based on the proportion of restricted units and may apply blended underwriting standards.

Projects that do not qualify include single-family homes, duplexes, triplexes, and fourplexes (the minimum is five units), as well as properties intended for transient use such as hotels and motels. Continuing care retirement communities and assisted living facilities may qualify under related HUD programs (Section 232) but are not eligible under 221(d)(4). Cooperative housing is covered under a separate section, 213.

LTV and DSCR Requirements by Project Type

HUD sets different leverage and debt service coverage thresholds depending on whether the project is market-rate or carries an affordability designation. These distinctions reflect the lower risk profile that HUD assigns to projects with government rental subsidies or deed restrictions that ensure stable occupancy.

Market-Rate Projects

  • Maximum LTV: 85% of replacement cost
  • Minimum DSCR: 1.18x (based on HUD's underwritten net operating income divided by annual debt service)
  • Equity requirement: 15% of total replacement cost, which can include land value, developer fee, and other eligible soft costs

Affordable and Subsidized Projects

  • Maximum LTV: 87% for projects with LIHTC allocations; up to 90% for projects receiving project-based Section 8 rental assistance or operating in a HUD-designated area
  • Minimum DSCR: 1.11x for projects with 90% or more low-income units and rental assistance
  • Equity requirement: As low as 10% of total replacement cost for the highest-leverage affordable projects

It is important to understand that HUD calculates LTV based on "replacement cost," which includes not just hard construction costs but also land value, soft costs (architecture, engineering, legal, permitting), financing costs, and developer fees. This broader cost basis typically results in a higher insurable mortgage amount than conventional lenders would approve, because conventional underwriting usually limits leverage to a percentage of appraised "as-completed" value. The replacement cost methodology is one of the reasons 221(d)(4) can achieve such high effective leverage.

Rate Structure and Mortgage Insurance Premium

The interest rate on a 221(d)(4) loan is fixed at closing and remains unchanged for the entire 40-year term. The rate itself is composed of several components that together determine the borrower's all-in cost of capital.

Base Note Rate

The base rate is set by the MAP-approved lender and reflects current pricing in the Ginnie Mae mortgage-backed securities (MBS) market. Because 221(d)(4) mortgages carry FHA insurance and are securitized through Ginnie Mae, they trade at spreads close to U.S. Treasury yields. This government-backed execution consistently produces base rates that are lower than what any conventional construction lender can offer on a fixed-rate basis.

FHA Mortgage Insurance Premium (MIP)

The borrower pays an annual MIP to HUD in exchange for the FHA insurance that makes the favorable loan terms possible. MIP rates vary by project type and affordability designation:

Project Type Annual MIP Rate
Market-rate (standard) 0.65%
Broadly affordable (LIHTC, below-market rents) 0.45%
Deeply affordable (Section 8 or similar subsidies) 0.25%
Green / energy-efficient certified projects Reduced by up to 0.20% from applicable tier

The MIP is calculated annually on the declining mortgage balance and paid monthly as part of the debt service. Over the life of a 40-year loan, the MIP tier can significantly affect total borrowing cost, which is why affordable housing developers with access to LIHTC or Section 8 often find 221(d)(4) particularly attractive.

Lender Servicing Fee

The MAP-approved lender charges an ongoing servicing fee, typically ranging from 0.05% to 0.15% annually, for administering the loan, processing payments, and managing the Ginnie Mae securitization. This fee is also built into the all-in rate.

When all components are combined, the all-in rate for a 221(d)(4) loan typically falls between 5.5% and 7.0% as of early 2026, depending on market conditions and the applicable MIP tier. While the headline rate may appear similar to some conventional permanent loans, the critical distinction is that this rate is fixed for 40 years and includes the construction period, eliminating the floating-rate exposure and refinance risk that come with conventional construction financing.

Davis-Bacon Wage Requirements

Every project financed through the HUD 221(d)(4) program must comply with the Davis-Bacon Act, a federal law enacted in 1931 that requires workers on federally assisted construction projects to be paid at least the locally prevailing wages and fringe benefits for their respective trades. This requirement applies to all contractors and subcontractors working on the project, from foundation to finish.

In practical terms, Davis-Bacon compliance involves several obligations. Before construction begins, the developer must obtain a wage determination from the U.S. Department of Labor that specifies the prevailing wage rates for each trade classification (carpentry, electrical, plumbing, ironwork, and so forth) in the project's geographic area. All construction contracts must incorporate these wage rates as minimums. Throughout the construction period, every contractor and subcontractor must submit certified weekly payroll reports documenting that all workers are being paid at or above the prevailing wage. HUD and the Department of Labor retain the right to audit these records and conduct on-site interviews with workers to verify compliance.

The cost impact of Davis-Bacon is significant. Depending on the local labor market, prevailing wage requirements typically add 10% to 20% to total construction costs compared to a project built without wage mandates. In markets where union labor already dominates (such as many Northeast and West Coast cities), the incremental cost may be minimal because prevailing wages closely track existing union scales. In markets with lower prevailing construction wages (many Southeast and Midwest locations), the premium can be more substantial.

Developers must incorporate Davis-Bacon wage premiums into their project budgets from the earliest feasibility stage. Underestimating these costs is one of the most common budgeting errors in 221(d)(4) projects and can lead to cost overruns that jeopardize project viability. Working with a general contractor experienced in Davis-Bacon projects is strongly recommended, as the payroll reporting and documentation requirements are rigorous and errors can trigger penalties, project delays, or both.

The Application Process: Three Stages

The HUD 221(d)(4) application process is structured into three distinct stages, each with its own requirements, timelines, and deliverables. Understanding this progression helps developers plan realistic project schedules and allocate resources appropriately.

Stage 1: Pre-Application

The pre-application is the developer's initial submission to HUD, filed through a MAP-approved lender. This package includes a market study demonstrating demand for the proposed units, preliminary architectural plans and site plans, an estimated development budget, a description of the development team's qualifications and track record, evidence of site control (purchase agreement or land ownership), and zoning confirmation. HUD reviews the pre-application to determine whether the project concept is feasible and whether the local market can support the proposed unit count and rent levels. If HUD finds the pre-application acceptable, it issues an invitation to submit a firm application. This stage typically takes 2 to 4 months.

Stage 2: Firm Application

The firm application is the full underwriting package and represents the most document-intensive phase of the process. Required exhibits include final architectural drawings and specifications, a detailed construction cost breakdown certified by the general contractor, an updated market study (if the pre-application study has aged), an environmental review (Phase I and Phase II if warranted), an appraisal meeting HUD's specific requirements, financial statements and background information for all principals, a detailed operating pro forma, evidence of all required equity and financing commitments, the proposed management plan and agent, and documentation of any affordability restrictions (LIHTC allocations, Section 8 contracts, or regulatory agreements). HUD's staff and third-party reviewers (including a cost analyst and an appraiser) evaluate every component of the firm application. The MAP-approved lender works closely with the developer to respond to HUD's questions and clear any conditions. This stage typically takes 6 to 10 months.

Stage 3: Initial Endorsement and Closing

Once HUD approves the firm application, it issues a Firm Commitment letter specifying all terms and conditions of the insured mortgage. The developer and lender then work through the closing process, which includes finalizing construction contracts, satisfying any remaining HUD conditions, establishing required escrow accounts, and executing the mortgage documents. At closing (known as "initial endorsement"), the construction loan funds become available and the developer can begin drawing down for construction. This stage typically takes 2 to 4 months after the Firm Commitment is issued.

Timeline: What to Expect

Realistic timeline expectations are essential for any developer considering a 221(d)(4) project. The HUD process is thorough by design, and developers who underestimate timelines often face costly delays, expired rate locks, or stale market studies that require updating.

Pre-Construction Phase (12 to 18 Months)

  • Pre-application preparation and submission: 1 to 2 months
  • HUD pre-application review: 1 to 2 months
  • Firm application preparation: 2 to 4 months (concurrent with third-party reports)
  • HUD firm application review and processing: 4 to 8 months
  • Firm commitment and closing: 2 to 4 months

Construction Phase (Up to 3 Years)

The construction period begins at initial endorsement and can extend up to 36 months, depending on project size, complexity, and local permitting timelines. HUD requires regular construction inspections (typically monthly) by a HUD-approved inspector, and loan draws are released only after the inspector certifies that work has been completed satisfactorily. This oversight adds a layer of process to the draw schedule but also provides quality assurance that protects both the developer and the lender.

Final Endorsement

After construction is complete and the project achieves a specified minimum occupancy level (typically 90% or higher for at least 3 consecutive months), HUD conducts a final review and issues the final endorsement. At this point, the mortgage insurance is fully in effect for the permanent loan period, the construction accounts are closed out, and the developer submits a cost certification audit to HUD. The cost certification process reconciles actual construction costs against the approved budget and can result in a mortgage adjustment if actual costs differ materially from projections.

From initial concept to stabilized operations, a typical 221(d)(4) project spans 3 to 5 years. Developers should plan their capital structure, partnership timelines, and investor expectations around this full lifecycle.

Costs, Reserves, and Working Capital Requirements

Beyond the direct costs of land, construction, and soft costs, HUD requires several escrow accounts and reserves that developers must fund as part of the 221(d)(4) closing. These requirements protect the project's long-term viability but add to the total capital needed at closing.

Initial Operating Deficit (IOD) Escrow

HUD requires the developer to deposit funds into an initial operating deficit escrow to cover projected operating shortfalls during the lease-up period. The IOD amount is calculated based on HUD's underwritten pro forma and typically covers the gap between projected expenses (including debt service) and projected revenue during the months between construction completion and stabilized occupancy. The IOD escrow can represent a significant cash outlay, often equivalent to several months of operating expenses and debt service payments. Unused IOD funds are returned to the developer after the project achieves stabilized occupancy and maintains it for a specified period.

Replacement Reserve

HUD mandates an annual deposit into a replacement reserve account to fund future capital expenditures such as roof replacement, appliance upgrades, parking lot resurfacing, and building system repairs. The required annual deposit is typically $250 per unit per year, though HUD may set higher amounts for projects with older building components or higher anticipated maintenance needs. The replacement reserve is held by the lender and can only be drawn with HUD approval for eligible capital expenditure items.

Working Capital Escrow

At initial endorsement, the developer must deposit a working capital escrow equal to at least 2% of the mortgage amount. This escrow serves as a contingency fund for unexpected costs during construction and early operations. Like the IOD escrow, unused working capital funds are returned after the project stabilizes, subject to HUD's release conditions.

Other Closing Costs and Fees

  • HUD examination fee: 0.3% of the mortgage amount, paid at firm application
  • HUD inspection fee: 0.5% of the mortgage amount, covering HUD's construction inspection program
  • Financing placement fee: Negotiated with the MAP lender, typically 1% to 2% of the mortgage amount
  • Title and recording fees: Standard closing costs that vary by jurisdiction
  • Legal fees: Both borrower's and lender's counsel, plus HUD's closing attorney in some jurisdictions

When totaled, the escrows, reserves, and fees associated with a 221(d)(4) closing can add 5% to 8% to the total capital requirement beyond the basic equity contribution. Developers should model these costs carefully in their capital stacks and ensure their equity partners understand the timing and magnitude of these obligations.

When 221(d)(4) Is the Right Choice

The HUD 221(d)(4) program is not for every project or every developer. The regulatory burden, the lengthy timeline, and the Davis-Bacon cost premium make it impractical for small deals, fast-turnaround projects, or developers who plan to sell shortly after stabilization. However, for the right project profile, the program's benefits are unparalleled.

Large-scale projects. The 221(d)(4) program is most cost-effective for projects with 100 or more units where the fixed costs of the HUD process (legal fees, application fees, third-party reports) can be spread across a larger loan amount. The administrative burden is essentially the same whether the project has 50 units or 500, so larger projects benefit from significantly lower per-unit transaction costs.

Long-hold investment strategies. Developers and investors who intend to own and operate a property for 15 years or more derive the greatest benefit from the 40-year fixed rate. The certainty of knowing the exact debt service payment for decades allows for precise long-term financial planning and protects against the interest rate cycles that can erode returns on shorter-term financings.

Affordable housing developments. LIHTC developers and owners of project-based Section 8 properties gain the most favorable 221(d)(4) terms: up to 90% LTV, reduced MIP rates, and lower DSCR thresholds. These enhanced terms, combined with the 40-year fixed rate, align well with the long-term regulatory agreements and restricted cash flows that characterize affordable housing portfolios.

Patient capital with disciplined timelines. The 221(d)(4) program rewards developers who plan ahead. If your project is in early planning stages and you can accommodate a 12-to-18-month pre-construction timeline without incurring excessive carrying costs on land or predevelopment expenses, the long-term savings on financing costs can far exceed the short-term cost of patience. Developers who rush to break ground and turn to conventional construction loans often end up paying more in floating-rate interest and refinance costs than they would have spent waiting for HUD approval.

Conversely, 221(d)(4) is generally not the best fit if your project is under 50 units (the fixed costs may outweigh the benefits), if you plan to sell within 5 years (the prepayment lockout creates friction), or if you need to start construction within the next 6 months (the HUD timeline will not accommodate that urgency). In those situations, conventional construction lending, bridge financing, or agency permanent loans such as Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac may be more practical alternatives.

Frequently Asked Questions

The total timeline for a HUD 221(d)(4) project typically ranges from 24 to 36 months from initial pre-application to construction completion and final endorsement. The pre-construction phase alone usually takes 12 to 18 months, broken into three main stages: pre-application (2 to 4 months), firm application and HUD processing (6 to 10 months), and initial endorsement and closing (2 to 4 months).

After closing, the construction period can last up to 3 years depending on project size and complexity. Following construction completion, the project must achieve stabilized occupancy (typically 90% or higher for at least 3 consecutive months) before HUD issues the final endorsement. The developer must also complete a cost certification audit reconciling actual costs against the approved budget.

Developers should plan accordingly and begin the HUD process well before they need to break ground. Working with an experienced MAP-approved lender and a HUD-savvy development team can help compress timelines where possible, but the regulatory review process has built-in steps that cannot be shortened beyond a certain point. Experienced teams with clean applications and complete documentation consistently achieve faster processing times than first-time applicants.

The HUD 223(f) and 221(d)(4) programs serve fundamentally different purposes in multifamily financing. Section 223(f) is designed for the acquisition or refinancing of existing, stabilized multifamily properties where renovations are limited to no more than a specified per-unit threshold (historically around $40,000 per unit, though this figure is adjusted periodically). It does not cover new construction or gut rehabilitation.

Section 221(d)(4), by contrast, is specifically designed for new construction and substantial rehabilitation of multifamily housing with five or more units. The 221(d)(4) program finances the entire project lifecycle from construction through permanent financing in a single closing, while 223(f) deals exclusively with permanent debt on existing, operating properties.

The 221(d)(4) process is longer and more involved because HUD must review architectural plans, construction cost estimates, and oversight requirements that are not applicable to an existing building. However, 221(d)(4) offers the unique advantage of locking in a fixed rate before construction begins, eliminating the refinancing risk inherent in conventional construction lending. Both programs offer non-recourse, FHA-insured, 35-year (223f) or 40-year (221d4) fully amortizing terms. The right program depends entirely on whether you are building new or acquiring existing.

The Davis-Bacon Act is a federal law requiring that workers on federally assisted construction projects be paid no less than the locally prevailing wages and fringe benefits for the type of work they perform. Because HUD 221(d)(4) loans carry FHA mortgage insurance (a form of federal assistance), all construction funded through this program must comply with Davis-Bacon wage requirements.

In practice, this means the developer must obtain a wage determination from the Department of Labor specifying prevailing rates for each trade, ensure all contractors and subcontractors pay at least those rates, submit certified weekly payroll reports throughout the construction period, and allow HUD and DOL inspectors to verify compliance through record audits and worker interviews.

Davis-Bacon compliance typically increases total construction costs by 10% to 20% compared to non-prevailing-wage projects, depending on the local labor market. In cities with strong union presence, the incremental cost may be minimal. In markets with lower prevailing wages, the premium can be more significant. Developers should factor this cost increase into project budgets from the earliest feasibility analysis and work with general contractors who have experience managing Davis-Bacon payroll reporting and compliance documentation.

Substantial rehabilitation is generally defined as renovation work that is extensive enough to warrant treating the project similarly to new construction in terms of HUD's review and oversight requirements.

Qualifying rehabilitation work can include gut renovations, structural modifications, full replacement of major building systems (HVAC, plumbing, electrical, roofing), seismic upgrades, significant reconfiguration of unit layouts, and conversion of non-residential structures to residential use. The rehabilitation must be substantial in both scope and cost. Minor cosmetic repairs, appliance replacements, or light renovations that fall within the per-unit cost thresholds for Section 223(f) would typically be processed under that program instead.

For substantial rehab projects, HUD applies the same underwriting standards, architectural review, Davis-Bacon compliance requirements, and construction oversight as for new construction. The LTV and DSCR thresholds are identical. Developers pursuing a substantial rehab 221(d)(4) should expect a similar timeline and documentation burden as a new construction project, though some phases may be streamlined if the existing building provides a clear baseline for cost estimation and design.

HUD 221(d)(4) interest rates are not published as a single benchmark because each loan is individually priced based on current market conditions, the applicable mortgage insurance premium (MIP) tier, and the MAP-approved lender's pricing. The all-in rate has three components: the base note rate (which tracks Ginnie Mae MBS pricing, closely tied to long-term Treasury yields), the annual FHA mortgage insurance premium (ranging from 0.25% to 0.65% depending on affordability designation), and the lender's servicing fee (typically 0.05% to 0.15%).

As of early 2026, all-in rates for 221(d)(4) loans generally fall in the range of 5.5% to 7.0%. Market-rate projects at the 0.65% MIP tier sit at the higher end of this range, while affordable projects with Section 8 contracts at the 0.25% MIP tier can achieve rates near the lower end. Projects that achieve green certification may qualify for additional MIP reductions.

The most important consideration is not the absolute rate level but the fact that this rate is fixed for the full 40-year term and locked before construction begins. A conventional construction loan may offer a lower initial rate on a floating basis, but the total interest cost over 40 years is almost always higher due to rate volatility, refinance costs, and shorter amortization periods. For a current rate quote specific to your project, contact a MAP-approved lender with experience in the 221(d)(4) program.
