Commercial Construction Loans: How to Finance Ground-Up Development

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Typical LTC: 60% to 75%
  • Rate range: Prime + 1% to 3%
  • Loan term: 12 to 36 months
  • Interest reserve: Required
  • Recourse: Typically full recourse
  • Disbursement: Monthly draws

What Is a Commercial Construction Loan?

A commercial construction loan is a short-term financing instrument designed to fund the ground-up development of commercial real estate. Unlike a permanent mortgage, which is placed on a completed and income-producing property, a construction loan provides capital during the building phase, when there is no existing structure to serve as collateral and no rental income to service debt. The loan proceeds are disbursed incrementally as construction progresses, and the full balance comes due at the end of the construction period, typically within 12 to 36 months of closing.

Construction loans occupy a unique position in the commercial lending landscape because of the elevated risk they present to lenders. A project under construction is an unfinished asset. Its value depends entirely on the borrower's ability to complete the building on time, within budget, and to a standard that attracts tenants or buyers. If the project stalls or fails, the lender is left holding a partially built structure that is worth far less than the loan balance. Because of this risk profile, construction lenders impose stricter qualification standards, require larger equity contributions, and charge higher interest rates than permanent lenders.

The fundamental difference between a construction loan and a permanent loan is one of purpose and duration. A permanent loan (also called a "takeout" loan) replaces the construction loan after the project is completed and stabilized. The construction loan funds the building process; the permanent loan provides long-term financing for the finished asset. Most developers plan their capital structure around this two-step sequence: secure a construction loan to build, then refinance into a permanent loan once the property is generating income. Some programs, like the HUD 221(d)(4) and certain SBA products, combine both stages into a single closing, but the majority of commercial construction deals use a two-loan structure.

How Construction Loan Draws Work

The draw process is the central mechanism of a construction loan. Rather than funding the entire loan amount at closing, the lender disburses funds in stages as construction milestones are completed and verified. This protects the lender by ensuring that loan proceeds are used for their intended purpose and that the project is progressing as planned.

The process begins with a draw schedule, which is established at closing and outlines the anticipated timing and amounts of each disbursement. The draw schedule is tied to the project's construction budget and timeline, broken into categories such as site work, foundation, structural framing, mechanical systems, interior finishes, and landscaping. Each category has an allocated dollar amount, and the borrower can request funds for a given category only after the corresponding work has been completed.

When the borrower is ready to draw funds, the general contractor or developer submits a draw request to the lender. This request includes a detailed breakdown of work completed since the last draw, supporting invoices from subcontractors and material suppliers, and signed lien waivers from all parties who were paid in the previous draw cycle. Lien waivers are critical because they confirm that subcontractors and suppliers have been paid and will not file a mechanic's lien against the property.

After receiving the draw request, the lender dispatches a third-party construction inspector to the site. The inspector physically verifies that the work described in the request has been performed to an acceptable standard and that the percentage of completion matches the amount being requested. The lender's construction monitor may also review the project's budget status to flag any potential cost overruns or timeline slippage.

Once the inspection is approved, the lender releases the draw, typically within five to ten business days. Most construction loans operate on a monthly draw cycle, though some lenders permit biweekly or as-needed draws for fast-track projects. Each draw increases the outstanding loan balance, and interest accrues only on the amount that has been disbursed (not on the full loan commitment), which is a meaningful cost advantage during the early stages of construction when only a fraction of the loan has been drawn.

Retainage

Most construction lenders withhold a retainage of 5% to 10% from each draw. Retainage is a holdback that serves as a financial safeguard against incomplete work, latent defects, or disputes with subcontractors. The retained funds are released after the project reaches substantial completion, all punch-list items are resolved, and the borrower provides a certificate of occupancy, final lien waivers, and any other documentation the lender requires. Retainage protects the lender's interest by ensuring that sufficient funds remain available to address any issues that surface at the tail end of the project.

Interest Reserves Explained

Because a construction project does not generate rental income during the building phase, most borrowers cannot make monthly interest payments from the property's cash flow. To address this, construction lenders require an interest reserve, which is a pool of money set aside at closing specifically to cover interest payments as they accrue during the construction period.

The interest reserve is built into the total loan commitment. At closing, the lender estimates the total interest cost for the projected construction timeline, taking into account the anticipated draw schedule (since interest accrues only on disbursed funds), the interest rate, and the expected construction duration. That estimated amount is carved out of the overall loan proceeds and held in a dedicated reserve account.

Each month, the lender draws from the interest reserve to cover the accrued interest on the outstanding loan balance. As more of the loan is drawn for construction costs, the monthly interest charge increases, which is why the reserve must be sized to account for the rising balance over time. A well-structured interest reserve should include a cushion to absorb minor timeline delays, since construction projects rarely finish exactly on schedule.

If the project is completed ahead of schedule, any unused interest reserve is typically applied to reduce the outstanding loan balance or returned to the borrower. If the project runs long and the reserve is depleted, the borrower must fund interest payments out of pocket, which can create cash flow pressure at the worst possible time. This is one reason experienced developers build conservative assumptions into their project timelines and maintain personal liquidity beyond what is committed to the project.

LTC vs. LTV: Understanding Leverage

Construction lenders use two primary metrics to determine how much they are willing to lend: loan-to-cost (LTC) and loan-to-value (LTV). Both are expressed as percentages, but they measure different things, and understanding the distinction is essential for structuring your capital stack.

Loan-to-cost (LTC) compares the loan amount to the total project cost. Total project cost includes land acquisition, hard construction costs, soft costs (architectural and engineering fees, permits, legal, environmental, insurance), interest reserves, and contingency reserves. For example, if your total project cost is $10 million and the lender offers 65% LTC, the maximum loan amount is $6.5 million. The remaining $3.5 million must come from the borrower's equity, mezzanine debt, or other sources. Most construction lenders cap LTC between 60% and 75%, with higher leverage available for well-capitalized sponsors building in strong markets with pre-leasing or pre-sales in place.

Loan-to-value (LTV) compares the loan amount to the appraised value of the completed project. This is sometimes referred to as "as-completed" or "as-stabilized" value, depending on whether the appraisal assumes the building is freshly completed or fully leased. If the as-stabilized value of your project is $14 million and the lender caps LTV at 65%, the maximum loan based on value alone would be $9.1 million.

In practice, lenders use both metrics and lend to the lower of the two. On most development deals, the LTC constraint is the binding one because it is tied to actual, verifiable costs. The LTV metric acts as a secondary check, ensuring the loan does not exceed a prudent share of the property's projected market value. In cases where land was acquired well below market or the project is in a high-appreciation area, the LTV check may actually be the binding constraint.

Qualification Requirements

Construction lending involves significant risk, and lenders impose rigorous qualification standards on both the borrower and the project. The five areas that receive the most scrutiny are developer experience, equity contribution, personal guarantees, net worth and liquidity, and general contractor qualifications.

Developer experience. This is the single most important factor. Lenders want to see that the sponsor has successfully completed projects of similar size, complexity, and asset type. A developer who has built three Class A multifamily projects will have a much easier time financing the fourth than a first-time developer proposing the same project. Lenders evaluate not just the number of prior projects but also their outcomes: were they delivered on time and on budget? Did they stabilize at the projected rents? Were there disputes with contractors or cost overruns? A strong track record dramatically improves your access to capital and your ability to negotiate favorable terms.

Equity contribution. Most construction lenders require the sponsor to contribute 25% to 40% of the total project cost as equity. This equity must typically be "cash-in" equity, meaning actual cash contributed to the project (as opposed to imputed equity from land appreciation). Some lenders will give credit for land equity if the developer purchased the land at a discount or owns it free and clear, but the amount of credit varies by lender. Higher equity contributions reduce the lender's risk and can result in better pricing and terms.

Personal guarantees. The vast majority of commercial construction loans require full personal recourse guarantees from the principal sponsors. This means the guarantors are personally liable for the full loan balance if the project defaults, not just for specific carve-out events like fraud or environmental contamination. Non-recourse construction financing does exist (notably through the HUD 221(d)(4) program for multifamily), but it is the exception rather than the rule. Private debt funds and some regional banks may offer partial recourse or "burn-off" provisions where recourse reduces as the project reaches completion milestones, but full recourse at origination is the standard expectation.

Net worth and liquidity. Lenders typically require the personal guarantor to have a net worth at least equal to the loan amount and liquid assets (cash, marketable securities) equal to 10% to 20% of the loan amount. The liquidity requirement exists because construction projects frequently encounter unexpected costs, and the lender wants assurance that the borrower has the financial reserves to address problems without threatening the project's completion.

General contractor qualifications. The GC is the lender's primary partner in managing construction risk. Lenders will review the contractor's track record, financial statements, bonding capacity, insurance coverage, and references from prior projects. Many lenders require a payment and performance bond, which guarantees that the GC will complete the work and pay all subcontractors. The GC must typically carry commercial general liability insurance, builder's risk insurance, and workers' compensation coverage at levels specified by the lender.

Lender Types for Construction Financing

Several categories of lenders participate in the construction lending market, each with distinct appetites, pricing, and requirements.

Banks and credit unions. Community banks and regional banks are the most active construction lenders for projects under $50 million. They offer competitive rates (typically Prime + 1% to 2%), require full recourse guarantees, and place heavy emphasis on the sponsor's relationship with the bank and local market knowledge. Credit unions have become increasingly active in construction lending for smaller projects, particularly for member-focused asset types like medical offices, retail centers, and mixed-use buildings. Bank construction loans are typically interest-only during the construction period, with the full balance due at maturity.

Private debt funds. For deals that do not fit traditional bank parameters (first-time developers, higher-leverage requests, unusual asset types, or fast timelines), private debt funds offer a flexible alternative. These lenders charge higher rates (typically Prime + 2% to 5% or more) and origination fees (1% to 3 points), but they can close faster, accept more complex deal structures, and are often willing to go higher on LTC than banks. Private debt funds are particularly useful for bridge-to-construction scenarios, speculative development in high-growth markets, and situations where the sponsor's experience or liquidity falls short of bank requirements.

Life insurance companies. Life companies participate selectively in construction lending, primarily for large, institutional-quality projects in core markets with significant pre-leasing. Their rates tend to be competitive with banks, and they may offer longer initial terms (36 to 48 months), but their underwriting is conservative and their approval timelines are longer. Life company construction loans are typically reserved for projects above $25 million with experienced, well-capitalized sponsors.

HUD 221(d)(4) for multifamily. The FHA's 221(d)(4) program is a powerful option for multifamily construction. It provides non-recourse, fixed-rate construction-to-permanent financing with terms up to 40 years (plus the construction period). The program covers up to 85% LTC for market-rate projects and up to 90% for affordable housing. The trade-off is a lengthy approval process (often 9 to 12 months from application to closing), significant upfront costs (including a mortgage insurance premium), and strict Davis-Bacon prevailing wage requirements for construction labor. For developers building large multifamily projects who can plan around the timeline, 221(d)(4) offers leverage and terms that no other construction lending program can match.

Ground-Up Construction vs. Substantial Rehabilitation

Lenders distinguish between ground-up new construction and substantial rehabilitation (sometimes called "gut rehab" or "adaptive reuse") because the two carry different risk profiles.

Ground-up construction starts with a vacant site and builds an entirely new structure. The lender has no existing building to fall back on if the project stalls; the collateral during construction is raw land plus whatever portion of the building has been completed. This makes ground-up deals the riskiest form of construction lending, and lenders price and structure them accordingly, with lower LTC ratios, higher equity requirements, and stricter experience thresholds.

Substantial rehabilitation involves renovating an existing structure, often to a degree that rivals the scope and cost of new construction. Examples include converting a historic warehouse into loft apartments, repurposing an office building as a hotel, or stripping a retail center down to its shell and rebuilding the interior. Because there is an existing structure in place, the lender's collateral position is somewhat stronger than in a ground-up scenario: even if the project stalls, the building has some residual value. However, rehab projects carry their own set of risks, including unexpected structural issues, environmental contamination (particularly in older buildings), code compliance challenges, and the difficulty of accurately budgeting renovation costs before demolition and investigation work are complete.

From a lending perspective, substantial rehab projects may qualify for slightly higher LTC ratios (because the existing structure provides a collateral floor) and may be eligible for programs that do not apply to new construction, such as historic tax credit financing or certain state economic development incentives. However, lenders expect detailed condition assessments, environmental reports, and a contingency budget that reflects the inherent uncertainty of working with an existing building.

Construction-to-Permanent (C2P) Loans

A construction-to-permanent loan, often called a C2P loan or a "single-close" construction loan, combines the construction financing and the permanent mortgage into a single transaction. Instead of closing two separate loans (one for construction and one for the permanent takeout), the borrower closes once, saving time and money on duplicate closing costs, title insurance, appraisals, and legal fees.

In a C2P structure, the loan functions as a standard construction loan during the building phase, with interest-only payments funded from the interest reserve and monthly draws based on construction progress. Once the project is complete and meets the lender's conversion requirements (typically a certificate of occupancy, final inspection, and evidence of stabilization), the loan automatically converts to a permanent amortizing mortgage with a fixed or variable rate and a term of 15 to 30 years, depending on the program.

Single Close vs. Two Close

The two-close approach gives the borrower more flexibility. You can shop for the best permanent financing after the project is complete, potentially securing better terms than what was available at the time of construction loan origination. The downside is the cost and uncertainty of executing two separate closings and the risk that permanent financing terms may be less favorable than expected if market conditions shift during construction.

The single-close approach eliminates refinance risk and reduces transaction costs, but it locks the borrower into permanent loan terms that were negotiated before the building exists. If interest rates decline during construction, the borrower may be stuck with a higher rate than what the market would otherwise offer at conversion.

C2P Through Government Programs

Two notable government-backed programs offer construction-to-permanent financing. The HUD 221(d)(4) program provides non-recourse, fixed-rate C2P loans for multifamily new construction and substantial rehabilitation, with combined terms of up to 40 years plus the construction period. The SBA 504 program can also be structured as a C2P loan for owner-occupied commercial real estate, combining SBA-backed permanent financing with a construction phase funded through a conventional lender. Both programs offer attractive long-term terms but require longer processing times and more extensive documentation than conventional C2P products.

Hard Costs vs. Soft Costs

Every construction budget is divided into two major categories: hard costs and soft costs. Understanding this distinction is important because lenders evaluate each category separately and may apply different funding rules to each.

Hard costs represent the physical construction work and materials. They are the largest component of the budget, typically accounting for 60% to 80% of total project cost depending on the property type and location. Soft costs cover the professional services, regulatory requirements, financing costs, and administrative expenses that surround the physical construction process. Lenders scrutinize both categories closely and may require third-party cost estimates or independent budget reviews to confirm that the numbers are realistic. Most lenders also require a hard cost contingency of 5% to 10% to absorb unexpected expenses during construction.

Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

Construction lending is one of the highest-risk segments of commercial real estate finance, and borrowers who fail to plan for common problems can find themselves in serious trouble. Here are the issues that most frequently derail construction projects and the steps experienced developers take to mitigate them.

Cost overruns. Material prices, labor shortages, and unexpected site conditions are the primary drivers of cost overruns. To protect against this risk, build a hard cost contingency of at least 5% to 10% into your budget, negotiate a guaranteed maximum price (GMP) contract with your general contractor when possible, and obtain fixed-price subcontracts for the largest trade items. Monitor your budget closely throughout construction by reviewing each draw request against the original line-item budget and investigating any variances immediately.

Timeline delays. Weather, permitting holdups, material delivery delays, labor availability, and inspection scheduling can all push a project past its expected completion date. Because construction loans have finite terms (typically 12 to 36 months), delays can cause the loan to mature before the project is finished. Negotiate an extension option (typically 6 to 12 months, with a fee) at origination rather than trying to obtain one after problems arise. Build a realistic timeline that includes weather contingency days, and sequence your permitting and procurement activities to start as early as possible.

Draw disputes. Disagreements between the borrower, the GC, and the lender's inspector over the percentage of work completed can delay funding and create cash flow problems for the project. To minimize disputes, maintain detailed progress documentation with photographs, keep all subcontractor invoices organized and reconciled to the budget, and establish a clear draw request process at the start of the project. Developing a good working relationship with the lender's inspector can also help resolve minor discrepancies quickly.

Change orders. Design changes during construction are expensive and disruptive. Each change order must be evaluated for cost impact, schedule impact, and budget availability. A pattern of frequent change orders signals poor planning and can erode lender confidence. Invest heavily in the pre-construction design phase to minimize changes once building begins, and establish a formal change order approval process that requires sign-off from both the developer and the lender before any work proceeds.

Interest rate exposure. Because construction loans are typically priced at a floating rate tied to Prime or SOFR, rising rates during the construction period can increase borrowing costs significantly. If your interest reserve was sized based on lower rate assumptions, a rate increase can deplete the reserve faster than anticipated. Consider negotiating a rate cap at origination to limit your maximum interest cost, and size your interest reserve conservatively to account for potential rate increases during the construction period.

The Construction Loan Timeline

A commercial construction project follows a predictable sequence of milestones from initial site acquisition through certificate of occupancy. Understanding this timeline helps borrowers plan their capital needs and communicate effectively with lenders.

  1. Site acquisition and entitlement (3 to 12 months before construction loan closing). The developer identifies and acquires the site, secures zoning approvals, obtains environmental clearance, and completes preliminary design. Many developers finance the land acquisition separately (through a land loan or personal capital) and bring the entitled site to the construction lender as part of their equity contribution.
  2. Pre-development and design (2 to 6 months). Architectural plans are finalized, engineering is completed, permits are submitted, and the developer selects a general contractor through a competitive bid or negotiated process. The construction budget and schedule are finalized during this phase.
  3. Construction loan closing (30 to 90 days). The lender completes underwriting, orders the appraisal and third-party reports, and issues a commitment letter. After the borrower satisfies all closing conditions, the loan closes, and the first draw (typically for site mobilization and initial soft costs) is disbursed.
  4. Vertical construction (6 to 24 months depending on project size). The building rises from the ground. Monthly draws fund each phase of construction, with the lender's inspector verifying progress before each disbursement. The developer manages the GC, monitors the budget, and addresses any issues that arise.
  5. Substantial completion and punch list (1 to 2 months). The building is essentially complete. The GC and developer walk the project to identify punch-list items (minor deficiencies that need correction). The lender's final inspection confirms that the project matches the approved plans and specifications.
  6. Certificate of occupancy and stabilization. The local building authority issues a certificate of occupancy (CO), confirming that the building meets all code requirements and is safe for its intended use. The developer begins lease-up or commences operations. Once the property is stabilized (typically defined as 90% or more occupied), the borrower refinances into permanent financing or, in a C2P structure, the loan converts automatically.

Choosing the Right Construction Lender

Selecting a construction lender is not just about getting the lowest rate. Construction lending is a relationship-intensive product that requires close collaboration between the borrower and the lender over 12 to 36 months of building activity. The lender you choose will control the draw process, approve (or delay) your funding requests, and have significant influence over the project's financial outcome. Here is what to evaluate when comparing construction lenders.

Construction lending experience. How many construction loans has the lender originated in the past two years? Do they have a dedicated construction lending team, or is construction a side activity for a generalist commercial lending group? Experienced construction lenders have established draw administration processes, maintain relationships with reliable third-party inspectors, and understand the realities of construction timelines. Inexperienced lenders may impose rigid draw requirements that slow down funding or misunderstand standard construction practices.

Draw process efficiency. The speed and reliability of the draw process directly affects your project's cash flow and your relationship with your general contractor. Ask prospective lenders about their typical draw turnaround time (from request submission to funding), how inspections are scheduled, and whether they use an in-house team or a third-party administrator. Some lenders offer online draw portals that streamline the documentation process, while others still rely on manual submission and review.

Extension and modification flexibility. Construction projects rarely go exactly according to plan. Ask whether the lender offers built-in extension options, what the extension fees are, and what their process is for handling budget modifications or scope changes. A lender that is willing to work constructively through problems is far more valuable than one that offers a slightly lower rate but becomes adversarial when issues arise.

Recourse requirements. Understand exactly what the lender expects in terms of personal guarantees. Is the loan full recourse for the entire term, or does recourse reduce as the project reaches completion milestones? Are there completion guarantees that burn off at certificate of occupancy? What net worth and liquidity covenants apply to the guarantors? Negotiate these terms carefully, because the recourse structure determines your personal financial exposure if the project encounters problems.

Relationship potential. If you plan to develop multiple projects over time, consider whether the lender is a good long-term partner. Banks and credit unions that specialize in construction lending often provide repeat borrowers with streamlined approval processes, better pricing, and more flexibility. Building a relationship with a construction lender who understands your business and track record can be one of the most valuable competitive advantages a developer possesses.

Frequently Asked Questions

Construction loan draws are periodic disbursements tied to the completion of specific phases of your project. The borrower or general contractor submits a draw request detailing the work completed since the last draw, along with supporting invoices and lien waivers from subcontractors. The lender then sends a third-party inspector to the site to verify that the work has been performed as described and meets quality standards.

Once the inspection is approved, the lender releases the funds, usually within five to ten business days. Most construction loans use a monthly draw cycle. Lenders also withhold a retainage amount (typically 5% to 10% of each draw) as protection against incomplete work or contractor disputes. This retainage is released after substantial completion, final inspections, and receipt of all required lien waivers and the certificate of occupancy.

Interest accrues only on the amount that has been disbursed, not on the full loan commitment. This means your interest cost is lower during the early stages of construction and increases as more of the loan is drawn. The draw schedule is established at closing and serves as the roadmap for the entire disbursement process.

An interest reserve is a portion of the construction loan that is set aside at closing to cover monthly interest payments during the building period. Because a project under construction produces no rental income, the borrower typically cannot service debt from property cash flow. The interest reserve pre-funds these payments from the loan proceeds themselves, eliminating the need for the borrower to make out-of-pocket interest payments during construction.

At closing, the lender calculates the estimated total interest cost based on the projected draw schedule, loan amount, interest rate, and expected construction duration. That amount is held in a reserve account and drawn down each month to cover accrued interest. If the project finishes early, any unused reserve is applied to reduce the loan balance. If the project runs long and the reserve is depleted, the borrower must fund interest payments from personal funds.

The interest reserve is a standard feature of virtually all commercial construction loans. Its size directly affects the total loan commitment and, by extension, the borrower's equity requirement. Experienced developers build conservative timeline assumptions into the reserve calculation to avoid the risk of depletion.

Obtaining a construction loan with no prior development experience is very challenging but not impossible. Lenders view sponsor experience as one of the most critical risk factors in construction lending. A first-time developer will face skepticism from most institutional lenders, and will need to compensate with other strengths.

The most effective strategy is to partner with an experienced developer or general contractor who brings a proven track record. Many lenders will evaluate the experience of the project team as a whole. You can also begin with a smaller, simpler project to build credibility before attempting larger ground-up development. Some private debt funds and hard money lenders are more flexible on experience requirements, though they charge higher rates and fees.

Regardless of your approach, expect to contribute more equity (often 30% to 40% of total project cost), provide full personal guarantees, and demonstrate strong net worth and liquidity. Starting small, partnering wisely, and documenting every aspect of your first project will position you for easier access to capital on future deals.

If your project exceeds the original budget, the lender will not automatically increase the loan to cover the shortfall. The construction loan commitment is fixed at closing, and any cost overrun is the borrower's responsibility. For minor overruns, developers typically draw from the contingency reserve (usually 5% to 10% of hard costs) that was built into the original budget.

For larger overruns, options include injecting additional personal equity, negotiating value engineering with the contractor to reduce remaining costs, or seeking mezzanine or preferred equity financing to fill the gap. In the worst case, if the borrower cannot fund the overrun and the project stalls, the lender may declare a default.

To protect against this risk, experienced developers build realistic contingency reserves, negotiate guaranteed maximum price (GMP) contracts with their general contractors, lock in pricing for major materials early, and maintain personal liquidity beyond what is committed to the project. Monitoring the budget closely at every draw cycle helps catch variances early, when they are easier and cheaper to address.

LTC (loan-to-cost) and LTV (loan-to-value) are both leverage metrics used to size construction loans, but they measure different things. LTC compares the loan amount to the total project cost, including land, hard costs, soft costs, and reserves. If your project costs $10 million and the lender offers 70% LTC, your maximum loan is $7 million.

LTV compares the loan amount to the appraised value of the completed project. If the finished project appraises at $14 million and the lender caps LTV at 65%, the maximum loan based on value would be $9.1 million. In construction lending, lenders calculate both ratios and lend to the lower of the two, meaning your actual loan is constrained by whichever metric produces the smaller number.

LTC is typically the primary sizing tool because it is based on verifiable costs. LTV serves as a secondary check to ensure the loan does not exceed a reasonable share of the property's projected market value. Understanding both metrics is essential for planning your equity contribution and structuring the rest of your capital stack.
