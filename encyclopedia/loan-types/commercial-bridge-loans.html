What Is a Commercial Bridge Loan?

A commercial bridge loan is a short-term financing tool designed to "bridge" the gap between an immediate capital need and a longer-term financial solution. Unlike traditional permanent loans with 5 to 30 year terms, bridge loans typically last 6 to 36 months and are structured as interest-only, meaning the borrower pays only interest during the loan term with the full principal due at maturity.

Bridge loans serve a critical function in commercial real estate because they provide speed and flexibility that conventional lenders cannot match. While a bank loan might take 60 to 90 days to close, a bridge loan can fund in as little as 1 to 3 weeks. This speed premium comes at a cost: bridge loans carry higher interest rates and origination fees compared to permanent financing. But for the right situation, the cost is well worth the speed and certainty of execution.

In 2026, bridge loans have become especially important as the $875 billion commercial loan maturity wall forces borrowers to find interim solutions while they stabilize properties, wait for more favorable permanent lending conditions, or complete value-add renovations that will improve their refinancing position.

Common Use Cases for Bridge Loans

Bridge loans are versatile, but they work best in specific situations where speed, flexibility, or transitional property conditions make conventional lending impractical.

1. Acquisition Before Permanent Financing

When a deal must close quickly (such as a competitive bidding situation or a seller deadline), a bridge loan provides the speed needed to secure the property. The borrower then refinances into permanent financing once the acquisition is complete and the property is stabilized.

2. Value-Add Renovation

Properties that need significant capital improvements to reach their full income potential are not attractive to permanent lenders. A bridge loan provides acquisition capital plus renovation funds. Once renovations are complete and the property is re-leased at higher rents, the borrower refinances into a long-term loan at the improved value.

3. Distressed Property Turnaround

Properties with high vacancy, deferred maintenance, or below-market rents need capital and time to stabilize. Bridge lenders specialize in evaluating the "as-stabilized" value and lending based on the property's potential rather than just its current condition.

4. Maturity Wall Solutions

Borrowers with loans maturing in 2026 who cannot qualify for permanent refinancing (due to lower property values, tighter DSCR requirements, or higher rates) use bridge loans to buy 12 to 36 months of additional time while working to improve their property's financial position.

5. 1031 Exchange Acquisitions

The strict 45-day identification and 180-day closing deadlines of a 1031 exchange make bridge loans a natural fit. Speed of execution is critical, and bridge lenders can accommodate the compressed timeline.

How Bridge Loan Pricing Works

Bridge loan costs have several components beyond just the interest rate. Understanding the full cost structure is essential for evaluating whether a bridge loan makes financial sense for your deal.

  • Interest rate: Typically floating, based on a reference rate (usually SOFR or Prime) plus a spread. All-in rates generally range from 8% to 14% or higher, depending on leverage, property type, and borrower strength.
  • Origination fee (points): Charged upfront at closing. Typically 1 to 3 points (1 point = 1% of the loan amount). A $5 million bridge loan with 2 points costs $100,000 in origination fees.
  • Exit fee: Some bridge lenders charge a fee when the loan is paid off, typically 0.25% to 1% of the loan amount.
  • Extension fees: If the borrower needs more time, extension options (usually 6 to 12 months each) are available for an additional 0.25% to 0.50% of the loan balance.
  • Legal and closing costs: Borrower typically pays the lender's legal counsel fees in addition to standard closing costs (title, escrow, recording).
  • Interest reserve: Many bridge lenders require an interest reserve, which is funds set aside at closing to cover several months of interest payments. This increases the effective loan amount.

Qualification Requirements

Bridge lenders evaluate deals differently than conventional banks. While traditional lenders focus heavily on the borrower's credit score and income history, bridge lenders prioritize the property and the exit strategy.

  • Exit strategy (most important): Lenders need to see a clear, realistic plan for repaying the bridge loan. This is typically refinancing into permanent debt or selling the property. The exit strategy should be specific and supported by market data.
  • Property value and location: The property is the primary collateral. Lenders want to see that the property can support the loan even in a downside scenario.
  • Sponsor experience: Borrowers with a track record of similar projects receive better terms. First-time borrowers may face higher rates or lower leverage.
  • Net worth and liquidity: Lenders want sponsors with net worth at least equal to the loan amount and liquid assets sufficient to cover 6 to 12 months of debt service.
  • Business plan: For value-add or repositioning deals, a detailed renovation budget, timeline, and projected stabilized income are required.

The Exit Strategy: Why It Matters Most

The single most important factor in bridge lending is the exit strategy. Unlike permanent loans where the lender expects to hold the debt for years, bridge lenders expect to be repaid within a defined period. A weak or unrealistic exit strategy is the number one reason bridge loan applications are declined.

Strong exit strategies include:

  • Permanent refinance with a specific lender type identified: For example, "refinance into Fannie Mae DUS once property reaches 90% occupancy and 1.25x DSCR." This is specific and measurable.
  • Property sale with market comparables: Demonstrating that similar properties have sold recently at values that support repayment of the bridge loan.
  • Take-out commitment: Having a permanent lender already issue a conditional commitment letter for long-term financing.

Weak exit strategies include vague statements like "we will refinance when the market improves" or relying on interest rate declines that may not materialize. Lenders want to see that the borrower controls the variables driving the exit.

Typical Bridge Loan Structure

A standard commercial bridge loan has several structural features that differ from permanent financing:

  • Interest-only payments: No principal amortization during the term. This keeps monthly payments lower and preserves cash flow for renovations or stabilization.
  • Floating rate: Most bridge loans carry a floating interest rate (SOFR plus a spread), though some lenders offer fixed-rate bridge options at a premium.
  • Initial term plus extensions: A common structure is 24 months initial with two 6-month extensions (written as "2+1+1"). Extensions require the borrower to meet certain conditions, such as paying an extension fee and demonstrating progress on the business plan.
  • Holdback or future funding: For value-add deals, the lender may commit to a total loan amount but hold back a portion that is released as renovation milestones are completed.
  • Prepayment flexibility: Most bridge loans have minimal or no prepayment penalties, unlike CMBS or agency loans. This flexibility allows borrowers to refinance as soon as the property qualifies for permanent financing.
  • Recourse or non-recourse: Larger bridge loans ($3M and above) from institutional lenders are often non-recourse with standard carve-outs. Smaller bridge loans typically require personal recourse.

Risk Factors to Consider

Bridge loans carry risks that borrowers should evaluate carefully before proceeding.

  • Higher total cost: The combination of higher interest rates, origination fees, and shorter terms means bridge loans cost significantly more per year than permanent financing. A bridge loan should be a temporary tool, not a long-term solution.
  • Maturity risk: If the exit strategy does not work as planned (the renovation takes longer, the property does not lease up, or permanent rates remain too high), the borrower may face a maturity default.
  • Renovation risk: For value-add deals, construction delays, cost overruns, or unexpected property issues can erode the financial assumptions that made the deal work on paper.
  • Rate risk: Floating-rate bridge loans expose borrowers to rising rates. If SOFR increases during the bridge term, monthly interest payments increase accordingly.

How to Find the Right Bridge Lender

The bridge lending market includes hundreds of active lenders, each with different appetites for property type, geography, loan size, and leverage. Finding the right match matters significantly because terms can vary dramatically between lenders.

  • Lender specialization: Some bridge lenders focus on multifamily, others on hospitality or retail. Working with a lender experienced in your property type leads to smoother execution.
  • Certainty of close: The lowest rate means nothing if the lender cannot close on time. Ask about the lender's recent closing track record and typical timeline.
  • Flexibility for modifications: If your business plan changes mid-loan, you want a lender who can accommodate modifications without excessive fees or delays.
  • Extension terms: Understand the extension fees and conditions upfront. The initial rate is only part of the total cost if you need more time.

When a Bridge Loan Is NOT the Right Choice

Bridge loans are powerful tools, but they are not appropriate for every situation.

  • Stabilized properties with strong cash flow: If your property already qualifies for permanent financing, paying bridge loan rates is an unnecessary expense.
  • No clear exit strategy: If you cannot articulate how and when you will repay the bridge loan, you should not take one. "Hoping the market improves" is not a strategy.
  • Insufficient equity: If the deal requires leverage beyond 80% LTV, a bridge loan alone will not solve the capital gap. Consider mezzanine debt or preferred equity as supplemental capital.
  • Long-term hold with no renovation: If you plan to hold for 10+ years and the property does not need improvements, pursue permanent financing from the start, even if it takes longer to close.
