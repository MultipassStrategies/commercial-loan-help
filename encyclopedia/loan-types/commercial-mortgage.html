What Is a Commercial Mortgage?

A commercial mortgage is a loan secured by commercial real estate rather than residential property. Borrowers use commercial mortgages to purchase, refinance, or renovate income-producing properties such as office buildings, retail centers, apartment complexes with five or more units, industrial warehouses, and hotels. The property itself serves as collateral for the loan, meaning the lender can foreclose on the building if the borrower fails to make payments.

Unlike a home loan, where underwriting focuses primarily on the borrower's personal income and credit score, commercial mortgage underwriting places significant emphasis on the property's ability to generate enough income to cover debt payments. Lenders evaluate what is known as the debt service coverage ratio (DSCR), which compares the property's net operating income to its annual debt obligations. A DSCR of 1.25x, for example, means the property produces 25% more income than needed to service the loan.

Commercial mortgages are originated by a variety of institutions, including traditional banks, credit unions, life insurance companies, government-backed programs like the SBA, conduit lenders that package loans into commercial mortgage-backed securities (CMBS), and private capital sources. Each lender type brings different strengths, rate structures, and qualification criteria to the table, giving borrowers a wide range of options depending on their specific needs.

How Commercial Mortgages Differ from Residential Mortgages

Although both commercial and residential mortgages are secured by real property, the two products differ in several important ways. Understanding these differences helps borrowers set realistic expectations before entering the application process.

Feature Residential Mortgage Commercial Mortgage
Borrower Individual person Business entity (LLC, corporation, partnership)
Primary underwriting focus Borrower income and credit Property income (DSCR) and borrower financials
Typical loan term 15 or 30 years, fully amortizing 5 to 30 years, often with balloon payment
Interest rates Generally lower Generally 0.5% to 2% higher than residential
Down payment 3% to 20% 20% to 35%
Prepayment penalties Rare on most conventional loans Common; may include yield maintenance or defeasance
Closing timeline 30 to 45 days 30 to 90 days (sometimes longer)
Government regulation Heavily regulated (TILA, RESPA, Dodd-Frank) Less consumer protection regulation

One of the most significant structural differences is the balloon payment. Many commercial mortgages have a term shorter than the amortization schedule. For example, a loan might amortize over 25 years but come due after 10. At that point, the borrower must either refinance the remaining balance, pay it off in full, or sell the property. This maturity date creates refinance risk that borrowers must plan for well in advance.

Another key distinction is that commercial mortgages are typically held in a business entity, such as an LLC or corporation, rather than in the borrower's personal name. This structure offers liability protection and is standard practice in commercial real estate. However, lenders still require personal guarantees from the principal owners in most cases, especially for smaller loans.

Types of Commercial Mortgages

The commercial lending market includes several distinct loan categories, each designed for different borrower profiles, property types, and investment strategies. Here is an overview of the most common options.

Conventional Bank Loans

Traditional banks and regional banks are the most common source of commercial mortgages for small to mid-size deals. Bank loans typically offer competitive fixed or variable interest rates, flexible terms, and the ability to build a long-term banking relationship. Loan amounts generally range from $500,000 to $25 million, though larger banks handle bigger transactions. Banks tend to favor stabilized properties with strong occupancy and experienced borrowers. Approval timelines usually fall between 45 and 75 days.

CMBS (Conduit) Loans

Commercial mortgage-backed securities loans are originated by conduit lenders, pooled together, and sold to investors on the secondary market. CMBS loans are popular for larger deals (typically $2 million and above) because they offer non-recourse terms, competitive fixed rates, and longer lock periods. The trade-off is less flexibility: CMBS loans come with standardized terms, strict prepayment penalties (usually defeasance or yield maintenance), and limited ability to modify the loan after closing. These loans work best for stabilized assets with predictable cash flow.

Life Insurance Company Loans

Life insurance companies are among the most conservative commercial lenders, but they also offer some of the lowest interest rates in the market. These lenders prefer high-quality, stabilized properties in strong markets with low leverage (typically 55% to 65% LTV). Loan sizes generally start at $2 million to $5 million. Life company loans frequently feature long fixed-rate terms of 10 to 30 years, full or partial interest-only periods, and non-recourse structures. The application process can be lengthy, often taking 60 to 120 days, but the favorable terms make these loans highly attractive for the right deals.

SBA Loans (7(a) and 504)

The U.S. Small Business Administration offers two loan programs commonly used for commercial real estate. The SBA 7(a) program provides loans up to $5 million for owner-occupied commercial property with down payments as low as 10%. The SBA 504 program combines a conventional bank loan (50% of the project cost) with a CDC-funded second mortgage (up to 40%), requiring the borrower to contribute just 10% to 15% equity. Both programs feature long terms (up to 25 years), competitive rates, and accessible qualification requirements, making them ideal for small business owners purchasing or expanding their own facilities. The borrower must occupy at least 51% of the property for a 7(a) loan or 51% for existing buildings (60% for new construction) under the 504 program.

Credit Union Loans

Credit unions have become increasingly active in commercial real estate lending, particularly for smaller deals under $5 million. Because they are member-owned and not-for-profit, credit unions can often offer lower fees, competitive rates, and more personalized service than larger banks. They tend to focus on local markets and may be more willing to work with borrowers who have unique circumstances. However, credit unions may have lower lending limits and less experience with complex deal structures compared to larger institutions.

Private and Bridge Loans

Private lenders, also called hard money lenders, provide short-term financing for borrowers who need to close quickly, are purchasing value-add or transitional properties, or do not yet qualify for conventional financing. Bridge loans typically carry higher interest rates (8% to 14%) and shorter terms (6 months to 3 years), but they offer speed, flexibility, and less stringent qualification requirements. Borrowers commonly use bridge loans to acquire and renovate a property, then refinance into permanent conventional financing once the asset is stabilized. Loan-to-value ratios on bridge loans can reach 75% to 80% of the as-is value, and some lenders will also fund a portion of renovation costs.

Qualification Requirements

Commercial mortgage lenders evaluate both the property and the borrower when making lending decisions. While each lender has its own specific criteria, the following factors are consistently important across the industry.

Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR)

The DSCR is the single most important metric in commercial mortgage underwriting. It measures whether the property generates enough net operating income (NOI) to cover its annual debt service payments. Most lenders require a minimum DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x, meaning the property must produce 20% to 25% more income than the loan payment requires. Some conservative lenders, particularly life insurance companies, may require a DSCR of 1.30x or higher. Properties with lower DSCRs may still qualify with compensating factors such as a larger down payment, additional collateral, or strong borrower financials. To learn more about how DSCR works, see our DSCR Explained guide.

Loan-to-Value Ratio (LTV)

LTV measures the loan amount as a percentage of the property's appraised value. Most commercial mortgage lenders cap LTV at 65% to 80%, depending on the property type, loan program, and borrower strength. This means borrowers typically need to provide a down payment of 20% to 35%. Owner-occupied properties financed through SBA programs may qualify for higher leverage, with LTVs up to 90%. Lower LTVs generally result in better interest rates and terms because the lender's risk is reduced.

Credit Score and Financial History

While the property's income is the primary focus, lenders also review the borrower's personal credit history and financial standing. Most conventional lenders look for a minimum credit score of 680, with 700 or higher preferred for the best rates. Lenders also review the borrower's personal financial statement, tax returns (typically two to three years), and any outstanding liabilities. A history of bankruptcy, foreclosure, or significant delinquencies within the past several years can disqualify a borrower from conventional programs, though alternative lenders may still consider the application.

Net Worth and Liquidity

Lenders want to see that borrowers have a net worth at least equal to the loan amount being requested. They also look for post-closing liquidity, meaning cash reserves available after the down payment and closing costs are paid. A common benchmark is six to twelve months of debt service payments held in reserve. These requirements ensure the borrower can weather vacancies, unexpected repairs, or other short-term financial pressures without defaulting on the loan.

Experience

Many lenders prefer borrowers who have a track record of owning and managing commercial properties. Experience demonstrates competence and reduces the lender's perceived risk. First-time investors may still qualify, particularly for owner-occupied properties or smaller deals, but they should expect stricter terms. Partnering with an experienced co-investor or hiring third-party property management can help offset a lack of personal experience.

The Application Process: An Overview

Applying for a commercial mortgage involves more documentation and a longer timeline than most residential loans. While the exact process varies by lender, the following steps provide a general roadmap.

  1. Pre-qualification and deal screening. Before submitting a formal application, most borrowers present their deal to one or more lenders for an initial review. The lender evaluates the property type, location, income, and borrower profile to determine whether the deal fits their lending criteria. This step can take a few days to two weeks.
  2. Application and document submission. Once a lender expresses interest, the borrower submits a formal application along with supporting documents. These typically include personal financial statements, two to three years of tax returns (personal and business), a current rent roll, historical operating statements for the property, a business plan or executive summary, and entity formation documents. Some lenders also require a property condition assessment at this stage.
  3. Underwriting and due diligence. The lender's underwriting team reviews all submitted materials, orders a third-party appraisal, and may commission additional reports such as an environmental Phase I assessment, a property condition report, and a seismic study (in certain regions). This phase typically takes three to six weeks and is the most time-intensive part of the process.
  4. Loan commitment (term sheet). If underwriting is favorable, the lender issues a commitment letter or term sheet outlining the approved loan amount, interest rate, term, amortization, fees, and conditions that must be satisfied before closing. The borrower reviews and accepts the commitment, often paying a commitment fee or good-faith deposit at this stage.
  5. Closing. After all conditions are met, legal documents are prepared, title insurance is obtained, and the loan closes through an escrow or settlement process. Funds are disbursed, and the mortgage is recorded against the property. Total time from application to closing typically ranges from 30 to 90 days, depending on deal complexity and lender efficiency.

Costs and Fees

Commercial mortgages carry a range of upfront and ongoing costs that borrowers should anticipate when budgeting for a transaction. Below is a summary of the most common fees.

Fee Typical Range Notes
Origination fee 0.5% to 2% of loan amount Charged by the lender at closing
Appraisal $2,500 to $10,000+ Varies by property size and complexity
Environmental (Phase I) $2,000 to $5,000 Required by most lenders
Title insurance $2,000 to $15,000+ Based on loan amount
Legal fees $3,000 to $15,000+ Borrower often pays lender's legal costs as well
Survey $2,000 to $8,000 May be required depending on property and lender
Property condition report $2,000 to $6,000 Assesses physical condition and deferred maintenance
Commitment/application fee $500 to $5,000 Sometimes credited toward closing costs
Prepayment penalty Varies widely Step-down, yield maintenance, or defeasance

In total, borrowers should expect to pay closing costs of 2% to 5% of the loan amount. These costs are in addition to the down payment and any required reserves. Some fees, such as the appraisal and environmental assessment, are due early in the process and are typically non-refundable even if the loan does not close. It is important to review all fee disclosures carefully and factor these costs into your overall project budget.

When to Consider a Commercial Mortgage

A commercial mortgage makes sense in a variety of scenarios, whether you are an investor seeking to build a portfolio or a business owner looking to own your operating space. Here are some of the most common situations where commercial mortgage financing is the right tool.

Purchasing income-producing property. If you are acquiring an apartment building, retail center, office property, or industrial facility, a commercial mortgage lets you leverage the property's income to support the debt. This is the most straightforward use case, and the widest variety of lenders compete for this type of business.

Buying your own business space. Small business owners who are tired of paying rent can use a commercial mortgage (often through an SBA program) to purchase the building they operate from. Owner-occupied financing frequently offers better terms, lower down payments, and longer amortization periods than investor loans.

Refinancing an existing commercial loan. Borrowers with maturing loans, adjustable-rate debt, or unfavorable terms can refinance into a new commercial mortgage to secure a better rate, extend the term, or pull out equity (cash-out refinance). With interest rates and market conditions changing regularly, refinancing is a strategic move that many commercial property owners make every five to ten years. See our CRE Refinance Guide for a detailed walkthrough.

Expanding or renovating a property. A commercial mortgage can fund major renovations, tenant improvements, or building expansions. Some lenders offer construction-to-permanent loans that convert from a short-term construction loan into a long-term mortgage once the project is complete, streamlining the financing process.

Consolidating multiple properties. Investors who own several commercial assets with different lenders can sometimes consolidate them under a single blanket mortgage, simplifying their debt management and potentially negotiating better terms based on the combined portfolio strength.

Ultimately, a commercial mortgage is one of the most powerful tools available for building wealth through real estate. The key to success is understanding your options, preparing a strong application, and selecting a lender whose terms align with your investment strategy and risk tolerance. Most traditional lenders require a minimum personal credit score of 680 for a commercial mortgage, though some banks prefer 700 or higher. SBA lenders may accept scores as low as 650 for well-qualified borrowers with strong collateral. Private and bridge lenders are often more flexible, sometimes working with borrowers who have scores in the 620 range if the deal fundamentals are strong. Keep in mind that your credit score is just one factor; lenders also weigh the property's income, your net worth, and your track record as a borrower.

Yes, it is possible to obtain a commercial mortgage without prior real estate investment experience, though your options may be more limited. Many SBA lenders and community banks will work with first-time commercial borrowers, especially for owner-occupied properties. You can strengthen your application by partnering with an experienced co-sponsor, hiring professional property management, and demonstrating strong personal financials. Expect to provide a larger down payment (25% to 30%) and accept slightly higher interest rates to offset the lender's perceived risk.

Commercial mortgages can be used to finance a wide range of income-producing and owner-occupied properties. Common qualifying property types include office buildings, retail centers, industrial warehouses, multifamily apartment buildings with five or more units, mixed-use properties, hotels, and special-purpose facilities such as self-storage or medical offices. Raw land and certain niche property types (like gas stations or churches) may also qualify, though they typically require specialized lenders and carry higher rates. The key requirement is that the property must be used for business or investment purposes rather than as a primary residence.

Commercial mortgage rates are determined by a combination of market benchmarks and deal-specific risk factors. Lenders typically start with a base index such as the U.S. Treasury yield, SOFR, or the prime rate, then add a spread (margin) that reflects the risk of the loan. Factors that influence the spread include the property type, loan-to-value ratio, debt service coverage ratio, borrower creditworthiness, loan term, and whether the rate is fixed or variable. Loans on stable, well-occupied properties with strong borrowers typically receive the most favorable rates, while higher-risk deals involving transitional properties or less experienced sponsors carry wider spreads.

In a recourse loan, the borrower is personally liable for the full loan balance if the property's value or income falls short during a default. This means the lender can pursue the borrower's personal assets, including bank accounts, other real estate, and investments, to recover losses. In a non-recourse loan, the lender's recovery is limited to the collateral property itself; the borrower's personal assets are generally protected. However, most non-recourse loans include "bad boy" carve-outs that restore personal liability in cases of fraud, misrepresentation, or environmental violations. Non-recourse financing typically requires lower LTVs, larger loan amounts, and stronger property fundamentals.
