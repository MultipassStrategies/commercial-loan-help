Mezzanine Loans in Commercial Real Estate: How They Work and When to Use Them

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Capital stack position: Between senior debt and equity
  • Interest rates: 10% to 18%
  • Typical size: $1M to $50M+
  • Term: 1 to 5 years
  • Collateral: Ownership interest pledge
  • Primary use: Fill the equity gap

What Is Mezzanine Debt?

Mezzanine debt is a form of subordinate financing that occupies the middle layer of the capital stack in a commercial real estate transaction. It sits below the senior mortgage (the first lien on the property) and above the borrower's equity. Because the mezzanine lender's position is junior to the senior lender, mezzanine carries more risk than a first mortgage, and that elevated risk translates directly into higher interest rates and returns for the mezzanine provider.

The defining characteristic of mezzanine financing is how it is secured. Unlike a traditional mortgage, which is secured by a lien recorded against the real property, mezzanine debt is secured by a pledge of the borrower's ownership interest in the entity that holds title to the property. In most commercial real estate transactions, the property is owned by a special-purpose entity, typically a limited liability company (LLC) or a limited partnership. The mezzanine lender takes a security interest in the borrower's membership interests or partnership interests in that entity, rather than in the real estate itself.

This structural distinction has significant practical consequences. If the borrower defaults on the mezzanine loan, the mezzanine lender does not foreclose on the property through the courts the way a mortgage lender would. Instead, the mezzanine lender forecloses on the pledged ownership interests through a Uniform Commercial Code (UCC) sale, which is a faster and more streamlined process. By acquiring the borrower's ownership stake, the mezzanine lender effectively gains control of the entity that owns the property, and with it, control of the underlying asset.

Mezzanine financing is widely used in commercial real estate because it allows borrowers to achieve higher leverage (often 80% to 85% or more of total project cost) without violating the terms of their senior loan, which typically prohibits additional liens on the property. For lenders, mezzanine provides an attractive risk-adjusted return that falls between the lower yields of senior debt and the higher (but more variable) returns of equity.

Where Mezzanine Fits in the Capital Stack

The capital stack is the total set of financing sources used to fund a commercial real estate transaction, arranged by priority of repayment and level of risk. Understanding where mezzanine debt sits in this hierarchy is essential for borrowers evaluating their financing options.

Senior Debt (First Mortgage)

At the base of the stack is the senior mortgage, typically covering 60% to 70% of the property's value or total project cost. The senior lender holds the first lien on the property and has the highest priority in repayment. Because it occupies the safest position, senior debt carries the lowest interest rate in the stack. In the event of a default and liquidation, the senior lender is paid first from the proceeds of the property sale. This priority position means the senior lender is only at risk if the property value falls below the outstanding loan balance, which is why senior lenders cap their loan-to-value (LTV) ratios at conservative levels.

Mezzanine Debt

Above the senior mortgage is the mezzanine layer, which fills the gap between what the senior lender will provide and the total amount needed to close the deal. Mezzanine typically fills the capitalization from 65% or 70% up to 80% or 85% of total cost. The mezzanine lender is repaid after the senior lender but before equity investors. This subordinate position means the mezzanine lender faces greater risk of loss: if the property is sold for less than the total debt, the mezzanine lender absorbs losses before the senior lender does. To compensate for this risk, mezzanine lenders charge significantly higher interest rates, typically ranging from 10% to 18%.

Equity

At the top of the stack is the borrower's equity, representing the remaining 15% to 20% (or more) of the total project cost. Equity investors are the last to be repaid and the first to absorb losses, which is why equity demands the highest returns in the capital stack. By using mezzanine debt to fill a portion of what would otherwise need to be equity, borrowers can reduce the amount of their own capital tied up in a single deal, freeing that capital for other investments or reserves.

Each layer of the capital stack carries a different risk and return profile. Senior debt is the safest and cheapest. Mezzanine sits in the middle: more risk than senior debt, but less risk than equity. Equity bears the most risk but captures the upside if the property performs well. The interplay among these layers determines the overall cost of capital for the project and the return profile for each participant.

Mezzanine vs. Second Mortgage

Mezzanine debt and second mortgages serve a similar economic purpose: both provide subordinate capital behind a first mortgage. However, they differ in one critical respect, which is the nature of the security interest.

A second mortgage is secured by a lien on the real property itself. The second mortgage is recorded in the public land records, just like the first mortgage, and sits in a junior lien position. If the borrower defaults, the second mortgage lender must foreclose on the property through the same judicial (or non-judicial, depending on the state) foreclosure process that applies to any mortgage. In judicial foreclosure states, this process can take six months to two years or longer, depending on the court system's backlog and the borrower's ability to contest the action.

Mezzanine debt, by contrast, is secured by a pledge of the borrower's ownership interest in the property-owning entity. It is not recorded against the property and does not create a lien on the real estate. If the borrower defaults, the mezzanine lender forecloses on the pledged ownership interests through a UCC sale, which is governed by Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code rather than state mortgage foreclosure law. A UCC sale can typically be completed in 60 to 90 days after proper notice is given, making it a significantly faster remedy than mortgage foreclosure.

This speed difference is one of the primary reasons mezzanine financing exists as a distinct product. But there is another equally important reason: most senior lenders explicitly prohibit subordinate liens on the property in their loan documents. A second mortgage would violate the senior loan agreement, potentially triggering a default on the first mortgage. Mezzanine, because it pledges ownership interests rather than creating a property lien, avoids this conflict. The senior lender and the mezzanine lender instead negotiate an intercreditor agreement that governs their respective rights and remedies, allowing both layers of debt to coexist without either violating the other's terms.

From the borrower's perspective, the practical effect is similar: both mezzanine and a second mortgage increase leverage and reduce the equity requirement. But the legal mechanics, enforcement timelines, and relationship with the senior lender are fundamentally different, and those differences shape the terms, costs, and risks of each option.

Mezzanine vs. Preferred Equity

Mezzanine debt and preferred equity are both used to fill the gap between senior debt and common equity, but they are structurally distinct instruments with different legal rights, enforcement mechanisms, and risk profiles. The following table summarizes the key differences.

Feature Mezzanine Debt Preferred Equity
Security Pledge of ownership interests Equity position in the entity
Priority Ahead of all equity (preferred and common) Behind mezzanine debt, ahead of common equity
Foreclosure mechanism UCC sale (60 to 90 days) Operating agreement remedies (dilution, removal of manager)
Tax treatment Interest payments are deductible to borrower Distributions are not deductible; taxed as partnership income
Return expectations 10% to 18% (fixed or floating interest) 12% to 20%+ (preferred return plus potential upside)
Intercreditor dynamics Separate intercreditor agreement with senior lender Governed by operating agreement; senior lender may require recognition agreement

The choice between mezzanine and preferred equity often depends on the senior lender's requirements, the borrower's tax situation, and the capital provider's risk appetite. Some senior lenders have strong preferences for one structure over the other. Preferred equity is sometimes favored in situations where the senior lender's loan documents restrict mezzanine debt or where the borrower wants to avoid the faster foreclosure remedy that comes with a UCC pledge. Conversely, mezzanine lenders prefer the pledge structure because it gives them a more direct and enforceable path to control the asset if something goes wrong.

Common Mezzanine Structures

Mezzanine loans are not one-size-fits-all. The payment and return structure can vary significantly depending on the deal type, the property's cash flow profile, and the negotiating leverage of the parties involved. The three most common structures are current pay, payment-in-kind (PIK), and participating mezzanine.

Current Pay

In a current pay structure, the borrower makes regular monthly (or quarterly) interest payments to the mezzanine lender throughout the loan term, just as it would on a senior mortgage. This is the simplest and most straightforward mezzanine structure. It works best for stabilized, income-producing properties that generate enough cash flow to service both the senior debt and the mezzanine interest. Current pay mezzanine typically carries lower headline rates than other structures because the lender receives its return in real time rather than waiting for a future event.

Payment-in-Kind (PIK)

In a PIK structure, interest is not paid in cash during the loan term. Instead, it accrues and is added to the principal balance, compounding over time. The full amount (original principal plus accrued interest) is due at maturity or upon a capital event such as a sale or refinance. PIK structures are common in transitional or development deals where the property is not yet generating sufficient cash flow to support current interest payments. The trade-off is that the effective cost of PIK mezzanine is higher due to the compounding effect, and the borrower's total obligation grows throughout the term.

Participating Mezzanine

Participating mezzanine combines a base interest rate (which may be current pay, PIK, or a blend) with a share of the property's profits or appreciation. The mezzanine lender receives its contractual interest plus a percentage of the upside, such as a share of net operating income above a threshold or a percentage of the profit upon sale. This structure aligns the mezzanine lender's interests with the borrower's success and is often used in value-add or development deals where significant appreciation is expected. In exchange for the upside participation, the base interest rate is typically lower than it would be in a pure current pay or PIK structure.

Hybrid Structures

Many mezzanine deals combine elements of these three approaches. A common hybrid is a current pay component at a modest rate combined with a PIK component that accrues, providing the borrower with partial cash flow relief while still compensating the lender for its risk. Another variation pairs a current pay base rate with a back-end participation in profits. The specific structure is negotiated based on the property's cash flow, the borrower's business plan, and the lender's return requirements.

When Mezzanine Is Used

Mezzanine financing is a tool for specific situations where the borrower needs more leverage than a senior lender will provide but wants to minimize the equity commitment. The most common use cases fall into four categories.

Acquisitions

Mezzanine is frequently used in property acquisitions to reduce the equity required at closing. If a senior lender will provide 65% of the purchase price, and the borrower wants to limit their equity to 15%, mezzanine can fill the remaining 20%. This is especially valuable for sponsors who are deploying capital across multiple deals simultaneously and want to stretch their equity across a larger portfolio rather than concentrating it in a single asset.

Development and Construction

Ground-up development and major renovation projects often require mezzanine capital because the equity requirements are larger and the risk profile discourages senior lenders from offering high leverage. Mezzanine in development deals typically carries PIK or hybrid structures because the property is not generating income during the construction phase. The mezzanine lender underwrites the completed project value and the sponsor's ability to execute the business plan.

Recapitalizations

Owners of existing properties sometimes use mezzanine to pull equity out of an asset without disturbing favorable senior financing. For example, if a property has appreciated significantly since acquisition, the owner may take a mezzanine loan against the increased value to fund distributions, new acquisitions, or capital improvements. Recapitalization mezzanine on stabilized assets tends to carry lower rates because the underlying property has a proven cash flow track record.

Refinancing

When a borrower's loan-to-value ratio is too high for a senior lender to refinance the full amount (for instance, if the property value has declined or the senior lending market has tightened), mezzanine can fill the gap. Rather than bringing additional equity to the table, the borrower uses mezzanine to bridge the difference between the new senior loan amount and the total payoff requirement. This is particularly common during periods of market stress when senior lenders reduce their maximum LTV ratios.

Typical mezzanine deal sizes range from $1 million to $50 million or more, though most active mezzanine lenders focus on the $5 million to $30 million range. Deals below $2 million can be more difficult to place because the fixed transaction costs (legal, due diligence, intercreditor negotiation) represent a larger percentage of the loan amount.

Intercreditor Agreements

The intercreditor agreement (ICA) is one of the most important and heavily negotiated documents in any transaction involving mezzanine debt. It is the contract between the senior lender and the mezzanine lender that defines how each party can exercise its rights and remedies, and it establishes the rules that govern their relationship throughout the life of the loan.

Key provisions in a typical intercreditor agreement include the following.

  • Standstill periods. The ICA typically requires the mezzanine lender to observe a standstill period before exercising remedies (such as a UCC foreclosure) after a default. During this period, the senior lender has the opportunity to work out the situation with the borrower or pursue its own remedies. Standstill periods commonly range from 60 to 180 days.
  • Cure rights. The mezzanine lender is usually granted the right to cure defaults on the senior loan. If the borrower misses a payment to the senior lender, the mezzanine lender can step in, make the payment, and add the amount to the borrower's mezzanine obligation. This protects the mezzanine lender's position by preventing the senior lender from accelerating and foreclosing on the property.
  • Purchase option. Many ICAs give the mezzanine lender the right to purchase the senior loan at par (the outstanding principal balance plus accrued interest and fees) if the senior lender accelerates the loan or commences foreclosure. This allows the mezzanine lender to take control of the entire debt stack and manage the workout directly.
  • Notification requirements. Both lenders are required to notify each other of defaults, acceleration events, and other material actions. These notification provisions ensure that neither party is caught off guard by the other's actions.

For borrowers, the intercreditor agreement matters because its terms can significantly affect your flexibility during the loan term. The ICA may restrict your ability to negotiate modifications with either lender without the other's consent. It may also impose conditions on refinancing or selling the property. Understanding the intercreditor terms before closing is critical, and borrowers should work with experienced legal counsel to review this document carefully.

Qualifying for Mezzanine Financing

Mezzanine lenders evaluate deals based on a combination of property fundamentals, sponsor quality, and the strength of the overall capital structure. The qualification criteria are less standardized than those for senior lending, but several factors are consistently important.

  • Sponsor experience. Mezzanine lenders want to see that the borrower (or sponsor) has a track record of successfully executing similar projects. A first-time developer will face more scrutiny than a seasoned operator with a portfolio of completed deals. Experience with the specific property type and market is especially valued.
  • Property quality and location. The underlying asset must be of sufficient quality to support the total leverage. Mezzanine lenders analyze the property's current and projected net operating income, its physical condition, its competitive position in the market, and the strength of the local real estate market. Properties in primary markets with strong demand fundamentals are easier to finance than those in secondary or tertiary markets.
  • Senior lender cooperation. Because the intercreditor agreement must be negotiated and executed before closing, the senior lender's willingness to work with a mezzanine lender is essential. Some senior lenders have established mezzanine programs and standardized ICA templates, making the process smoother. Others may resist subordinate capital altogether.
  • Clear business plan. The borrower must present a well-defined business plan that explains how the property will generate sufficient returns to service all layers of debt and provide a return to equity. For stabilized assets, this means demonstrating stable or growing cash flow. For transitional or development deals, it means presenting a credible execution plan with realistic timelines and budgets.
  • Exit strategy. Mezzanine lenders need confidence that the borrower has a viable path to repay the mezzanine loan at maturity. Common exit strategies include refinancing into a single senior loan at a lower LTV (after the property has stabilized or appreciated), selling the property, or recapitalizing the capital stack with permanent financing. A vague or speculative exit strategy will make it difficult to secure mezzanine capital.

Minimum deal sizes vary by lender, but most institutional mezzanine lenders prefer loans of $5 million or more. Smaller mezzanine loans (in the $1 million to $5 million range) are available from private lenders and specialized funds, though the terms may be less favorable due to the proportionally higher transaction costs.

Rates, Fees, and Returns

The cost of mezzanine financing reflects its elevated risk position in the capital stack. Borrowers should expect to pay significantly more than they would for senior debt, but less than the return an equity investor would require.

  • Interest rates: 10% to 18%, depending on the property type, risk profile, structure, and market conditions. Stabilized properties with strong cash flow command the lower end of this range. Transitional, value-add, and development deals fall toward the higher end.
  • Origination fees: Typically 1% to 2% of the mezzanine loan amount, paid at closing.
  • Exit fees: Some mezzanine lenders charge an exit fee (often 0.5% to 1%) upon repayment. This is more common in shorter-term or higher-leverage deals.
  • Legal and due diligence costs: The borrower typically pays the mezzanine lender's legal fees and due diligence costs, which can range from $25,000 to $75,000 or more depending on the complexity of the deal.

The total cost of mezzanine depends heavily on the payment structure. A current pay loan at 12% costs less in total dollars than a PIK loan at 12% because PIK interest compounds. A participating structure may have a lower base rate but a higher total return if the property performs well. When evaluating mezzanine offers, borrowers should model the true all-in cost under realistic scenarios, including the impact of compounding on PIK interest and the potential payout on participation features.

It is also important to compare the cost of mezzanine to the cost of equity. If a borrower's equity investors expect a 20% to 25% internal rate of return, then mezzanine at 14% is a cheaper source of capital, even though it is more expensive than senior debt. The decision to use mezzanine versus additional equity is ultimately a cost-of-capital calculation, and the right answer depends on the borrower's return targets, risk tolerance, and capital availability.

Risks of Mezzanine Debt

While mezzanine financing is a powerful tool for increasing leverage and reducing equity requirements, it introduces several risks that borrowers must evaluate carefully.

  • Higher cost of capital. Mezzanine rates of 10% to 18% significantly increase the blended cost of the total debt stack. If the property's returns do not exceed this cost, the borrower's equity returns will be diluted rather than enhanced. Leverage amplifies returns in both directions: it magnifies gains when the property performs well, and it magnifies losses when it does not.
  • Complexity. Adding a mezzanine layer to the capital stack introduces a second lender relationship, an intercreditor agreement, and additional legal and administrative complexity. The closing process is longer and more expensive, and ongoing compliance obligations multiply.
  • Intercreditor restrictions. The intercreditor agreement may limit the borrower's flexibility in ways that are not immediately obvious. Restrictions on loan modifications, property sales, additional financing, and management changes can constrain the borrower's ability to adapt to changing market conditions.
  • Personal recourse. While mezzanine loans are often structured as non-recourse to the borrower personally, many include carve-outs for "bad boy" acts (fraud, misrepresentation, environmental violations, voluntary bankruptcy) that can trigger full personal recourse. Some mezzanine lenders also require limited personal guarantees, particularly for smaller deals or less experienced sponsors.
  • Subordination risk. In a liquidation scenario, the mezzanine lender is repaid only after the senior lender is made whole. If the property is sold for less than the total debt, the mezzanine lender absorbs the loss first. For borrowers, this means the mezzanine lender may become adversarial in a distress situation, pushing for aggressive remedies to protect its position.
  • Loss of control through UCC foreclosure. If a borrower defaults on mezzanine debt and the lender exercises its UCC foreclosure rights, the borrower loses its ownership interest in the entity that owns the property. Unlike a traditional mortgage foreclosure, which can take a year or more, a UCC sale can be completed in as little as 60 to 90 days. This speed, while advantageous for the lender, means the borrower has limited time and leverage to negotiate an alternative resolution.

Capital Stack Example

To illustrate how mezzanine financing works in practice, consider a straightforward acquisition of a $10 million commercial property. The borrower structures the deal with three layers of capital.

Senior Debt

The borrower obtains a first mortgage of $6.5 million, representing 65% of the purchase price. The senior lender charges an interest rate of 6.5% on a 30-year amortization with a 10-year term. The annual debt service on the senior mortgage is approximately $493,000.

Mezzanine Debt

To reduce the equity requirement, the borrower secures a mezzanine loan of $2 million, filling the capital stack from 65% to 85% of total cost. The mezzanine lender charges 14% interest on a current pay basis with a 3-year term (interest only, with principal due at maturity). The annual mezzanine interest payment is $280,000.

Borrower Equity

The borrower contributes $1.5 million in equity, representing 15% of the purchase price. The borrower's target return on equity is 20% or higher.

Blended Cost of Capital

The weighted average cost of capital (WACC) for this deal can be calculated as follows. Senior debt ($6.5M at 6.5%) contributes a weighted cost of 4.23%. Mezzanine ($2M at 14%) contributes 2.80%. If we assign an equity cost of 20% to the borrower's $1.5M, equity contributes 3.00%. The blended WACC is approximately 10.03%. This means the property needs to generate an unlevered return above 10% to create positive value for the equity investor. Because the senior debt is significantly cheaper than the equity, the mezzanine layer (at 14%) is still accretive to the borrower's equity returns as long as the property's cash-on-cash yield exceeds the blended debt cost.

In this example, without mezzanine, the borrower would need $3.5 million in equity (35% of the purchase price) instead of $1.5 million. The mezzanine allows the borrower to retain $2 million for other uses while only modestly increasing the overall cost of debt. If the property generates a 7.5% capitalization rate ($750,000 in net operating income), the equity investor's cash-on-cash return with mezzanine is approximately negative $23,000 divided by $1.5 million before considering principal paydown and appreciation. However, if the cap rate is 8.5% ($850,000 NOI), the annual cash flow after debt service is $77,000 on $1.5 million of equity, and the total return including appreciation and principal paydown is substantially higher. The point is that mezzanine amplifies the effect of property performance on equity returns, for better or worse.
