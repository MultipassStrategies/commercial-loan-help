Manufactured Housing Community Loans: MHC and Mobile Home Park Financing

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Typical loan amount: $1M to $50M+
  • Max LTV: 70% to 80%
  • Interest rates: 5.5% to 7.5% (agency); 6.5% to 9% (non-agency)
  • Term length: 5 to 30 years
  • Agency programs: Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac MHC programs available
  • DSCR requirement: 1.20x to 1.35x

Overview of MHC Financing

Manufactured housing communities (MHCs), also known as mobile home parks, have become one of the most actively sought commercial real estate asset classes in the United States. These communities operate on a land-lease model: the operator owns the land, infrastructure, roads, and common areas, while residents own or rent the manufactured homes that sit on individual pads. The operator collects monthly lot rent from each pad, creating a recurring income stream that is remarkably stable compared to other property types.

Investor interest in MHCs has surged for several reasons. Manufactured housing is the largest source of unsubsidized affordable housing in the country, with median lot rents of $300 to $700 per month in most markets. This affordability creates consistent demand regardless of economic cycles. Local zoning ordinances make it extremely difficult to develop new communities, so existing parks face little competitive supply. And the cost to move a manufactured home ($5,000 to $10,000 or more) creates natural tenant retention, resulting in lower turnover and more predictable occupancy than apartments.

From a financing perspective, MHCs are treated as a specialized subset of multifamily lending. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac both have dedicated MHC programs, and conventional lenders, CMBS conduits, and government programs such as USDA 538 also serve this market. Loan sizes range from under $1 million for small rural parks to $50 million or more for large communities or portfolio transactions.

Fannie Mae Manufactured Housing Programs

Fannie Mae offers dedicated financing for manufactured housing communities through its DUS (Delegated Underwriting and Servicing) program. DUS lenders underwrite and close MHC loans on Fannie Mae's behalf, following standardized guidelines that create predictable terms and pricing.

Key features include non-recourse financing (with standard bad-boy carve-out guarantees), fixed and floating rate options, terms from 5 to 30 years, and amortization up to 30 years. Maximum leverage is typically 75% to 80% LTV, depending on occupancy, condition, and market strength. The minimum DSCR is generally 1.25x, though Fannie Mae may require higher coverage for communities with risk factors such as a high percentage of park-owned homes.

Fannie Mae's Green Rewards program is available for MHC operators who commit to energy and water efficiency improvements. Borrowers who complete qualifying upgrades (low-flow fixtures, LED lighting, energy-efficient HVAC for community buildings) can receive a modest interest rate reduction and additional loan proceeds to fund the improvements.

The ideal Fannie Mae MHC candidate has at least 50 pads, physical occupancy of 85% or higher, a predominance of tenant-owned homes, well-maintained infrastructure, and a location in a market with stable housing demand. Fannie Mae also considers communities with fewer than 50 pads on a case-by-case basis, but underwriting is more selective and the borrower's experience becomes a more significant factor.

Freddie Mac Manufactured Housing Programs

Freddie Mac finances manufactured housing communities through its Optigo lending platform, which includes both standard and small balance loan programs. Like Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac treats MHCs as a subset of multifamily and applies specialized underwriting guidelines that account for the unique characteristics of land-lease communities.

The standard Freddie Mac MHC loan offers non-recourse financing with fixed or floating rates, terms from 5 to 20 years, and amortization up to 30 years. Maximum LTV is generally 75% to 80%, and the minimum DSCR is 1.25x. Freddie Mac's underwriting considerations are similar to Fannie Mae's: pad count, occupancy, tenant-owned vs. park-owned home ratio, infrastructure condition, and market fundamentals all factor into the credit decision.

Where Freddie Mac stands out is its Small Balance Loan (SBL) program, which targets loans between $1 million and $7.5 million. The SBL program is particularly well-suited for smaller manufactured housing communities that may not fit the standard Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac execution. The SBL program features streamlined underwriting with faster processing timelines, competitive rate pricing that does not penalize smaller loan sizes, and more flexible community size requirements. For operators of 25 to 75 pad communities seeking agency-quality terms on a smaller loan, the Freddie Mac SBL program is often the best available option.

Freddie Mac also maintains a focus on affordable housing preservation and recognizes MHCs as a natural fit for this mission. Communities that serve residents at or below area median income levels may qualify for additional pricing benefits. The agency has also been receptive to financing communities where the operator is implementing a home ownership program, selling park-owned units to residents as a way to stabilize the community and improve the operating profile over time.

Conventional Bank Lending

Community banks, regional banks, and credit unions remain an important source of financing for manufactured housing communities, especially for smaller parks and operators who have established banking relationships. Bank loans for MHCs are portfolio products held on the lender's balance sheet, which gives banks flexibility to structure terms that agency and CMBS lenders cannot match.

Bank MHC loans are typically recourse, meaning the borrower personally guarantees the debt. Terms are shorter, usually 5 to 10 years with a balloon payment, and amortization is typically 20 to 25 years. Interest rates are generally higher than agency execution, ranging from 6.5% to 9% depending on the bank's cost of funds, the property's risk profile, and the borrower's relationship. Maximum LTV is usually 65% to 75%, and DSCR requirements range from 1.20x to 1.35x.

The advantages of bank lending for MHCs include speed and flexibility. A local bank familiar with the market can close a loan in 30 to 60 days, compared to 60 to 90 days or longer for agency execution. Banks can also finance properties that do not meet agency guidelines, such as communities with fewer than 25 pads, those with occupancy below 80%, parks with a high ratio of park-owned homes, or communities with deferred infrastructure maintenance. For operators who are acquiring a value-add park and plan to stabilize it before refinancing into permanent agency debt, a bank loan can serve as an effective short-term solution.

The primary disadvantage is recourse. Bank borrowers put their personal assets at risk, which limits the ability to scale a portfolio. Additionally, shorter loan terms create refinancing risk at maturity, and higher rates reduce cash-on-cash returns compared to agency alternatives. For these reasons, bank financing is best suited for smaller deals where agency execution is not practical, for transitional properties that will be refinanced later, or for operators who value the speed and relationship-driven flexibility that banks provide.

CMBS for Larger Communities

CMBS (Commercial Mortgage-Backed Securities) conduit lending is available for stabilized manufactured housing communities with loan amounts of $5 million or more. The originating lender underwrites and closes the loan, then pools it with other commercial mortgages into a bond sold to capital markets investors, providing non-recourse, fixed-rate financing with standardized terms.

Typical CMBS MHC loan terms include fixed rates for 5 or 10 years, amortization of 25 to 30 years, LTV of 65% to 75%, and minimum DSCR of 1.25x to 1.30x. Rates generally carry a modest spread premium above standard multifamily. As of early 2026, all-in fixed rates for well-qualified MHC borrowers land in the 6.5% to 8% range depending on leverage, property quality, and market conditions.

CMBS can be a good fit for operators who want non-recourse terms but do not qualify for agency financing, perhaps because the community has a mixed-use component, unusual infrastructure, or a tenant-owned home ratio below agency thresholds. The trade-offs include inflexible loan documents, defeasance or yield maintenance prepayment provisions, and the potential for special servicing complications. Borrowers who plan to hold a stabilized community for the full loan term will find CMBS to be a reliable execution.

Key Underwriting Metrics for MHC Loans

Lenders evaluating manufactured housing communities focus on a specific set of metrics that differ from conventional multifamily underwriting. Understanding these metrics is essential for borrowers preparing a loan application.

Lot Rent Analysis

Lot rent is the monthly fee residents pay for the use of the pad and community infrastructure. Lenders analyze current lot rents relative to the local market to determine whether rents are at market, below market, or above market. Below-market lot rents represent upside potential (which value-add investors seek), but they also introduce execution risk because the operator must actually implement increases over time. Lenders will underwrite to the lower of actual in-place rents or a conservatively projected rent level, not to aspirational future rents. A detailed rent comparable analysis showing what similar communities in the market charge is a standard requirement in any MHC loan application.

Pad Occupancy

Occupancy is measured by the number of occupied pads relative to total available pads. Agency lenders typically require 80% to 85% minimum occupancy, while CMBS lenders may accept 75% or higher for strong properties. Lenders distinguish between physical occupancy (pads with homes on them) and economic occupancy (pads that are actually producing rent). A community may have 95% physical occupancy but only 88% economic occupancy if some residents are delinquent or if park-owned homes are vacant. Economic occupancy is the more conservative metric and the one lenders rely on for underwriting purposes.

Tenant-Owned vs. Park-Owned Homes

As noted earlier, the ratio of tenant-owned to park-owned homes is critical. Tenant-owned communities generate higher-margin income because the operator has no responsibility for home maintenance, replacement, or vacancy in individual units. Park-owned homes require the operator to fund maintenance, handle turnover, and bear the risk of extended vacancy. Lenders typically assign a lower expense ratio to communities with a high tenant-owned percentage and a higher expense ratio to communities with significant park-owned inventory. Most agency lenders prefer at least 70% tenant-owned homes.

Infrastructure Condition

The condition of roads, water systems, sewer lines, electrical distribution, and stormwater drainage is a major underwriting factor. Deferred infrastructure maintenance can result in large capital expenditure requirements that reduce the property's net cash flow. Lenders require a property condition assessment (PCA) from a licensed engineering firm, and the estimated cost of near-term repairs and replacements will be reflected in the loan sizing through required reserves or escrows. Communities with private well water, septic systems, or aging underground utilities face additional scrutiny and may receive less favorable terms than communities connected to municipal water and sewer systems.

Utility Billing Structure

Whether utilities are billed directly to residents or included in lot rent (master-metered) significantly affects the property's operating expense profile. In a direct-billed community, residents pay their own water, sewer, electric, and gas bills, and the operator's utility expense is limited to common area usage. In a master-metered community, the operator pays the entire utility bill and may or may not pass some of that cost through via a RUBS (Ratio Utility Billing System) charge. Lenders prefer direct-billed or sub-metered communities because the operator's expense exposure is lower and more predictable. Master-metered communities carry higher operating expenses and are penalized in underwriting through lower NOI and reduced loan proceeds.

Value-Add Opportunities in MHC Investing

Manufactured housing communities offer several value-add strategies that investors use to increase cash flow and property value. Lenders are familiar with these strategies and will evaluate the borrower's ability to execute them when underwriting transitional or value-add loans.

Utility Billing Conversion

Converting from master-metered to sub-metered utility billing is one of the most common and impactful value-add strategies in MHC investing. By installing sub-meters on individual pads and billing residents for their actual utility consumption, the operator shifts the cost burden from the property's operating expenses to the residents. This can reduce operating expenses by $50 to $150 per pad per month or more, depending on the market and utility rates. The resulting NOI increase flows directly to property value through the income capitalization approach. Sub-metering installation typically costs $500 to $1,500 per pad, making it a high-return capital improvement. Local regulations govern how and when utility billing can be changed, so operators must verify compliance with state and municipal rules before implementing this strategy.

Infill of Vacant Pads

Many older communities have vacant pads ready for homes to be placed. The cost to purchase and place a new manufactured home ranges from $40,000 to $90,000 depending on size and market. Operators can sell the home to a resident, rent it as a park-owned unit, or offer a rent-to-own arrangement. Infill is a proven way to increase occupancy and revenue, but each home placement requires site preparation, delivery, utility connections, and resident placement, typically taking 60 to 120 days per home.

Lot Rent Increases to Market

Communities acquired at below-market lot rents present an opportunity to increase revenue by raising rents to market levels over time. Because the cost to relocate a manufactured home is high, residents are generally less sensitive to moderate rent increases than apartment tenants, who can move at the end of a lease with minimal friction. However, aggressive or sudden rent increases can create regulatory and reputational risk, particularly in states with tenant protection laws or rent control provisions. Most experienced operators implement gradual, annual rent increases of $20 to $50 per month over several years, allowing the community's income to grow steadily toward market rates without triggering resident backlash or regulatory intervention.

Home Sales Program

Operators of communities with a high percentage of park-owned homes can implement a home sales program, converting park-owned units to tenant-owned over time. This strategy reduces the operator's maintenance burden, lowers operating expenses, and improves the community's financing profile. Homes can be sold outright for cash, financed through third-party chattel lenders, or offered on a rent-to-own basis. As the tenant-owned percentage increases, the community becomes more attractive to agency lenders, potentially qualifying for better rates and higher leverage on a future refinance.

Amenity Upgrades

Adding or improving community amenities (playgrounds, dog parks, community buildings, laundry facilities, paved roads, landscaping) can justify higher lot rents and improve resident satisfaction and retention. While amenity upgrades alone rarely drive significant value increases, they support lot rent increases and reduce turnover when combined with other value-add strategies. Lenders view amenity improvements favorably as part of a broader repositioning plan.

Regulatory Landscape

Manufactured housing communities operate within state and local regulations that vary significantly by jurisdiction. Understanding the regulatory environment is essential, because these rules directly affect operating flexibility, expense structures, and property value.

Zoning restrictions are among the most significant factors. Most municipalities restrict where MHCs can be located, and many prohibit new community development altogether. This supply constraint benefits existing operators by limiting competition, but it also means expansion (adding pads on adjacent land) may require a zoning variance or special use permit that is difficult to obtain.

Several states and municipalities have enacted rent control or rent stabilization laws for manufactured housing communities. These regulations typically cap annual lot rent increases, often tying permissible increases to the Consumer Price Index. Rent control limits the operator's ability to raise rents to market levels and can reduce the value of below-market communities. States with some form of MHC rent regulation include California, New York, Oregon, and several others. Lenders underwriting in rent-controlled jurisdictions apply more conservative rent growth assumptions and may reduce loan proceeds accordingly.

Health and safety codes govern water quality, sewage disposal, electrical systems, fire safety, and road maintenance. Failure to comply can result in fines, mandatory repairs, or orders to cease operations. Communities with outstanding code violations may face difficulty obtaining financing until violations are resolved.

Tenant Protections and Impact on Operations

A growing number of states have enacted tenant protection laws specific to manufactured housing communities. These protections reflect the unique position of MHC residents, who own their homes but lease the land, making relocation costly and disruptive.

Notice requirements are common. Many states require 60 to 180 days' advance written notice before lot rent increases, rule changes, or tenancy terminations. Some jurisdictions require that notices include a justification for the increase. These requirements do not prevent rent increases, but they slow the implementation timeline and must be factored into operational planning.

Relocation assistance laws exist in some states. When a community is being closed or redeveloped, operators may be required to pay residents a fixed amount (ranging from a few thousand dollars to $10,000 or more per household) to cover relocation costs. These expenses can be substantial for larger communities.

Right of first refusal (ROFR) laws in several states give residents or their associations the right to purchase the community if the owner decides to sell. The owner must notify residents and provide a specified period (typically 30 to 90 days) to match the purchase offer. While ROFR provisions rarely prevent a sale from closing, they can delay the transaction timeline. Lenders familiar with MHC transactions in ROFR states account for this in their due diligence and closing expectations.

USDA 538 for Rural Communities

The USDA Section 538 Guaranteed Rural Rental Housing Program provides government-guaranteed financing for rental housing in rural areas, and manufactured housing communities in qualifying locations are eligible. The USDA 538 program can be an excellent financing option for MHC operators with communities in rural markets that may not attract conventional or agency lenders.

Under the 538 program, the USDA guarantees up to 90% of a loan made by an approved private lender, enabling more favorable terms. Key features include loan terms up to 40 years, LTV up to 90%, competitive fixed interest rates (typically lower than conventional bank rates), and full amortization over the loan term.

Eligibility requires location in a USDA-designated rural area (generally populations of 35,000 or less), a commitment to affordable housing (rent restrictions may apply), and compliance with USDA environmental and construction standards. The program covers both new construction and acquisition or rehabilitation. Processing timelines run 4 to 8 months, longer than conventional lending. For operators of rural MHCs who can meet these requirements, USDA 538 provides longer terms, higher leverage, and lower rates than most private-sector alternatives.

Frequently Asked Questions

The terms are frequently used interchangeably in casual conversation, but there are meaningful distinctions that matter to lenders and investors. A manufactured housing community (MHC) refers to a land-lease community where the operator owns the land, infrastructure, and common areas, and residents either own or rent manufactured homes placed on individual pads. The homes in an MHC are built to the federal HUD code, which was established in 1976 and sets construction, design, and safety standards for factory-built housing.

A "mobile home park" is the older term, originally referring to communities of pre-1976 homes that were built before the HUD code took effect. These older homes were constructed under less rigorous standards and are generally viewed as lower quality. In practice, most lenders and investors now use "manufactured housing community" as the industry-standard term regardless of the age of the homes on site, because it reflects the modern, professionalized nature of the asset class.

When seeking financing, the distinction matters most in terms of home quality and age. Communities with newer HUD-code manufactured homes in good condition are viewed more favorably by agency and CMBS lenders. Parks dominated by older pre-HUD units may face more limited financing options, and lenders will apply more conservative underwriting to account for the higher likelihood of home replacement needs and the potential for deferred maintenance issues.

Agency lenders evaluate MHC properties against several core criteria. The community should have at least 50 pads (though some programs consider smaller communities on a case-by-case basis). Physical occupancy should be 80% to 85% or higher, with stable or improving trends over the trailing 12 months. Infrastructure including roads, water, sewer, and electrical distribution must be in acceptable condition without major deferred maintenance issues. The borrower should demonstrate a track record of managing manufactured housing communities or, at minimum, other multifamily properties.

Both Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac prefer communities where the majority of homes are tenant-owned rather than park-owned, because tenant-owned homes create a more stable resident base with lower turnover and reduced operating expenses for the community operator. The property must be located in a market with demonstrated demand for affordable housing, and the community's lot rents should be competitive with local alternatives.

Standard third-party reports are required, including an MAI appraisal, a property condition assessment from a licensed engineering firm, a Phase I environmental site assessment, and a survey. Borrowers typically need a net worth equal to or greater than the loan amount and liquid assets of at least 10% of the loan balance. The borrowing entity must be a single-purpose entity, and agency lenders will also review the operator's management track record, rent collection history, and maintenance practices as part of the underwriting process.

The tenant-owned versus park-owned home ratio is one of the most important underwriting factors in MHC financing. When residents own their homes and lease only the land beneath them, the community operator collects lot rent with minimal responsibility for the home itself. This arrangement creates a lower operating expense ratio, more predictable cash flow, and reduced capital expenditure requirements. Agency lenders strongly prefer communities where 70% or more of the homes are tenant-owned.

A high percentage of park-owned homes increases the operator's cost structure significantly. The operator must maintain and repair the homes, handle turnover (cleaning, repairing, and re-leasing units when residents leave), and absorb vacancy risk when a home sits empty. These expenses reduce NOI and make the property riskier from a lender's perspective. Communities with substantial park-owned home inventory may still be financeable, but lenders will apply more conservative underwriting, requiring higher DSCR thresholds (1.30x to 1.35x instead of 1.25x) and offering lower LTV ratios.

Some operators use a strategy of acquiring communities with a significant number of park-owned homes and then selling those homes to residents over time. This conversion from park-owned to tenant-owned improves the operating profile, increases NOI, and positions the community for a more favorable refinance at better terms and lower rates. Lenders familiar with MHC operations understand this strategy and may provide bridge or transitional financing to support the conversion process.

Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac generally prefer manufactured housing communities with at least 50 pads. This threshold is not an absolute cutoff, but communities below 50 pads face more scrutiny and may not qualify for standard agency terms or pricing. The reasoning is similar to multifamily lending, where larger properties with more units provide more diversified income streams. A 100-pad community with one vacant pad loses 1% of its income; a 20-pad community with one vacant pad loses 5%. The larger community is inherently less volatile.

For communities with 25 to 49 pads, Freddie Mac's Small Balance Loan program (targeting loans between $1 million and $7.5 million) provides more flexibility on community size. The SBL program's underwriting is more accommodating for smaller properties, and the streamlined process keeps closing costs reasonable relative to the loan size. Some Fannie Mae DUS lenders will also consider smaller communities if the property is well-maintained, the market is strong, and the borrower has significant MHC experience.

Communities under 25 pads are generally too small for any agency program and are better served by local bank or credit union financing. At that scale, the fixed costs of agency loan origination (appraisal, environmental, legal, third-party reports) become disproportionate to the loan size, and the economics favor a simpler, relationship-driven bank execution where closing costs are lower and the underwriting process is more flexible.

If the community is currently stabilized (at least 80% occupied with market-level lot rents) but has upside through utility billing conversion, modest rent increases, or amenity improvements, agency lenders may finance the acquisition based on current income. You then capture the value-add upside over time through operational improvements, and the increased NOI positions you for a favorable refinance or supplemental loan down the road.

For more significant value-add strategies, where the community has substantial vacancy, deeply below-market rents, significant deferred maintenance, or a large inventory of park-owned homes that need to be sold or replaced, you will likely need a bridge loan to acquire and stabilize the property before refinancing into permanent agency or CMBS debt. Bridge lenders specializing in manufactured housing underwrite to the projected stabilized value rather than current income, fund capital improvements through a draw schedule, and typically offer 1 to 3 year terms with extension options.

The key to obtaining value-add financing is presenting a realistic, well-documented business plan. Lenders will evaluate your infill timeline (how quickly you can place homes on vacant pads), the market support for your target lot rents, your experience executing similar strategies on previous communities, and the total capital required to complete the business plan. Operators with a strong track record of successfully stabilizing MHCs will have access to more favorable bridge terms, including higher leverage and lower rates, than first-time operators attempting the same strategy.
