Hotel and Hospitality Loans: Financing for Hotels, Motels, and Resorts

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Typical loan amount: $1M to $100M+
  • Max LTV: 55% to 70%
  • Interest rates: 6.0% to 10.0% (varies by program and flag)
  • Term length: 5 to 25 years
  • DSCR requirement: 1.25x to 1.50x
  • Loan types: SBA, CMBS, bank, bridge, construction

Overview of Hotel Financing

Hotel and hospitality financing is fundamentally different from lending on other commercial real estate asset classes. While an office building or apartment complex generates predictable income through multi-year leases, a hotel operates as a combination of real estate and an active business. Every guest room is effectively re-leased on a nightly basis, and revenue is driven by a complex mix of occupancy rates, room pricing, seasonality, local demand generators, and the operator's ability to manage costs. This dual nature (real estate asset plus operating business) makes hospitality one of the most challenging property types for lenders to underwrite and for borrowers to finance.

Revenue volatility is the central concern. A hotel's income can swing dramatically in response to economic downturns, seasonal patterns, new competitive supply, changes in local demand (such as the loss of a major employer or convention center), or disruptions like natural disasters and public health events. The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated this vulnerability in extreme form, with many hotels seeing occupancy drop to single digits almost overnight. Even in normal economic cycles, a hotel's net operating income can fluctuate 15% to 25% year over year, compared to 5% to 10% for a well-leased office or industrial property. Lenders account for this volatility by requiring lower leverage, higher debt service coverage ratios, and larger cash reserves than they would for more stable property types.

Despite these challenges, hotel lending is a large and active market. Hospitality properties generate strong cash-on-cash returns when operated well, and the sector offers significant upside through renovations, rebranding, and operational improvements. Multiple loan programs exist for every segment of the hospitality market, from a 40-room independent motel financed through an SBA loan to a 500-room full-service resort funded by a CMBS securitization. Understanding which loan product fits your property, your experience level, and your business plan is the first step toward securing the right financing.

Flagged vs. Independent Hotel Lending

One of the most significant factors in hotel financing is whether the property operates under a national franchise brand (a "flag") or as an independent hotel. The distinction between flagged and independent properties directly affects which lenders will consider the deal, the leverage available, the interest rate, and the overall ease of the financing process.

Flagged hotels operate under brands like Marriott, Hilton, IHG (Holiday Inn, Crowne Plaza), Hyatt, Wyndham, Choice Hotels, and Best Western. These franchise affiliations provide the property with access to centralized reservation systems, loyalty programs with millions of enrolled members, national marketing campaigns, revenue management tools, and standardized operating procedures. From a lender's perspective, these brand resources translate into more predictable revenue and occupancy, which reduces risk. A flagged select-service hotel (such as a Hampton Inn or Fairfield Inn) benefits from brand recognition that drives consistent demand regardless of the individual owner's marketing efforts.

Independent hotels lack these built-in demand drivers. They must rely on their own reputation, location, online travel agency (OTA) visibility, direct marketing, and unique positioning to attract guests. While boutique and lifestyle independents can achieve premium pricing in certain markets, lenders view them as inherently riskier because their revenue depends more heavily on the operator's skill and the property's individual reputation. If management quality declines or local market conditions shift, an independent hotel's occupancy can erode more quickly than a flagged property that still benefits from brand loyalty and reservation system bookings.

The practical impact on financing is substantial. A flagged select-service hotel from a top-tier brand might qualify for 65% to 70% LTV from a conventional lender, with DSCR requirements of 1.25x to 1.35x and interest rates at the lower end of the hospitality range. An independent hotel of similar size and quality in the same market might be limited to 55% to 60% LTV, face DSCR requirements of 1.40x to 1.50x, and pay 75 to 150 basis points more in interest rate spread. CMBS lenders, in particular, strongly prefer flagged properties and may decline to quote independent hotels altogether unless the property has an exceptionally strong track record and market position.

SBA Hotel Loans

The Small Business Administration's lending programs are among the most accessible financing options for smaller hotel and motel acquisitions, particularly for owner-operators entering the hospitality industry or expanding a small portfolio. The two primary programs used for hotel financing are the SBA 7(a) loan and the SBA 504 loan.

SBA 7(a) for Hotels

The SBA 7(a) program provides loans up to $5 million for hotel acquisition, renovation, or refinancing. Terms can extend up to 25 years for real estate, keeping monthly payments manageable. Interest rates are typically variable, tied to the prime rate plus a margin of 1.50% to 2.75%, though some lenders offer fixed-rate options for shorter terms. The SBA guarantee (75% for loans over $150,000 and 85% for smaller loans) significantly reduces the participating lender's risk, which is why SBA 7(a) loans can offer higher leverage (85% to 90% LTV) and longer terms than conventional hotel financing.

To qualify, the borrower must be an owner-operator who will actively manage the hotel. Passive investors and absentee owners do not qualify. The SBA also requires that the borrower inject a minimum equity contribution (typically 10% to 15% of the purchase price), demonstrate relevant industry experience or a plan to hire experienced management, and meet the SBA's size standards for the accommodation sector. SBA 7(a) loans work best for properties priced under $5 million, such as limited-service motels, small boutique inns, and economy-tier flagged hotels in secondary and tertiary markets.

SBA 504 for Hotels

The SBA 504 program is structured differently, involving a conventional bank loan (covering approximately 50% of the project cost), a CDC (Certified Development Company) loan funded by an SBA-backed debenture (covering up to 40%), and the borrower's equity injection (typically 10% to 15%). The CDC portion offers a below-market fixed rate locked for either 10 or 20 years, providing long-term rate certainty on a significant portion of the financing. The maximum CDC loan is $5.5 million for standard projects.

SBA 504 loans are particularly attractive for hotel acquisitions where the borrower wants to lock in a low fixed rate on the real estate component while accepting a variable rate on the bank's first-mortgage portion. Like the 7(a) program, the 504 requires owner-occupancy and active management involvement. The 504 program tends to be favored for larger hotel purchases in the $3 million to $10 million range, where the combination of the bank loan and CDC debenture can cover the full acquisition cost with relatively low equity requirements.

CMBS Financing for Hospitality

CMBS (Commercial Mortgage-Backed Securities) loans are a primary financing source for stabilized, flagged hotel properties in the $5 million to $100 million+ range. CMBS lenders pool individual commercial mortgages into bonds sold to institutional investors, which creates a deep and liquid capital source that is not constrained by any single bank's balance sheet or appetite for hotel exposure.

For hotel borrowers, CMBS offers fixed-rate, non-recourse financing with terms of 5 to 10 years (10-year terms being the most common). The non-recourse structure is particularly appealing because it limits the borrower's personal exposure to "bad boy" carve-out guarantees rather than a full personal guarantee. However, CMBS underwriting for hotels is significantly more conservative than for other property types. Typical requirements include a maximum LTV of 55% to 65% (compared to 75% for office or industrial), a minimum DSCR of 1.35x to 1.50x (compared to 1.25x for multifamily), and larger reserve requirements for furniture, fixtures, and equipment (FF&E) replacement, property improvement plans, and seasonal cash flow shortfalls.

CMBS lenders strongly prefer flagged properties with at least two to three years of stabilized operating history. The STR (Smith Travel Research) report is a critical underwriting document, as it allows the lender to benchmark the property's RevPAR, ADR, and occupancy against its competitive set. Properties that consistently outperform their comp set are viewed favorably, while underperformers face tighter terms or outright declines. CMBS is generally not suitable for independent hotels, newly opened properties that have not yet stabilized, or hotels undergoing significant renovation or repositioning. For those situations, bridge financing or bank loans are more appropriate.

Construction Loans for New Hotel Builds

Ground-up hotel construction is one of the more complex commercial real estate financing scenarios. Construction loans for hotels typically provide 60% to 75% of total project cost (land, hard costs, soft costs, and pre-opening expenses), with the borrower contributing the remaining equity. Interest rates during the construction phase are floating, usually priced at SOFR plus 300 to 500 basis points, and the borrower pays interest only on the outstanding drawn balance as funds are disbursed.

The construction period for a hotel typically runs 18 to 30 months, depending on the size and complexity of the project. Lenders require a detailed construction budget, a fixed-price or guaranteed maximum price (GMP) general contractor agreement, a project timeline with milestone benchmarks, and evidence of all required permits and entitlements. For flagged hotel construction, the lender will also require an executed franchise agreement, the franchisor's approval of the project plans and specifications, and confirmation that the project meets all brand standards for the chosen flag.

One of the unique aspects of hotel construction financing is the pre-opening budget. Unlike an apartment building that can begin leasing units immediately upon completion, a hotel requires several months of preparation before opening day: recruiting and training staff, procuring furniture, fixtures, and equipment (FF&E), installing technology systems (property management, point of sale, key card), marketing the opening, and building initial inventory of linens, amenities, and supplies. Lenders expect the pre-opening budget to be funded as part of the construction loan, and they scrutinize this budget carefully because cost overruns during the pre-opening phase can deplete the borrower's reserves before the hotel generates any revenue.

After construction is complete, the hotel enters a ramp-up period (typically 12 to 24 months) during which occupancy and revenue gradually build toward stabilized levels. Construction loan terms must be long enough to accommodate this ramp-up, and the conversion to permanent financing (whether through a pre-arranged takeout loan or a single-close construction-to-permanent structure) is contingent on the hotel achieving specified performance targets. Borrowers with hotel development experience, strong franchise relationships, and well-capitalized balance sheets are best positioned to secure construction financing at favorable terms.

RevPAR and ADR: How Lenders Underwrite Hotel Revenue

Hotel underwriting relies on a set of industry-specific performance metrics that do not apply to other commercial property types. The three most important are Average Daily Rate (ADR), occupancy rate, and Revenue Per Available Room (RevPAR).

Average Daily Rate (ADR) measures the average rental income per paid occupied room per day. It is calculated by dividing total room revenue by the number of rooms sold. ADR reflects the hotel's pricing power and is influenced by location, brand tier, room quality, seasonality, and competitive dynamics. A select-service hotel in a suburban market might achieve an ADR of $110 to $140, while a full-service urban hotel could command $180 to $300 or more.

Occupancy rate is the percentage of available rooms that were sold during a given period. Most stabilized hotels operate at occupancy rates between 60% and 80%, with significant variation by market, segment, and season. Economy and midscale hotels in drive-to leisure markets may average 55% to 65%, while well-located select-service hotels near airports or business centers might sustain 70% to 80%.

Revenue Per Available Room (RevPAR) combines ADR and occupancy into a single metric by multiplying them together (or equivalently, dividing total room revenue by total available room nights). RevPAR is the most important metric for lenders because it captures both pricing power and demand. A hotel with a $150 ADR and 70% occupancy has a RevPAR of $105. This is a more useful indicator than either metric alone, because a high ADR means little if occupancy is low, and high occupancy at deeply discounted rates may not generate sufficient revenue to cover operating costs and debt service.

Lenders benchmark a property's RevPAR against its competitive set using the STR (Smith Travel Research) report, an industry-standard benchmarking tool. The STR report provides a RevPAR index (also called the RGI, or Revenue Generating Index) that compares the subject property's RevPAR to the average of its defined competitive set. An index of 100 means the property performs at the average; above 100 indicates outperformance. Lenders typically want to see a RevPAR index of 95 or higher for the trailing 12 months, with a stable or improving trend. A declining RevPAR trend or a RevPAR index significantly below 100 raises concerns about the property's competitive position and can lead to reduced loan sizing or unfavorable terms.

Franchise Agreement Requirements

For flagged hotel financing, the franchise agreement is not just an operating document; it is a critical component of the loan underwriting and security structure. Lenders view the franchise affiliation as integral to the property's ability to generate revenue, and they require specific protections to ensure the flag remains in place throughout the loan term.

The most important requirement is term alignment. Lenders want the franchise agreement to extend at least through the maturity date of the loan, and preferably several years beyond. If the franchise term expires before the loan matures, the lender faces the risk that the property could lose its brand affiliation (and the revenue support that comes with it) while the loan is still outstanding. When the franchise term is shorter than the proposed loan term, the borrower must either negotiate an extension with the franchisor or accept a shorter loan to match.

Lenders also require a "comfort letter" (sometimes called a "comfort agreement" or "SNI letter") from the franchisor. This document confirms that the franchisor acknowledges the lender's mortgage interest in the property and agrees to provide the lender with notice of any default under the franchise agreement, along with an opportunity to cure the default before the franchisor terminates the franchise. The comfort letter protects the lender's collateral by ensuring the flag cannot be pulled without advance warning and a chance to intervene. Most major franchisors have standardized comfort letter templates, but negotiating the specific terms can take several weeks and should be initiated early in the loan process.

Additionally, lenders will review any Property Improvement Plan (PIP) requirements imposed by the franchisor as a condition of maintaining or transferring the franchise. PIPs can require significant capital expenditures (often $5,000 to $25,000 per room or more) and must be completed within specified timeframes. The cost and timing of PIP work directly affect the loan structure, reserve requirements, and in some cases, the feasibility of the financing itself.

PIP (Property Improvement Plan) Financing

A Property Improvement Plan, or PIP, is a set of renovations and upgrades that a hotel franchisor requires as a condition of granting, renewing, or transferring a franchise agreement. PIPs are issued when a property changes ownership, when a franchise agreement comes up for renewal, or when the franchisor updates its brand standards. The scope can range from minor cosmetic updates (new soft goods, paint, signage) to major structural renovations (lobby redesigns, room reconfiguration, addition of new amenities like fitness centers or meeting space).

PIP costs vary widely depending on the brand, the property's current condition, and the scope of work required. A limited-service hotel undergoing a standard PIP might face costs of $8,000 to $15,000 per room, while a full-service hotel requiring a major renovation could see costs of $20,000 to $40,000 per room or more. For a 120-room hotel, that translates to a PIP budget of roughly $960,000 to $4.8 million, a significant capital outlay that must be factored into the acquisition or refinancing analysis.

Financing a PIP typically involves one of several strategies. The most common is a bridge-to-permanent structure, where the borrower secures a short-term bridge loan (12 to 36 months) that covers both the acquisition price and the PIP renovation budget. The bridge loan is interest-only during the renovation period, giving the borrower time to complete the PIP and stabilize the property at higher revenue levels before refinancing into a permanent loan at more favorable terms. Alternatively, some lenders will structure the PIP budget into a conventional acquisition loan by holding back a portion of the loan proceeds in a renovation reserve account, disbursing funds as the PIP work is completed and inspected. SBA loans can also incorporate PIP costs into the total project budget, though the combined loan amount must stay within SBA program limits.

Market Cycle Considerations in 2026

The hospitality lending environment in 2026 reflects a market that has largely recovered from the pandemic disruption but continues to navigate shifting demand patterns and evolving lender sentiment.

Leisure travel remains the strongest demand segment. The post-pandemic "revenge travel" surge has matured into a sustained preference for experiential spending, with consumers continuing to prioritize travel over goods purchases. Resort and drive-to leisure markets have performed particularly well, with many properties operating at or above pre-pandemic RevPAR levels (adjusted for inflation). Hotels in markets with year-round leisure appeal, such as coastal destinations, national park gateway cities, and warm-weather metros, are attracting strong lender interest and competitive financing terms.

Business travel has recovered to approximately 85% to 90% of 2019 levels nationally, but the recovery is uneven. Hotels in markets with diverse corporate demand generators (major cities with multiple Fortune 500 headquarters, convention destinations, regional business hubs) have bounced back strongly. Properties that relied heavily on a single corporate account or a narrow base of business travel demand continue to lag. Group and convention travel has rebounded more slowly, with many corporate meeting planners reducing event frequency or shifting to shorter formats. Hotels dependent on large-group bookings should plan for continued variability in that revenue stream.

On the supply side, new hotel construction starts slowed significantly in 2023 and 2024 due to elevated construction costs and higher interest rates. The pipeline of projects under construction in 2026 is well below the long-run average, which is a positive signal for existing hotel owners because limited new supply supports occupancy and pricing power for properties already in operation. However, lenders remain cautious about financing new construction in markets where existing supply already exceeds demand or where proposed projects lack clear differentiation.

Interest rates for hotel loans remain elevated compared to the ultra-low environment of 2020 and 2021, but they have stabilized. Borrowers with strong assets, experienced operating teams, and healthy debt service coverage are finding a competitive lending market with multiple capital sources actively quoting hotel deals. The key challenge in 2026 is not the availability of debt but rather the higher equity requirements and tighter coverage ratios that lenders impose on hospitality properties compared to other commercial real estate sectors.

Frequently Asked Questions

Flagged hotels (those operating under a national brand such as Marriott, Hilton, or IHG) consistently receive more favorable financing terms than independent hotels. Lenders view franchise-affiliated properties as lower risk because they benefit from centralized reservation systems, loyalty programs with millions of members, national marketing campaigns, and standardized operating procedures that drive higher and more predictable occupancy. The franchise flag essentially serves as a form of credit enhancement from the lender's perspective, reducing perceived revenue risk and broadening the pool of lenders willing to underwrite the deal.

The practical differences in loan terms are significant. A flagged select-service hotel might qualify for 65% to 70% LTV and a DSCR requirement of 1.25x to 1.35x, while a comparable independent hotel may be limited to 55% to 60% LTV with a DSCR of 1.40x to 1.50x. Interest rate spreads for independents are typically 75 to 150 basis points wider than for flagged properties of similar quality. CMBS lenders, which represent one of the largest sources of hotel financing for properties over $5 million, strongly prefer flagged hotels and may decline to quote independent properties unless they have an exceptionally strong operating history and market position.

Independent hotels can still secure financing, but they need to demonstrate their competitive strength through other means: a proven track record of three or more years of stable or growing RevPAR, strong online reputation scores, a well-defined market niche, experienced management, and a location or physical product that creates a durable competitive advantage. Boutique hotels in high-barrier-to-entry urban or resort markets, for example, can achieve favorable terms if the historical financials support the underwriting. For newer independents without an established track record, SBA loans or local bank relationships are typically the most realistic financing paths.

The minimum down payment for a hotel loan varies significantly depending on the loan program and the property's risk profile. SBA loans offer the highest leverage for qualifying borrowers, with down payments as low as 10% to 15% for owner-operators purchasing flagged properties with strong cash flow. The SBA 7(a) and SBA 504 programs are specifically designed to help small business owners acquire hospitality properties with less equity than conventional programs require. However, SBA eligibility is limited to active owner-operators, and loan sizes are capped at $5 million (7a) or approximately $5.5 million for the CDC portion (504).

For conventional bank loans, expect a minimum down payment of 30% to 45%, depending on the property type, market, franchise affiliation, and borrower strength. Banks lending on flagged select-service hotels with strong operating histories may accept 30% down, while independent properties or those in weaker markets may require 40% to 45%. CMBS hotel loans typically require 35% to 45% down (55% to 65% LTV) because the securitized structure requires additional protection against hospitality's inherent revenue volatility.

Bridge and construction loans base their equity requirements on total project cost rather than purchase price alone. Borrowers should expect to contribute 25% to 40% of the total cost, inclusive of land, construction, soft costs, and pre-opening expenses. In all cases, lenders want to see post-closing liquidity reserves (typically six to twelve months of debt service) in addition to the down payment. These reserves are not optional; they are a condition of the loan commitment and must be verified at closing.

Revenue per available room (RevPAR) is the single most important performance metric lenders use when underwriting hotel loans. RevPAR is calculated by multiplying the average daily rate (ADR) by the occupancy rate, or equivalently by dividing total room revenue by the total number of available room nights. It captures both pricing power and demand in a single number, making it a more useful indicator than either ADR or occupancy alone. A hotel with a high ADR but low occupancy may be overpricing its rooms, while a hotel with high occupancy at deeply discounted rates may not be generating enough revenue to cover operating costs and debt service.

Lenders benchmark a property's RevPAR against its competitive set using the STR (Smith Travel Research) report, which is an industry-standard benchmarking tool required by virtually every hotel lender. The STR report provides a RevPAR index (also called the Revenue Generating Index or RGI) that compares the subject property's RevPAR to the weighted average of its defined competitive set. An index of 100 means the property performs at the comp set average; above 100 indicates outperformance. Lenders typically want to see a RevPAR index of 95 or higher for the trailing 12 months, with a stable or improving trend. A declining RevPAR trend, even if the absolute number is still acceptable, can signal deteriorating competitiveness and may lead to reduced loan proceeds or less favorable terms.

For acquisition loans, lenders underwrite based on trailing 12-month RevPAR (not the seller's projections), often applying a conservative haircut of 5% to 10% to account for transition risk. For construction or value-add loans, the lender projects a stabilized RevPAR based on market studies and competitive analysis, then sizes the permanent loan using that projection. In both cases, the RevPAR analysis directly drives the net operating income calculation, which in turn determines the maximum loan amount through the DSCR constraint.

The SBA 7(a) program offers loans up to $5 million for hotel acquisitions, with terms up to 25 years, down payments as low as 10% to 15%, and interest rates that are competitive with conventional bank financing. The SBA 504 program provides fixed-rate financing for the real estate component of a hotel purchase, with the CDC portion going up to $5.5 million and a below-market fixed rate locked for 10 or 20 years.

Both programs require the borrower to be an active owner-operator, meaning you must be involved in the day-to-day management of the hotel. Absentee ownership does not qualify. The SBA also requires that the business meet its size standards for the hospitality industry (generally under $8 million in average annual revenue for hotels) and that the borrower contribute a minimum equity injection. Prior hotel management or ownership experience strengthens the application significantly, though first-time operators can qualify if they demonstrate relevant business experience and present a credible operating plan.

SBA hotel loans typically take 60 to 90 days to close and require standard third-party reports including an appraisal, environmental assessment, and franchise approval documentation if the property is flagged. The SBA guarantee (75% to 85% of the loan amount) significantly reduces the participating lender's risk, which is why SBA programs can offer higher leverage and longer terms than conventional hotel financing. For borrowers who meet the owner-operator requirement, SBA loans offer the best leverage and longest terms available in the hotel lending market, making them the preferred option for first-time hotel buyers and small portfolio operators acquiring properties in the $1 million to $5 million range.

Construction-to-permanent financing for hotels is a two-phase structure that begins with a construction loan to fund the building of the hotel and then converts into a permanent (long-term) mortgage once the property is completed and stabilized. During the construction phase, the lender disburses funds in stages as construction milestones are met, and the borrower typically pays interest only on the drawn balance. Construction loan terms for hotels usually run 18 to 36 months, with LTV based on 60% to 75% of the projected completed value or total project cost, whichever produces the lower loan amount.

The conversion from construction to permanent financing is the critical transition point. It is not automatic. The property must demonstrate that it can support the debt service at the permanent loan terms, which usually means achieving a specified occupancy threshold (often 60% to 65%) sustained for three to six consecutive months. Some lenders offer single-close construction-to-permanent loans where both phases are documented at the outset. This approach saves the borrower the cost and uncertainty of arranging a separate takeout loan, but it also means the permanent loan terms must be agreed upon before the property has any operating history, which can result in more conservative underwriting.

Hotel construction loans carry several additional requirements beyond standard commercial construction. Lenders typically require a signed franchise agreement (for flagged properties), an approved pre-opening budget covering FF&E procurement, staff hiring, training, and marketing, a detailed feasibility study from a recognized hospitality consulting firm, and evidence of the development team's prior hotel construction experience. The pre-opening budget is a unique element of hotel development that does not exist in other property types. It typically represents 3% to 5% of total project cost and must be funded before the hotel generates any revenue. Borrowers should plan for a total development timeline of 30 to 48 months from groundbreaking to stabilization, and they must have sufficient liquidity to cover carrying costs throughout that period.
