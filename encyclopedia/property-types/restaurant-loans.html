Restaurant Real Estate Loans: Financing for Owner-Occupied Restaurants

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Typical loan amount: $250K to $5M
  • Max LTV: 65% to 80% (up to 90% with SBA)
  • Interest rates: 6.5% to 9% (varies by program)
  • Term length: 10 to 25 years
  • SBA options: SBA 504 (real estate), SBA 7(a) (flexible use)
  • DSCR requirement: 1.20x to 1.35x

Restaurant Real Estate Financing: The Landscape

Financing the purchase of restaurant real estate sits at the intersection of two lending disciplines: commercial real estate and small business lending. Unlike a standard office building or warehouse, a restaurant property is deeply tied to the operating business inside it. The building's value depends not just on its location, square footage, and condition, but also on the revenue-generating capacity of the food service operation it houses. This dual nature creates both opportunities and challenges for borrowers seeking capital.

Most restaurant real estate loans fall in the $250,000 to $5 million range, covering everything from standalone fast-casual locations in suburban strip centers to full-service dining establishments in urban cores. The financing landscape is dominated by three main channels: SBA government-guaranteed loans (504 and 7(a) programs), conventional bank loans from community and regional banks, and specialized franchise lending programs offered by national banks with dedicated restaurant divisions. Each channel has its own underwriting approach, rate structure, and borrower requirements.

What makes restaurant lending distinct from other commercial property types is the risk profile. Restaurants have higher failure rates than most other small businesses, and lenders price that risk into their terms. The National Restaurant Association reports that roughly 60% of restaurants close within the first year and nearly 80% shutter within five years. Lenders respond to these statistics by requiring stronger operator credentials, larger cash reserves, and more conservative loan-to-value ratios than they would for, say, a medical office building or industrial warehouse. Borrowers who understand these dynamics and prepare accordingly will find a much smoother path to closing.

Owner-Occupied vs. Investor Scenarios

The underwriting approach for restaurant real estate varies significantly depending on whether the borrower intends to operate the restaurant themselves or is purchasing the property as an investment (leasing it to a restaurant tenant).

Owner-Occupied Restaurant Properties

When the restaurant owner is also the property owner, lenders evaluate both the real estate and the business as a single unit. This is the most common scenario for restaurant acquisitions, and it opens the door to SBA financing, which offers the most favorable terms available. Owner-occupancy means the borrower must operate their restaurant out of at least 51% of the building's usable space. Underwriting focuses heavily on the restaurant's historical financial performance (or projected performance for new locations), the operator's industry experience, and the business's ability to generate enough cash flow to service the debt while covering operating expenses.

The advantage of owner-occupancy is access to lower down payments (as little as 10% with SBA 504), longer amortization schedules (up to 25 years), and below-market interest rates on the government-guaranteed portion of the loan. The trade-off is that lenders require a personal guarantee from the borrower, and underwriting scrutiny of the business operations is more intensive than for a passive real estate investment.

Investor-Owned Restaurant Properties

When an investor purchases a property and leases it to a restaurant operator, the underwriting shifts to a traditional commercial real estate model. The lender evaluates the tenant's creditworthiness, the lease terms (length, rent escalations, renewal options), and the property's value independent of the restaurant operation. Investor-owned restaurant properties typically face stricter LTV limits (65% to 75%), higher interest rates, and shorter amortization periods because the lender cannot rely on SBA guarantees. The strength of the tenant matters enormously: a property leased to a national franchise chain with a 15-year triple-net lease will receive far better terms than one leased to a first-time independent operator on a three-year lease.

SBA 504 Loans for Restaurant Real Estate

The SBA 504 program is the single most powerful financing tool available to restaurant owners who want to purchase their own building. The program is specifically designed for owner-occupied commercial real estate and offers a unique structure that minimizes the borrower's out-of-pocket investment while locking in a below-market fixed interest rate on a significant portion of the loan.

How the 504 Structure Works

An SBA 504 loan is actually two loans working together. A conventional bank (the "first mortgage lender") provides 50% of the total project cost as a first-position mortgage at market rates. A Certified Development Company (CDC), a nonprofit entity licensed by the SBA, provides up to 40% of the project cost as a second-position loan at a fixed rate set by the SBA debenture market. The borrower contributes the remaining 10% as a down payment. For new businesses (less than two years of operation) or special-purpose properties (which many restaurants qualify as), the borrower's injection may increase to 15% or 20%.

The CDC portion is the standout feature. Its interest rate is based on the current market rate for SBA debentures, which historically runs 50 to 150 basis points below conventional commercial mortgage rates. The term on the CDC portion is 20 or 25 years with full amortization, meaning there is no balloon payment. The maximum SBA debenture amount is $5.5 million for standard projects. When combined with the bank's first mortgage (which can be larger), the total project financing can exceed $10 million.

504 Requirements for Restaurant Borrowers

To qualify, the restaurant business must have a tangible net worth under $20 million and average net income under $6.5 million after taxes for the prior two years. The borrower must occupy at least 51% of the building. The property must be located in the CDC's geographic service area. The borrower must demonstrate the ability to service the combined first mortgage and CDC loan payments from the restaurant's cash flow, typically with a debt service coverage ratio of at least 1.20x. Personal guarantees are required from anyone owning 20% or more of the business.

SBA 7(a) Loans for Restaurant Purchases

The SBA 7(a) program is the SBA's most popular and most flexible loan product. Unlike the 504, which is restricted to real estate and major fixed assets, the 7(a) can be used for virtually any legitimate business purpose: real estate acquisition, equipment purchases, leasehold improvements, working capital, inventory, debt refinancing, or even business acquisition (including goodwill). This versatility makes the 7(a) particularly attractive for restaurant deals where the borrower needs to finance more than just the building.

Key 7(a) Parameters for Restaurant Borrowers

The maximum 7(a) loan amount is $5 million. Interest rates are variable, typically pegged to the prime rate plus a spread of 1.5% to 2.75% depending on the loan size and term. For loans over $500,000 with terms longer than 10 years, the maximum spread is prime plus 2.75%. Real estate loans under the 7(a) program can carry terms up to 25 years. Equipment and working capital portions are typically limited to 10-year terms. The SBA guarantees 75% of the loan for amounts over $150,000, which reduces the lender's risk and enables them to offer terms they would not otherwise provide to a restaurant borrower.

Down payment requirements under the 7(a) are set by the individual lender, not the SBA, but typically range from 10% to 20% of the total project cost. Lenders with SBA Preferred Lender Program (PLP) status can approve loans in-house without SBA review, which significantly speeds up the process. For restaurant deals, working with a PLP lender is strongly recommended because the faster turnaround can make the difference between securing a property and losing it to a competing buyer.

Franchise Restaurant Financing

Franchise restaurants occupy a unique position in the lending landscape because the brand's track record supplements the individual borrower's profile. Lenders financing a McDonald's, Chick-fil-A, Subway, or Wingstop location can draw on system-wide data covering thousands of units, including average unit volumes (AUVs), food cost ratios, labor percentages, and failure rates. This data-driven underwriting reduces the lender's uncertainty and often results in better terms for the borrower.

The SBA Franchise Registry

The SBA maintains a Franchise Registry, a database of franchise systems whose franchise agreements have been pre-reviewed and approved for SBA lending. If a franchise is listed on the registry, the SBA lender does not need to review the franchise agreement for eligibility, which eliminates weeks of processing time and legal expense. Most major restaurant franchises are listed. Borrowers should verify their target franchise's registry status before beginning the loan application process.

Franchisor Financial Requirements

Many franchise systems impose their own financial requirements on franchisees, which may exceed what the lender requires. These often include minimum net worth thresholds (ranging from $250,000 to over $1 million depending on the brand), minimum liquid capital requirements, and restrictions on how much of the total investment can be financed with debt. Some franchisors require that the franchisee have a certain amount of "unencumbered cash" after the investment is complete. Borrowers need to satisfy both the franchisor's requirements and the lender's requirements simultaneously, which requires careful financial planning before submitting applications to either party.

Multi-Unit and Area Development Financing

Experienced franchise operators who have committed to opening multiple units under an area development agreement can often secure better financing terms. Lenders view multi-unit operators as lower risk because they have proven management systems, existing cash flow from operating units, and a demonstrated ability to execute. Some national banks have dedicated franchise lending divisions that offer streamlined processing and portfolio pricing for operators with three or more existing units.

Liquor License Considerations

A restaurant's liquor license can significantly affect both the property's value and the lender's appetite for the deal. Full liquor licenses (allowing the sale of beer, wine, and spirits) are limited in quantity by most state and local jurisdictions, making them valuable transferable assets. In some markets, a full liquor license alone can be worth $50,000 to $500,000 or more. Lenders recognize this value, and a transferable liquor license attached to the property or business can strengthen a loan application by providing additional collateral and demonstrating higher revenue potential.

However, liquor licenses also introduce regulatory risk. If the license is non-transferable, expires, or is subject to revocation due to violations, the property's income potential drops sharply. Lenders will want to verify the license type, its transferability, any pending violations, and the local regulatory environment before committing to a loan. In jurisdictions where licenses are scarce and valuable, lenders may require that the license be pledged as additional collateral. Borrowers should consult with a liquor license attorney early in the acquisition process to understand the transfer requirements and timeline, as license transfers can take 60 to 120 days and may require separate regulatory approval.

Buildout and Renovation Financing

Restaurant properties almost always require significant buildout or renovation before they can open for business. Kitchen equipment installation, hood and ventilation systems, grease trap infrastructure, dining room finishes, ADA compliance upgrades, and signage represent substantial capital expenditures that typically range from $100 to $500 per square foot depending on the restaurant concept and the condition of the existing space.

Construction Draw Structures

When financing includes a buildout component, lenders typically structure the construction portion as a draw-based facility. Rather than disbursing the full construction amount at closing, the lender releases funds in stages as work is completed and verified by a third-party inspector. This protects the lender from funding a project that stalls mid-construction. Borrowers should plan for the cash flow implications of this structure: they will need to pay contractors upfront (or on short payment terms) and then submit draw requests for reimbursement, which can take 5 to 15 business days to process.

Cost Overrun Risks

Restaurant buildouts are notoriously susceptible to cost overruns. Unexpected conditions behind walls (outdated plumbing, insufficient electrical capacity, asbestos), permitting delays, and supply chain disruptions can push project costs 10% to 30% above the original budget. Lenders are well aware of this risk and typically require a contingency reserve of 5% to 15% of the total construction budget. Borrowers who underestimate buildout costs and exhaust their loan proceeds before the restaurant opens face a dangerous gap: they need additional capital to finish the project but have no operating revenue to support a second loan. Building a realistic budget with adequate contingency reserves is one of the most important steps in the financing process.

Key Underwriting Factors for Restaurant Loans

Restaurant lending underwriting goes beyond the standard commercial real estate metrics. Lenders evaluate a set of restaurant-specific financial and operational indicators that reveal whether the business can reliably service its debt.

Food Cost Ratios

Lenders examine the restaurant's cost of goods sold (COGS) as a percentage of total revenue. A well-run restaurant typically maintains food costs between 28% and 35% of revenue, depending on the concept. Fine dining establishments may run higher food costs (32% to 38%) offset by higher check averages. Fast-casual concepts often target food costs below 30%. A food cost ratio that is significantly above industry benchmarks signals operational inefficiency or pricing problems, both of which threaten the cash flow needed to service debt.

Revenue per Square Foot

This metric helps lenders assess whether the restaurant is generating adequate sales relative to the space it occupies. Higher revenue per square foot indicates efficient use of the real estate and stronger ability to cover occupancy costs (rent, property taxes, insurance, and loan payments). The benchmark varies widely by concept: a high-volume fast-casual restaurant might generate $500 to $800 per square foot annually, while a full-service restaurant in a suburban market might produce $250 to $400 per square foot. Lenders compare the subject restaurant's performance against these benchmarks when evaluating the loan request.

Operator Experience

The borrower's restaurant management and ownership experience is one of the most critical underwriting factors. Lenders want to see at least two to three years of relevant industry experience, and they strongly prefer borrowers who have managed or owned restaurants of a similar concept and scale. A first-time restaurant owner with no food service background will face significantly higher borrowing costs and more restrictive terms than a seasoned operator with a track record of profitable locations.

Lease Term vs. Loan Term Alignment

For properties where the restaurant occupies leased space (rather than owning the building), lenders require that the remaining lease term (including any exercisable renewal options) exceed the loan term. If a borrower is seeking a 10-year equipment and improvement loan, the lease should have at least 10 to 15 years remaining. This protects the lender from a scenario where the restaurant loses its lease before the loan matures, destroying the collateral value of the leasehold improvements and equipment in place.

Fast-Casual vs. Full-Service Lending Differences

Lenders do not treat all restaurants the same. The distinction between fast-casual and full-service concepts has meaningful implications for underwriting, loan terms, and the total cost of financing.

Fast-Casual Restaurants

Fast-casual concepts (think Chipotle, Sweetgreen, or Panera-style operations) are generally viewed more favorably by lenders. These restaurants typically require less square footage (1,200 to 2,500 square feet), lower buildout costs, smaller staffing levels, and simpler kitchen infrastructure. Their operating margins tend to be more predictable because the limited-service model reduces labor cost volatility. Revenue per square foot is often higher than full-service restaurants because of faster table turns and lower overhead. Lenders may offer fast-casual borrowers slightly better LTV ratios and modestly lower rates compared to full-service operators.

The capital requirements for a fast-casual location also tend to be lower overall, with total project costs (real estate plus buildout plus equipment) frequently falling in the $350,000 to $1.5 million range. This makes the deal easier to finance and gives the borrower a shorter runway to breakeven, which reduces the lender's risk exposure.

Full-Service Restaurants

Full-service restaurants with sit-down dining, full bar programs, and larger footprints (3,000 to 8,000+ square feet) require significantly more capital and carry higher operating risk. Buildout costs are greater due to larger kitchen facilities, bar infrastructure, dining room finishes, and more complex HVAC and ventilation systems. Staffing costs are higher because full-service operations require servers, bartenders, bussers, and hosts in addition to kitchen staff. Revenue can be more volatile because full-service restaurants are more sensitive to economic downturns, weather, and seasonal fluctuations.

Lenders underwriting full-service restaurant loans typically require higher DSCR thresholds (1.30x to 1.35x versus 1.20x to 1.25x for fast-casual), larger down payments, and may impose operating covenants such as minimum cash reserve requirements. The borrower's operating experience is scrutinized more closely because the complexity of running a full-service restaurant is significantly greater than managing a fast-casual operation. Total project costs for full-service locations commonly range from $750,000 to $5 million or more, depending on location and concept.

Preparing a Strong Loan Application

Restaurant borrowers can improve their chances of approval and secure better terms by assembling a well-organized loan package that addresses the specific concerns lenders have about restaurant properties.

  • Three years of tax returns and financial statements: Personal and business returns for all guarantors and any existing restaurant operations. If the business has fewer than three years of history, provide whatever is available along with detailed projections.
  • Profit and loss projections: For new locations, provide month-by-month P&L projections for the first 24 months with clearly stated assumptions about revenue ramp-up, food costs, labor costs, and operating expenses.
  • Operator resume: A detailed resume highlighting all food service and restaurant management experience. Include specific roles, years of experience, and performance metrics from prior positions.
  • Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD): For franchise deals, include the current FDD and the executed franchise agreement. Highlight the franchise's Item 19 financial performance representations.
  • Construction budget and timeline: If the project includes buildout or renovation, provide a detailed budget broken down by trade (plumbing, electrical, HVAC, kitchen equipment, finishes) along with contractor bids and a construction timeline.
  • Lease or purchase agreement: The executed purchase contract or letter of intent for the property, including any seller concessions, contingencies, or special terms.

Borrowers who present a thorough, well-organized application package signal to the lender that they are serious, prepared, and capable operators. This is particularly important in restaurant lending, where the lender's confidence in the operator is often the deciding factor between approval and decline.

That said, strong independent operators with profitable existing locations and a clear expansion strategy can absolutely secure competitive financing; they simply need to work harder to make the case to the lender.

The minimum down payment depends on the loan program and the borrower's profile. The lowest available down payment is 10%, offered through the SBA 504 program for established restaurant operators purchasing owner-occupied property. This 10% injection applies when the business has been operating for at least two years and the property is not classified as a special-purpose or single-use asset. For startup restaurants (less than two years of operating history) or special-purpose properties, the SBA 504 program requires 15% to 20% down.

SBA 7(a) loans typically require 10% to 20% down, depending on the lender's internal requirements and the overall strength of the deal. Lenders have discretion to set their own equity injection standards within SBA guidelines, so shopping multiple lenders can yield different down payment requirements for the same deal. Conventional bank loans for restaurant properties generally require 20% to 35% down because there is no government guarantee to cushion the lender's risk.

It is important to note that "down payment" in commercial lending includes more than just the cash at closing. Lenders count the borrower's total equity injection, which can include cash, the appraised value of equipment being contributed to the project, seller financing (if structured properly and subordinated), and in some cases, the value of land already owned by the borrower. Borrowers should discuss all potential equity sources with their lender early in the process to minimize the cash outlay required at closing.

Restaurant experience is not a formal requirement for most loan programs, but it is one of the most heavily weighted factors in the underwriting decision. SBA lenders are specifically instructed to evaluate whether the borrower has the management experience and technical skills needed to operate the business successfully. A borrower with 10 years of restaurant management experience will receive a very different reception than a first-time operator with no food service background.

For borrowers who lack direct restaurant experience, there are several ways to strengthen the application. The most effective approach is to bring an experienced operating partner into the business. This could be a general manager, executive chef, or co-owner with a proven track record in restaurant operations. Lenders view this favorably because it addresses their primary concern about operational risk. Another option is to pursue a franchise concept where the franchisor provides structured training and ongoing operational support. Many SBA lenders will accept completion of a franchisor's training program as partial mitigation of the experience gap.

Borrowers should also consider related experience that demonstrates transferable skills. Prior experience in hospitality management, food manufacturing, retail management, or other customer-facing businesses can help make the case. The key is to present a credible narrative showing that the borrower understands the operational demands of running a restaurant and has either the personal skills or the team in place to execute successfully.

Under the 7(a) program, you can use loan proceeds for real estate acquisition, equipment purchases, leasehold improvements, working capital, and even business acquisition costs, all within the same loan up to $5 million. SBA 504 loans are more restrictive. Kitchen equipment, point-of-sale systems, and furniture typically have useful lives of 5 to 10 years, and lenders generally will not amortize these items over a 20- or 25-year real estate term. Separating the equipment loan allows the bank to match the repayment period to the asset's useful life, which is standard practice in commercial lending. Borrowers should discuss the optimal structure with their lender early in the process to avoid surprises at closing.
