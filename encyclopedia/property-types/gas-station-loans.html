Gas Station and Convenience Store Loans: Financing Guide

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Typical loan amount: $500K to $10M
  • Max LTV: 65% to 75%
  • Interest rates: 6.5% to 9.5% (varies by program)
  • Term length: 10 to 25 years (SBA); 5 to 10 years (conventional)
  • DSCR requirement: 1.20x to 1.35x
  • Common lenders: SBA lenders, community banks, specialty fuel lenders

Why Gas Station Financing Is Different

Gas stations and convenience stores occupy a unique position in commercial lending. Unlike a typical retail property or office building, a gas station is both a piece of commercial real estate and an active operating business. Lenders must evaluate not just the land, building, and improvements, but also fuel sales volume, convenience store revenue, environmental compliance, and the strength of the operator. This dual nature makes gas station loans more complex than standard commercial mortgages, and it explains why many mainstream lenders avoid the property type altogether.

The fuel retail industry in the United States includes roughly 150,000 gas stations, the majority of which are independently owned and operated. Whether you are buying an existing station, refinancing one you already own, or building a new location from the ground up, understanding how lenders approach this property type will help you structure a stronger application and negotiate better terms.

Revenue at a gas station typically comes from two distinct streams. Fuel sales generate the highest gross revenue, but profit margins on gasoline are thin, usually between $0.10 and $0.30 per gallon depending on market conditions and whether the station is branded or independent. Convenience store operations (often called "inside sales") carry much higher margins, typically 25% to 35% on food, beverages, tobacco, and general merchandise. Lenders pay close attention to the ratio between these two income streams. A station that derives a large share of its profit from the convenience store is generally seen as less risky than one that depends entirely on fuel volume.

SBA Loan Programs for Gas Stations

Small Business Administration (SBA) loans are the most common financing tool for gas station purchases, particularly for first-time buyers and owner-operators. Two programs dominate this space: the SBA 7(a) and the SBA 504.

SBA 7(a) Loans

The SBA 7(a) program is the more flexible of the two. It provides loans up to $5 million that can be used to purchase real estate, equipment, inventory, and business goodwill, all within a single loan. Terms can extend up to 25 years for real estate and up to 10 years for equipment and working capital. Interest rates may be fixed or variable, and the SBA guarantees up to 85% of the loan (for loans of $150,000 or less) or 75% (for loans above $150,000), which reduces the lender's risk and makes approval more likely.

For gas station buyers, the SBA 7(a) is particularly useful because it allows you to finance business goodwill. When purchasing an existing station, a significant portion of the purchase price often reflects the value of the established customer base, fuel supply agreements, and operating history. Conventional lenders rarely finance goodwill, but SBA 7(a) lenders will. You will need a down payment of 10% to 15%, a personal credit score of at least 680, and relevant experience in the fuel retail or convenience store industry. The SBA also requires the borrower to occupy at least 51% of the property.

SBA 504 Loans

The SBA 504 program is designed specifically for the purchase of owner-occupied commercial real estate and major fixed assets. In a 504 transaction, financing comes from two sources: a conventional lender provides approximately 50% of the project cost, and a Certified Development Company (CDC) provides up to 40% through an SBA-backed debenture. The borrower contributes the remaining 10% as a down payment. The CDC portion carries a fixed interest rate, which protects borrowers from rising rates over the life of the loan.

The maximum CDC loan amount is $5.5 million for standard projects. Terms are either 10 or 20 years. The SBA 504 is best suited for buyers who are purchasing the real estate itself and plan to operate the station long-term. It does not cover business goodwill or working capital, so if the purchase price includes a significant goodwill component, you may need to pair the 504 with a separate loan or seller financing to cover that portion.

Environmental Concerns and Due Diligence

Environmental risk is the single most important factor that separates gas station lending from other commercial property types. Every gas station sits atop underground storage tanks (USTs) that hold thousands of gallons of petroleum. If those tanks have leaked, the resulting soil and groundwater contamination can cost hundreds of thousands of dollars to remediate, and the property owner is typically responsible.

Phase I and Phase II Environmental Site Assessments

Every lender will require a Phase I Environmental Site Assessment (ESA) before issuing a gas station loan. A Phase I is a records review and site inspection that identifies recognized environmental conditions (RECs), meaning evidence of past or potential contamination. The Phase I does not involve sampling; it is a desktop and visual investigation conducted by a licensed environmental professional.

If the Phase I identifies RECs, the lender will require a Phase II ESA. This involves physical sampling of soil, groundwater, and sometimes soil vapor to determine whether contamination actually exists and, if so, its extent. Phase II assessments can cost $10,000 to $50,000 or more, depending on the number of samples and the complexity of the site. If contamination is confirmed, the lender will want to see a remediation plan with cost estimates before proceeding.

Underground Storage Tank Compliance

Federal and state regulations require that USTs meet specific standards for construction, leak detection, corrosion protection, and financial responsibility. Tanks installed before 1998 that have not been upgraded typically do not meet current standards and will need to be replaced. Modern double-walled tanks with electronic leak detection systems are strongly preferred by lenders. Tank age is a significant underwriting factor; most lenders will not finance a station with single-walled tanks or tanks older than 25 to 30 years unless a replacement plan is part of the loan.

Environmental Insurance

Environmental insurance policies, sometimes called pollution legal liability (PLL) policies, can be an effective tool for mitigating lender concerns. These policies cover cleanup costs if previously unknown contamination is discovered after the property changes hands. Premiums vary based on the property's environmental history, tank age, and coverage limits, but they typically range from $3,000 to $15,000 per year. Some lenders require environmental insurance as a condition of the loan; others will accept it as a credit enhancement that can improve your terms.

Branded vs. Unbranded Gas Stations

Whether a gas station operates under a major oil company brand (such as Shell, BP, ExxonMobil, or Chevron) or as an independent unbranded station has a meaningful impact on financing.

Branded Stations

Branded stations operate under a fuel supply agreement with a major petroleum company. These agreements typically run 10 to 15 years and require the operator to purchase fuel exclusively from the brand's supply network. In exchange, the operator receives brand signage, marketing support, loyalty program participation, and sometimes equipment financing from the brand. Lenders generally view branded stations favorably. The brand relationship provides revenue predictability, consumer recognition, and a level of corporate oversight that reduces perceived risk.

However, branded agreements also come with obligations. The brand may require specific facility upgrades, image standards, and equipment specifications. These covenants can affect the borrower's capital requirements and operating flexibility. If the fuel supply agreement is near expiration or includes early termination clauses, lenders will scrutinize the risk that the station could lose its brand affiliation. Some lenders will require the fuel supply agreement to extend beyond the loan maturity date.

Unbranded (Independent) Stations

Unbranded stations purchase fuel on the open market, typically from regional distributors or through spot market purchases. This flexibility allows independent operators to shop for the best wholesale fuel prices, which can improve per-gallon margins. However, unbranded stations lack the marketing support and consumer trust associated with a national brand. Lenders may view them as higher risk, particularly in competitive markets where branded stations dominate.

Financing terms for unbranded stations often reflect this increased risk. Expect higher down payment requirements (often 5% to 10% more than branded equivalents), slightly higher interest rates, and more conservative LTV ratios. That said, many specialty fuel lenders are comfortable with independent stations, especially those that have strong convenience store operations or are located in areas with limited competition.

Income Verification and Underwriting

Underwriting a gas station loan requires lenders to separate the business income from the real estate income, a distinction that is more nuanced here than in almost any other property type.

Fuel Margins vs. Convenience Store Margins

Fuel sales may represent 60% to 80% of total gross revenue, but the actual profit from fuel is relatively small. Net fuel margins typically range from $0.10 to $0.30 per gallon after accounting for credit card processing fees, which consume 2% to 3% of each transaction. A station pumping 100,000 gallons per month might generate only $15,000 to $30,000 in net fuel profit.

Convenience store operations, by contrast, often generate equal or greater profit on a fraction of the gross revenue. A well-run c-store with food service, beverages, tobacco, and general merchandise can achieve net margins of 25% to 35%. Lenders increasingly focus on the c-store component as the primary driver of debt service coverage, particularly as electric vehicle adoption gradually reduces fuel demand in some markets.

How Lenders Evaluate Income

Lenders will typically request three years of business tax returns, profit and loss statements, and balance sheets. They will reconstruct net operating income by starting with total revenue, subtracting cost of goods sold (fuel and merchandise), operating expenses, and reserves. Key metrics include the debt service coverage ratio (DSCR), which lenders want to see at 1.20x to 1.35x or higher, and the loan-to-value ratio (LTV), which typically caps at 65% to 75% for conventional loans and up to 90% for SBA loans.

Inventory is another consideration. Gas stations carry significant fuel inventory (often $50,000 to $150,000 in the tanks at any given time) plus merchandise inventory in the store. Lenders will want to understand inventory turnover rates and whether inventory values are stable or fluctuating with commodity prices.

Key Underwriting Factors

Beyond financials and environmental compliance, lenders evaluate several property-specific and market-specific factors when underwriting gas station loans.

Location and Traffic

Location is paramount. Lenders prefer stations on high-traffic corridors, major intersections, highway interchange locations, and areas with strong commuter traffic. Daily traffic counts are a standard part of the underwriting file. A station on a road with 20,000 or more vehicles per day is significantly more attractive to lenders than one on a secondary road with 5,000 vehicles per day. Corner locations with good ingress and egress, high visibility, and adequate parking are ideal.

Competition Radius

Lenders will map competing stations within a defined radius, typically one to three miles, to assess market saturation. Fewer competitors generally means stronger pricing power and more stable fuel volumes. If a new station is planned nearby, or if a competitor has recently undergone a major renovation, lenders will factor that into their risk assessment.

Tank Age and Compliance

As noted in the environmental section, tank age and regulatory compliance are critical. Lenders want to see modern, double-walled USTs with electronic leak detection, overfill prevention, and corrosion protection. Stations with recently installed tanks (within the last 10 to 15 years) will receive the most favorable treatment. Older tanks may require a tank replacement budget as part of the loan structure.

Convenience Store Size and Condition

The physical characteristics of the convenience store matter. Larger c-stores (2,000 to 4,000+ square feet) with food service capability, walk-in coolers, and modern fixtures generate higher revenue and improve the overall risk profile. Lenders may discount the value of a station with a small, outdated c-store that lacks room for growth. Properties with car wash facilities or additional revenue streams (such as ATMs, lottery sales, or propane exchange) are viewed as stronger collateral.

Franchise vs. Independent Operations

The distinction between franchise and independent gas station operations extends beyond just the brand on the sign. It affects loan structure, lender appetite, and the overall financing process.

Franchise Operations

Franchise gas stations operate under agreements that govern fuel supply, branding, site standards, and sometimes even convenience store product mix. Major franchisors include brands like 7-Eleven (which combines fuel and c-store), Circle K, and branded fuel operations tied to companies like Marathon, Phillips 66, and Valero. Lenders tend to be more comfortable with franchise operations because the franchisor provides training, operational support, and brand equity that can stabilize revenue.

However, franchise agreements also introduce complexity. Lenders will review the franchise agreement for terms that could affect their collateral position, including restrictions on transfer, requirements for franchisor consent in the event of default, and covenants that obligate the borrower to make capital improvements. If the franchise agreement has a shorter remaining term than the loan, lenders may require evidence that the agreement will be renewed or they may shorten the loan term accordingly.

Independent Operations

Independent operators have full control over their fuel sourcing, product mix, pricing strategy, and store operations. This autonomy can be a significant advantage in markets where independent operators can capture higher fuel margins through savvy purchasing. Some of the most profitable gas stations in the country are independents that have built strong local brands and invested heavily in their convenience store offerings.

From a lending perspective, independent operators face a higher burden of proof. Without the implicit backing of a national brand, the borrower's personal experience, credit history, and financial strength carry even more weight. Lenders will scrutinize the operator's track record in the industry, the stability of their fuel supply relationships, and the station's historical financial performance. New-to-industry borrowers will find it significantly harder to obtain financing for an independent station than for a branded franchise.

Preparing Your Gas Station Loan Application

A well-prepared loan application can make the difference between approval and denial in gas station financing. Here is what most lenders will require.

Financial documents: Three years of personal and business tax returns, year-to-date profit and loss statements, a current balance sheet, and a personal financial statement for each guarantor. If you are purchasing an existing station, you will also need the seller's financial records for the same period.

Environmental documentation: A current Phase I ESA (completed within the past 180 days), and Phase II results if applicable. Copies of UST registration, tank test results, and any state environmental compliance correspondence.

Property details: A current appraisal (the lender will typically order this), site survey, property condition report, fuel equipment inventory, and details on the age and condition of USTs, dispensers, and the canopy.

Business information: Fuel supply agreements, franchise or brand agreements, vendor contracts, equipment leases, and a current fuel volume report showing monthly gallons pumped by product type. Lenders will also want to see a business plan if you are a first-time buyer or if you plan to make significant operational changes.

Operator qualifications: Your resume highlighting relevant industry experience, any certifications related to fuel handling or convenience store management, and references from industry contacts or suppliers.

Organizing these materials before you approach lenders will speed up the process and demonstrate that you are a serious, knowledgeable buyer. Gas station loans often take 60 to 120 days to close, depending on the program and the complexity of the environmental review.

A Phase I ESA typically costs $2,500 to $5,000, while a Phase II can range from $10,000 to $50,000 depending on site conditions. These costs are borne by the borrower and are not refundable if the deal falls through.

Branded gas stations operate under a fuel supply agreement with a major oil company such as Shell, ExxonMobil, BP, or Chevron. These agreements provide revenue predictability, consumer recognition, and marketing support, all of which make lenders more comfortable. Branded stations typically qualify for lower down payments, better interest rates, and higher LTV ratios compared to their unbranded counterparts. The brand itself serves as a form of risk mitigation in the lender's eyes.

Unbranded (independent) stations purchase fuel on the open market, which can result in lower wholesale costs and higher per-gallon margins. However, they lack the backing of a national brand, which means lenders may perceive them as carrying more risk. Financing for unbranded stations often comes with higher down payment requirements (sometimes 5% to 10% more), slightly elevated interest rates, and more conservative LTV caps. Specialty fuel lenders and community banks with gas station experience are typically the best sources for unbranded station financing.

The fuel supply agreement itself also affects the loan. Lenders will review the agreement's remaining term, renewal provisions, and any covenants that could affect the borrower's operations or the lender's collateral position. If you are buying a branded station, make sure the fuel supply agreement will transfer to you at closing, and verify that the remaining term extends at least as long as your loan term. A short remaining agreement term can complicate financing or limit your available loan programs.

The minimum down payment varies by loan program. SBA 7(a) and SBA 504 loans offer the lowest entry point, typically requiring 10% to 15% of the total project cost. For a $1.5 million gas station purchase, that translates to $150,000 to $225,000. Conventional bank loans require 25% to 35% down, and specialty or private lenders may require 30% to 40%. The specific percentage depends on the borrower's experience, credit profile, and the property's risk characteristics.

Several factors can push your down payment requirement higher than the standard minimum. Environmental risk on the property, limited operator experience, declining fuel volumes, a location in a secondary or rural market, and older underground storage tanks can all lead lenders to require additional equity. Conversely, strong borrower credentials, a clean environmental record, high-traffic locations, and modern equipment can sometimes allow you to negotiate a lower down payment within the program's guidelines.

Seller financing is sometimes used to bridge the gap between the buyer's available cash and the required down payment. In these arrangements, the seller carries a note for a portion of the purchase price, reducing the cash the buyer needs at closing. SBA programs permit seller financing under specific conditions: the seller note must typically be on full standby (no payments) for at least two years, or it must meet other SBA requirements regarding payment terms and subordination.

Gas station valuation is more complex than typical commercial real estate appraisals because the property includes both a real estate component and an active business operation. Appraisers and lenders typically use three approaches. The income approach examines the station's net operating income from fuel sales and convenience store operations, then applies a capitalization rate to determine value. The market (sales comparison) approach compares recent sale prices of similar stations in the region. The cost approach estimates the replacement cost of the land, buildings, canopy, fuel dispensers, and underground storage tanks, minus depreciation.

Most lenders place the greatest weight on the income approach. They will separate fuel income from c-store income to assess each revenue stream independently. Fuel margins are typically thin ($0.10 to $0.30 per gallon), so lenders focus on gallon volume and the stability of that volume over time. C-store margins are much healthier (25% to 35%), and a well-performing convenience store can significantly increase the property's appraised value. Lenders also consider other income sources, including car wash revenue, ATM fees, lottery commissions, and rental income from co-tenants.

Goodwill and business value are treated separately from real estate value in most appraisals. The purchase price of a gas station often includes a goodwill component that reflects the value of the established customer base, fuel supply agreements, and brand affiliation. SBA lenders will finance goodwill, but conventional lenders generally will not. Understanding how the appraiser allocates value between real estate, equipment, inventory, and goodwill is important because it directly affects how much a lender is willing to lend on the property.
