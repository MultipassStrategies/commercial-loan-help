Cannabis Real Estate Loans: Financing for Dispensaries and Grow Facilities

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Typical loan amount: $500K to $15M
  • Max LTV: 50% to 65%
  • Interest rates: 9% to 14% (typically higher than conventional CRE)
  • Term length: 1 to 5 years (bridge); 5 to 10 years (stabilized)
  • Common lenders: Private lenders, cannabis-focused funds, credit unions
  • Down payment: 35% to 50% of property value

Why Cannabis Real Estate Financing Is Different

Cannabis is one of the fastest-growing industries in the United States, with legal sales surpassing $30 billion annually across more than 40 states that have legalized medical or recreational use. Yet financing the real estate that supports this industry remains one of the most challenging problems in commercial lending. The core issue is a conflict between state and federal law: while states continue to legalize and regulate cannabis, the federal government still classifies it as a Schedule I controlled substance under the Controlled Substances Act.

This federal classification creates a cascading set of problems for borrowers. Banks and other federally regulated financial institutions face the risk of prosecution for money laundering if they knowingly provide services to cannabis businesses. As a result, the vast majority of commercial real estate lenders simply will not touch cannabis-related properties. Borrowers who need to purchase, build out, or refinance dispensaries, cultivation facilities, processing centers, or distribution warehouses must look beyond conventional lending channels.

The gap between demand and available capital has given rise to a specialized lending ecosystem. Private lenders, cannabis-focused debt funds, a small number of state-chartered credit unions, and sale-leaseback investors have stepped in to fill the void. These lenders charge higher rates and require more equity than conventional commercial real estate loans, but they offer a critical lifeline to an industry that would otherwise have no access to real estate financing.

Federal vs. State Legality: The Root of the Lending Problem

Understanding why cannabis real estate financing is so difficult starts with the federal-state conflict. Cannabis has been classified as a Schedule I substance since the Controlled Substances Act of 1970, placing it in the same category as heroin and LSD. Schedule I status means the federal government considers cannabis to have no accepted medical use and a high potential for abuse.

In 2013, the Department of Justice issued the Cole Memorandum, which signaled that federal prosecutors would generally not pursue cannabis businesses operating in compliance with state law. This guidance gave the industry a measure of stability and encouraged more states to legalize. However, the Cole Memo was rescinded in 2018 under then-Attorney General Jeff Sessions, creating renewed uncertainty. While federal enforcement against state-legal cannabis operations has remained rare in practice, the legal risk has never been formally eliminated.

The SAFE Banking Act (Secure and Fair Enforcement Regulation Banking Act) has been introduced in Congress multiple times with the goal of protecting financial institutions that serve state-legal cannabis businesses. The bill has passed the U.S. House of Representatives on several occasions but has stalled in the Senate. As of early 2026, the SAFE Banking Act has not been signed into law, leaving the banking industry in limbo. Some legislators have pushed for broader cannabis reform that would include banking protections as part of a larger legalization or rescheduling package, but no final legislation has been enacted.

For commercial real estate lenders, the practical effect is straightforward: any institution whose deposits are insured by the FDIC or that is regulated by the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) faces real legal exposure if it finances cannabis properties. This eliminates most banks, savings institutions, and large insurance company lenders from the market.

Who Actually Lends for Cannabis Real Estate

The cannabis real estate lending market is dominated by three categories of capital providers, each with distinct structures, risk tolerances, and pricing models.

Private Cannabis Lenders and Debt Funds

Private lenders represent the largest source of cannabis real estate capital. These are typically structured as private debt funds that raise money from accredited investors, family offices, and institutional allocators willing to accept the legal risk in exchange for above-market returns. Many of these funds focus exclusively on cannabis, employing underwriters and legal teams with deep knowledge of state licensing regimes, facility requirements, and the unique risks of the sector.

Private cannabis lenders typically offer loan amounts from $500,000 to $15 million, with LTVs ranging from 50% to 65%. Interest rates generally fall between 10% and 14%, and terms are usually shorter than conventional commercial loans, often ranging from one to five years. Many of these loans are structured as bridge financing, with the expectation that federal legalization or rescheduling will eventually open the door to cheaper permanent financing.

Credit Unions

A small but growing number of state-chartered credit unions have developed cannabis banking and lending programs. Because credit unions are regulated at the state level (through the National Credit Union Administration for federal credit unions, or state regulators for state-chartered ones), some state-chartered credit unions in legal cannabis states have determined they can serve cannabis businesses with appropriate compliance controls in place.

Credit unions that lend to cannabis businesses typically require borrowers to maintain deposit accounts, provide detailed financial reporting, and demonstrate full compliance with state cannabis regulations. Rates from credit unions tend to be lower than private lenders, often in the 9% to 11% range, but the approval process is more rigorous and the total number of institutions willing to lend remains small.

Sale-Leaseback Investors

Sale-leaseback transactions have become one of the most important financing mechanisms in the cannabis industry. In a sale-leaseback, a cannabis operator that owns its real estate sells the property to an investor (often a real estate investment trust or private equity firm) and then leases it back under a long-term triple-net lease. The operator receives a lump sum of cash and converts its real estate equity into working capital, while the investor acquires a property with a built-in tenant and a steady income stream.

Sale-leasebacks are popular in cannabis for several reasons. First, they sidestep the banking restrictions that make traditional lending difficult, because the transaction is structured as a real estate purchase rather than a loan. Second, cannabis-zoned properties are scarce, which gives landlords pricing power and supports above-market rents. Third, many cannabis operators are capital-intensive businesses that benefit from freeing up equity for inventory, licensing fees, and expansion.

Hard Money Options for Cannabis Facilities

Hard money loans are a natural fit for the cannabis real estate market because they are asset-based, meaning the lender underwrites primarily on the value of the collateral rather than the borrower's creditworthiness or cash flow. For cannabis borrowers who may have limited operating history or whose financials are complicated by Section 280E tax limitations, hard money provides a path to capital that does not depend on traditional underwriting metrics.

Cannabis hard money loans typically come with higher interest rates (12% to 14%), lower LTVs (50% to 60%), and shorter terms (12 to 36 months). Origination fees of 2 to 4 points are standard. The primary advantage is speed: hard money lenders can close in two to four weeks, compared to 60 to 90 days for a credit union or structured debt fund. This makes hard money particularly useful for acquisitions where timing is critical, or for borrowers who need to close on a cannabis-zoned property before a competitor does.

The main risk for borrowers is the short term. Hard money loans must be refinanced or paid off within one to three years, and if federal policy has not changed by maturity, refinancing options may be limited to another high-cost private loan. Borrowers should have a clear exit strategy before taking on hard money debt.

Specialized Cannabis Real Estate Lenders: An Emerging Niche

Over the past several years, a new category of lender has emerged: firms that focus exclusively on cannabis real estate and related assets. These specialized lenders combine real estate underwriting expertise with deep knowledge of the cannabis regulatory landscape. They understand how to evaluate a cultivation facility's license value, how to assess the risk of license revocation, and how to structure loans that account for the unique operational requirements of cannabis properties.

Some of these specialized lenders have raised dedicated cannabis real estate funds with hundreds of millions of dollars in committed capital. Others operate as direct lenders backed by family office capital or high-net-worth investors. What sets them apart from general private lenders is their willingness to underwrite the cannabis operation itself, not just the real estate. This can result in higher proceeds and better terms for borrowers who have strong licenses, proven operating track records, and compliant operations.

Facility Types and Their Unique Financing Needs

Cannabis real estate spans several distinct property types, each with its own set of financing considerations. Lenders evaluate these facility types differently based on their capital requirements, regulatory complexity, and reuse potential if the cannabis operation were to fail.

Cultivation and Grow Facilities

Indoor cultivation facilities are among the most capital-intensive cannabis properties. They require specialized HVAC systems to control temperature, humidity, and airflow; high-capacity electrical systems to power grow lights; advanced water filtration and irrigation systems; and structural modifications to support the weight of equipment, soil, and water. Build-out costs for indoor cultivation can range from $150 to $400 per square foot, depending on the level of environmental control required.

Lenders evaluating cultivation facilities pay close attention to the property's electrical capacity, water rights, and mechanical systems. They also assess the facility's reuse potential: a well-built warehouse with upgraded electrical and HVAC may be repurposable for data centers, food processing, or other industrial uses if the cannabis operation fails. Properties with more specialized, single-purpose buildouts are considered higher risk.

Processing and Extraction Centers

Processing facilities, where raw cannabis is converted into concentrates, edibles, topicals, and other products, present unique underwriting challenges. These properties often contain volatile chemical extraction equipment (such as butane or CO2 extraction systems) that introduces fire and explosion risk. Lenders and insurers scrutinize the facility's safety systems, ventilation, chemical storage, and compliance with local fire codes.

Financing for processing facilities may require specialized insurance coverage and higher equity requirements. LTVs tend to be on the lower end of the range (50% to 55%) because the hazardous-use classification reduces the pool of potential future tenants and buyers.

Dispensary and Retail Locations

Dispensaries are the retail storefronts where cannabis is sold to consumers. From a real estate perspective, dispensaries are the most similar to conventional commercial properties. They are typically located in retail or mixed-use zones, require standard commercial buildouts (display cases, point-of-sale systems, security systems), and can often be converted to other retail uses with minimal modification.

Because of their higher reuse potential and lower buildout cost, dispensaries are generally easier to finance than cultivation or processing facilities. Lenders may offer higher LTVs (up to 65%) and slightly lower rates for well-located dispensary properties in markets with limited license availability. The value of the cannabis license itself, while not directly used as collateral in most real estate loans, significantly influences how lenders view the risk of the deal.

Distribution and Warehousing

Distribution facilities serve as logistics hubs where cannabis products are stored, tested, and transported to dispensaries. These properties are typically standard industrial warehouses with added security features (cameras, access control, alarm systems) required by state regulations. Because the physical modifications are relatively minor, distribution warehouses have strong reuse potential and are considered lower-risk collateral by most cannabis lenders.

Financing terms for distribution facilities tend to fall in the middle of the range, with LTVs of 55% to 65% and rates of 9% to 12%. Lenders favor distribution properties in states with well-established regulatory frameworks and large, active cannabis markets.

Zoning and Licensing: The Foundation of Cannabis Property Value

Zoning compliance is the single most important factor in cannabis real estate value. A property that is properly zoned for cannabis use in a market with limited license availability can command a substantial premium over comparable properties without cannabis zoning. Conversely, a property that loses its cannabis zoning or whose operator loses its license can see its value decline dramatically.

Most municipalities impose strict zoning restrictions on cannabis operations. Common requirements include minimum distances from schools (often 500 to 1,000 feet), parks, churches, daycare centers, and residential neighborhoods. Some cities restrict cannabis to specific industrial or commercial zones, and many impose caps on the total number of licenses available in a given area. These restrictions create artificial scarcity, which drives up property values for compliant locations.

Lenders take zoning very seriously. Before funding a cannabis real estate loan, lenders will verify that the property is properly zoned, that the borrower holds (or has applied for) the required state and local licenses, and that no pending zoning changes could jeopardize the property's cannabis use. Some lenders require borrowers to obtain a zoning confirmation letter from the local municipality as a condition of closing.

License transferability is another important consideration. In some states, cannabis licenses are tied to the property; in others, they are tied to the operator. When the license is property-specific, the real estate itself becomes more valuable as collateral because a new operator could potentially take over the license if the original tenant defaults. When the license is operator-specific, the lender's collateral value is more closely tied to the underlying real estate value without any cannabis premium.

Environmental Compliance Requirements

Cannabis facilities, particularly cultivation and processing operations, must meet a range of environmental compliance requirements that affect both construction costs and ongoing operating expenses. Lenders evaluate these requirements carefully because non-compliance can result in fines, license revocation, or forced shutdowns.

HVAC and climate control: Indoor cultivation facilities require industrial-grade HVAC systems to maintain precise temperature and humidity levels. These systems must also include air filtration to prevent the release of odors, pollen, and particulates. Many municipalities have adopted odor control ordinances that require carbon filtration or other mitigation technologies. The cost of HVAC systems for a mid-sized cultivation facility can range from $500,000 to $2 million.

Water usage: Cannabis cultivation is water-intensive. Facilities must comply with local water use regulations, which may include metering requirements, wastewater treatment mandates, and restrictions on groundwater extraction. In water-scarce regions like parts of California and Colorado, water availability can be a limiting factor for facility size and a key underwriting consideration for lenders.

Energy consumption: Indoor grow operations consume enormous amounts of electricity, primarily for lighting and climate control. Some municipalities have adopted energy efficiency requirements specifically for cannabis cultivation, mandating the use of LED lighting or setting maximum energy-use thresholds per square foot. Lenders consider energy costs as part of their cash flow analysis, since utility expenses can represent 20% to 30% of a cultivation facility's total operating costs.

Waste disposal: Cannabis facilities must comply with state-mandated waste disposal protocols for plant material, chemical solvents (in processing facilities), and packaging. Improper waste handling can trigger regulatory penalties and environmental liability, both of which concern lenders evaluating facility risk.

Exit Strategy Considerations for Lenders

Every real estate lender thinks about what happens if the borrower defaults. For cannabis real estate, this question is more complicated than for conventional properties because the property's value may be heavily dependent on its cannabis use authorization.

If a cannabis operator defaults on a loan and the lender forecloses, several scenarios can unfold. If the cannabis license is tied to the property and transferable, the lender (or a subsequent buyer at foreclosure) may be able to find a new cannabis operator to take over the space. This preserves the cannabis premium in the property's value and gives the lender the best recovery outcome.

If the license is tied to the operator and non-transferable, the lender is left with a property that may have limited value for non-cannabis uses, especially if the buildout is highly specialized. In this case, the lender's recovery depends on the property's value as a standard industrial, retail, or commercial space. This is why lenders place so much emphasis on reuse potential and why LTVs for highly specialized cannabis buildouts tend to be lower.

Lenders also consider the risk of regulatory change. If a state or municipality tightens its cannabis regulations, reduces the number of available licenses, or bans cannabis operations altogether, properties in that market could lose value rapidly. To mitigate this risk, most cannabis lenders focus on states with well-established, stable regulatory frameworks and strong market demand.

Another exit concern is federal enforcement risk. While the likelihood of federal action against state-legal cannabis operations has been low in recent years, lenders must still account for the possibility. Some loan documents include specific provisions addressing federal enforcement scenarios, and lenders may require borrowers to carry additional insurance or maintain reserve accounts as a buffer against regulatory disruption.

What Borrowers Need to Prepare

Cannabis real estate borrowers should expect a more demanding due diligence process than conventional commercial borrowers face. Lenders in this space will typically require the following documentation and conditions:

  • Valid state and local cannabis licenses: Lenders will verify that the borrower holds all required licenses and that those licenses are in good standing. Any pending regulatory actions, violations, or compliance issues can derail a loan application.
  • Detailed financial statements: Because cannabis businesses cannot deduct ordinary business expenses under IRC Section 280E, their tax returns may look very different from those of conventional businesses. Lenders familiar with the cannabis industry understand how to read 280E-adjusted financials, but borrowers should be prepared to provide detailed profit and loss statements, balance sheets, and cash flow projections alongside their tax returns.
  • Property appraisal: Lenders will require a third-party appraisal that accounts for both the property's value as cannabis-zoned real estate and its value for alternative uses. Some appraisers specialize in cannabis properties and can provide more accurate valuations than general commercial appraisers.
  • Environmental and compliance documentation: Evidence of compliance with local building codes, fire codes, environmental regulations, and cannabis-specific facility requirements. This may include inspection reports, HVAC specifications, security system documentation, and odor mitigation plans.
  • Business plan and operating history: Lenders want to see that the borrower has a viable business plan, experienced management, and (ideally) a track record of profitable cannabis operations. First-time operators may face higher rates or lower LTVs.

The Outlook for Cannabis Real Estate Lending

The cannabis real estate lending market is at an inflection point. Federal rescheduling of cannabis from Schedule I to Schedule III, which the DEA has signaled it may pursue, would not directly legalize cannabis banking but could reduce the legal risk for financial institutions and encourage more lenders to enter the market. Full federal legalization or passage of the SAFE Banking Act would be even more transformative, potentially opening the door to conventional bank lending, CMBS securitization, and agency financing for cannabis properties.

In the meantime, the market continues to mature. More private capital is flowing into cannabis real estate funds, underwriting standards are becoming more standardized, and the number of successful loan originations is building a track record that helps lenders calibrate risk. For borrowers, this means that while cannabis real estate financing remains more expensive and more complex than conventional commercial lending, the options are better today than they were even two years ago, and they are likely to continue improving.

Frequently Asked Questions

Traditional banks regulated by the FDIC generally will not lend on cannabis real estate. Because cannabis remains a Schedule I substance under federal law, banks that knowingly provide financing to cannabis operations risk violating federal anti-money laundering statutes. This has effectively shut most national and regional banks out of the cannabis lending market.

However, some state-chartered banks and credit unions in legalized states have developed cannabis banking programs with strict compliance frameworks. These institutions typically require borrowers to hold valid state licenses, maintain detailed financial records, and submit to enhanced monitoring and reporting. The number of financial institutions willing to work with cannabis businesses has grown, though availability remains limited compared to conventional commercial lending.

If the SAFE Banking Act is eventually signed into law, it would provide a legal safe harbor for banks and credit unions that serve state-legal cannabis businesses. This would likely bring a significant increase in lending competition and drive down borrowing costs for cannabis real estate. Until that happens, most borrowers will need to rely on private lenders, credit unions with cannabis programs, or sale-leaseback structures.

Interest rates for cannabis real estate loans typically range from 9% to 14%, which is significantly higher than conventional commercial real estate financing. For comparison, a standard commercial mortgage for a retail or industrial property might carry a rate of 6% to 8%, depending on market conditions. The premium on cannabis loans reflects the legal risk lenders assume, the limited pool of capital willing to enter the space, and the specialized underwriting these transactions require.

Within the cannabis lending market, rates vary based on lender type and borrower strength. Credit unions with cannabis banking programs may offer rates in the 9% to 11% range for well-qualified borrowers with established operations and strong licenses. Private debt funds and cannabis-focused lenders generally charge 10% to 14%, with rates on the higher end for bridge loans, construction financing, or borrowers with limited operating history. Hard money lenders, which provide the fastest but most expensive capital, typically charge 12% to 14% plus 2 to 4 points in origination fees.

Rates also vary by property type and market. Dispensary properties in high-demand markets with limited license availability tend to command better terms than cultivation or processing facilities, which are more capital-intensive and carry higher operational risk. Borrowers who can demonstrate strong cash flow, experienced management, and a clean regulatory record are in the best position to negotiate favorable pricing.

Zoning is arguably the most critical factor in cannabis real estate financing. Lenders will not fund a property unless it is properly zoned for its intended cannabis use, because a zoning violation could result in license revocation and a total loss of the property's cannabis-related value premium. Before approving a loan, lenders verify the property's zoning designation, check for any pending zoning changes, and may require a formal zoning confirmation letter from the local municipality.

Most municipalities impose strict distance requirements for cannabis operations. Properties must typically be located at least 500 to 1,000 feet from schools, parks, churches, daycare centers, and residential neighborhoods. Some cities limit cannabis to specific industrial or commercial zones, and many cap the total number of cannabis licenses in a given area. These restrictions create a limited supply of compliant properties, which drives up values for those that qualify.

From a lender's perspective, proper zoning is both a risk factor and a value driver. A cannabis-zoned property in a market with strict licensing caps can be worth significantly more than a comparable non-cannabis property, because the zoning itself enables a high-revenue use. However, if zoning regulations change or the property falls out of compliance, that premium can evaporate. This is why lenders underwrite cannabis properties with a dual valuation approach: one estimate based on cannabis use and another based on the property's value for alternative, non-cannabis purposes.

Federal illegality is the single largest barrier to cannabis real estate financing. Cannabis is classified as a Schedule I controlled substance under the Controlled Substances Act, which means any federally regulated financial institution risks prosecution for money laundering or aiding drug trafficking if it knowingly provides financing to cannabis operations. This removes the largest pools of commercial real estate capital (banks, insurance companies, CMBS lenders, and government-sponsored enterprises) from the market entirely.

Beyond lending restrictions, federal illegality affects cannabis borrowers through IRS Section 280E, which prohibits businesses engaged in trafficking of controlled substances from deducting ordinary business expenses. This dramatically reduces the reported profitability of cannabis operations, even those that are highly successful by industry standards. Lower reported income means weaker debt service coverage ratios, which makes it harder for borrowers to qualify for loans and may reduce the amount a lender is willing to advance.

Federal illegality also creates title and insurance complications. Some title companies are reluctant to insure properties used for cannabis operations, and the availability of property and liability insurance for cannabis facilities remains limited compared to conventional commercial properties. Lenders must navigate these gaps carefully, often requiring specialized insurance products and working with title companies that have specific cannabis experience. Until federal law changes, these barriers will continue to shape the cannabis real estate lending landscape and keep costs elevated for borrowers.

A sale-leaseback is a transaction in which a cannabis operator sells the real estate it owns to an investor or real estate investment company and then leases the property back under a long-term agreement, typically 10 to 20 years. The operator continues to occupy and use the property exactly as before but is now a tenant rather than an owner. In return, the operator receives a lump sum payment equal to the property's sale price, which can be redeployed into operations, inventory, licensing, or expansion.

Sale-leasebacks have become one of the most popular financing structures in the cannabis industry for several reasons. They sidestep many of the banking restrictions that make traditional lending difficult, because the transaction is structured as a property purchase rather than a loan. The investor acquires the property with a built-in tenant and a long-term lease, often at above-market rental rates that reflect the limited supply of cannabis-zoned locations. For the investor, the appeal is a stable, high-yielding real estate investment backed by a tenant with strong revenue.

The primary trade-off for the cannabis operator is the loss of property ownership and the obligation to make lease payments for the duration of the agreement. If the operator's business declines or the cannabis market contracts, the lease obligation remains. Operators should also negotiate lease terms carefully, paying attention to renewal options, rent escalation clauses, and what happens to tenant improvements if the lease is terminated. Despite these considerations, sale-leasebacks remain an essential tool for cannabis companies that need to access the equity in their real estate without navigating the traditional lending market.
