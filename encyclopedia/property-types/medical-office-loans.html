Medical Office Building Loans: Financing for Healthcare Real Estate

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Typical loan amount: $1M to $30M
  • Max LTV: 65% to 80% (up to 90% SBA)
  • Interest rates: 5.75% to 8.5%
  • Term length: 5 to 25 years
  • DSCR requirement: 1.20x to 1.35x
  • Common lenders: SBA lenders, banks, CMBS, life companies

Overview of Medical Office Financing

Medical office buildings (MOBs) occupy a favored position in commercial real estate lending. Lenders view healthcare real estate as one of the most resilient property types available, and for good reason. People need medical care regardless of economic conditions, which gives medical office buildings a level of recession resistance that few other property types can match. During the 2008 financial crisis and the 2020 pandemic, medical office vacancy rates remained well below those of traditional office buildings, reinforcing the sector's reputation for stability.

Several characteristics make MOBs particularly attractive to lenders. First, healthcare tenants tend to sign longer leases than general office tenants, often committing to 7 to 15 year terms because of the significant investment required to build out medical space. Second, medical tenants are inherently "sticky." Once a physician or group practice has invested hundreds of thousands of dollars in specialized buildout (exam rooms, procedure suites, imaging equipment, plumbing infrastructure), relocating becomes prohibitively expensive and operationally disruptive. This tenant stickiness translates directly into stable, predictable cash flow for building owners and, by extension, lower risk for lenders.

Third, the credit quality of healthcare tenants is often stronger than that of typical office tenants. Many MOBs are leased to hospital systems, regional health networks, or large group practices with strong balance sheets and investment-grade credit ratings. Even smaller medical tenants (solo practitioners, dental offices, specialty clinics) tend to have stable revenue streams because healthcare spending is driven by demographic need rather than discretionary consumer choices. These factors combine to make medical office buildings a preferred collateral type for many commercial lenders, which often results in more favorable terms for borrowers.

Owner-Occupied vs. Investment Medical Office

The financing landscape for medical office buildings splits into two distinct categories based on whether the borrower is a physician or medical group that will occupy the building, or an investor who will lease space to healthcare tenants. This distinction fundamentally shapes which loan programs are available, what terms the borrower can expect, and how the lender underwrites the deal.

Owner-occupied medical office buildings are properties where the borrower's medical practice will occupy at least 51% of the rentable space. This category opens the door to SBA loan programs, which offer the highest leverage and most favorable terms in the medical office lending market. Physicians, dentists, veterinarians, and other healthcare providers who are buying or building their own practice space can access SBA 7(a) and SBA 504 loans with as little as 10% down, fixed interest rates, and terms up to 25 years. Lenders underwrite these loans based on a combination of the practice's financial performance, the borrower's personal credit and net worth, and the property's appraised value. The key advantage is leverage: while investment property loans typically cap at 65% to 75% LTV, owner-occupied SBA loans can reach 85% to 90% LTV.

Investment medical office buildings are properties owned by investors who lease space to healthcare tenants but do not operate a medical practice themselves. These buildings are financed through conventional commercial mortgages, CMBS loans, or life company loans. Underwriting focuses on the property's income, tenant credit quality, lease terms, and market fundamentals rather than the borrower's personal practice revenue. LTV ratios are lower (typically 65% to 75%), DSCR requirements are stricter (1.25x to 1.35x), and the borrower usually needs more equity and a stronger track record in commercial real estate ownership. The trade-off is access to larger loan amounts, non-recourse structures, and loan products designed for long-term hold strategies.

SBA Loans for Medical Professionals

SBA loans are the single most important financing tool for medical professionals who want to own their practice real estate. The two primary programs, SBA 7(a) and SBA 504, each serve different needs but share a common advantage: they allow healthcare providers to purchase or build medical office space with significantly less equity than conventional commercial loans require.

The SBA 7(a) program offers loans up to $5 million for a wide range of business purposes, including real estate purchase, construction, equipment acquisition, working capital, and practice acquisitions. For medical office purchases, the SBA 7(a) provides up to 85% to 90% LTV with terms up to 25 years for real estate. Interest rates can be fixed or variable, typically ranging from prime plus 1.5% to prime plus 2.75% depending on loan size and term. The 7(a) program is particularly useful when the borrower needs to bundle multiple financing needs into a single loan, such as buying a building, purchasing equipment, and financing the practice acquisition all in one transaction.

The SBA 504 program is specifically designed for real estate and major equipment purchases. It uses a unique structure: a conventional bank loan covers approximately 50% of the project cost, a CDC (Certified Development Company) loan covers up to 40%, and the borrower contributes 10% as a down payment. The CDC portion carries a fixed interest rate for the full 10 or 20 year term, which provides payment certainty that is especially valuable for medical practices managing cash flow. Total project costs under the 504 program can reach $5.5 million for the CDC portion (with no cap on the total project cost), making it suitable for larger medical office acquisitions. The 504 program requires the borrower to occupy at least 51% of existing buildings or 60% of new construction.

Medical professionals are among the most successful SBA borrowers because lenders recognize the stability and earning power of healthcare practices. Physicians, dentists, optometrists, and veterinarians generally have strong personal credit, high income relative to loan size, and business models that generate consistent revenue. Many SBA lenders have dedicated healthcare lending divisions that understand the nuances of practice finance, including how to evaluate production-based revenue, insurance reimbursement patterns, and the impact of payer mix on cash flow stability.

Conventional Commercial Mortgages

For larger medical office buildings and investment-grade MOBs, conventional commercial mortgages from banks, CMBS lenders, and life insurance companies provide the financing. These products do not carry the size limitations or owner-occupancy requirements of SBA programs, making them the standard choice for multi-tenant medical office investments and institutional-quality healthcare real estate.

Bank loans for medical office buildings typically range from $1 million to $15 million, with terms of 5 to 10 years and amortization schedules of 20 to 25 years. Banks offer both fixed and variable rate options, and the relationship nature of bank lending can result in favorable pricing for borrowers with strong banking histories. Most bank loans are full recourse, meaning the borrower is personally liable for the debt, though some banks will offer limited recourse on larger, well-stabilized deals. Banks are a good fit for mid-sized MOBs, borrowers who value flexibility in loan terms, and deals that benefit from a local lender who understands the healthcare market in the area.

CMBS (Commercial Mortgage-Backed Securities) loans serve the $3 million to $30 million+ segment of the medical office market. CMBS lenders pool loans into securities that are sold to bond investors, which creates a standardized product with fixed rates, 5 to 10 year terms, and non-recourse structures. CMBS is best suited for stabilized, multi-tenant MOBs with strong occupancy and creditworthy tenants. The non-recourse feature is a significant advantage for investors who want to limit their personal exposure. The trade-off is rigidity: CMBS loans have strict prepayment provisions (typically defeasance or yield maintenance), limited flexibility for property modifications during the loan term, and a standardized underwriting process that may not accommodate unusual deal structures.

Life insurance company loans represent the top tier of medical office financing. Life companies provide long-term, fixed-rate, non-recourse loans for the highest-quality MOBs, typically in the $5 million to $50 million range. Terms of 10 to 25 years with full or partial amortization are common, and rates are among the lowest available because life companies have a low cost of capital and a long-term investment horizon. Life companies are highly selective, favoring newer buildings in strong markets with investment-grade anchor tenants and long weighted average lease terms. A medical office building leased to a major hospital system on a 15-year NNN lease is exactly the type of asset that life company lenders compete to finance.

Tenant Credit Analysis

The creditworthiness of a medical office building's tenants is one of the most critical factors in loan underwriting. Unlike multifamily properties where income is diversified across dozens or hundreds of residential tenants, medical office buildings typically have a smaller number of larger tenants, and the financial strength of each one directly impacts the property's risk profile.

Hospital systems and large health networks are the gold standard for medical office tenants. These organizations often carry investment-grade credit ratings, operate across multiple facilities, and have strong revenue streams from diversified payer mixes. A lease with a major hospital system is viewed by lenders as nearly equivalent to a bond, particularly if the lease is structured as a triple-net (NNN) lease where the tenant is responsible for taxes, insurance, and maintenance. Buildings anchored by hospital system tenants typically qualify for the lowest rates, highest leverage, and longest terms available in the market.

Group practices fall in the middle of the credit spectrum. A well-established, multi-physician group practice with a diversified patient base and strong revenue history is considered a solid tenant, though not as creditworthy as a hospital system. Lenders evaluate group practices based on their financial statements, the number of physicians in the group (more physicians means less key-person risk), the practice's payer mix, and the length of time the practice has been operating. Groups that are affiliated with or employed by a hospital system receive credit treatment closer to the hospital system itself.

Solo practitioners and small practices present the highest tenant risk in medical office lending. The revenue of a solo practitioner depends entirely on one person's ability to work, which introduces key-person risk that does not exist with larger organizations. If a solo practitioner retires, becomes disabled, or relocates, the landlord faces a vacant suite that may require significant re-tenanting costs. Lenders adjust for this risk by reducing the credit weight given to solo practitioner leases, requiring higher DSCR ratios, or limiting the percentage of building income that can come from solo tenants.

Lease structure is equally important. Lenders prefer longer lease terms (7+ years remaining), NNN or modified gross lease structures that pass operating cost increases to tenants, and staggered lease expirations that avoid concentrated rollover risk. A building where all leases expire in the same year is far riskier than one with expirations spread across multiple years, because the owner faces the possibility of sudden, large-scale vacancy.

Specialized Buildout Considerations

Medical office space requires significantly more buildout investment than standard office space, and lenders factor this reality into their underwriting. A typical general office buildout might cost $40 to $60 per square foot, while medical office buildout routinely costs $80 to $200+ per square foot depending on the specialty. Surgical suites, imaging centers, and dental operatories can exceed $300 per square foot when equipment is included.

The specialized infrastructure required for medical use includes additional plumbing (exam rooms, procedure rooms, and labs all require sinks and, in some cases, specialized waste drainage), upgraded electrical systems (to support medical equipment, backup generators, and redundant power), enhanced HVAC (infection control requirements, operating room air handling, and specialized ventilation for certain procedures), and structural reinforcements (for heavy imaging equipment such as MRI and CT scanners that can weigh thousands of pounds). ADA compliance is particularly rigorous for healthcare facilities, requiring wider corridors, accessible exam rooms, and compliant restroom facilities throughout the building.

Lenders treat medical buildout costs differently depending on the loan type and borrower. For owner-occupied SBA loans, buildout costs are typically included in the total project budget and financed as part of the loan. For investment property loans, the landlord's TI (tenant improvement) allowance is a key underwriting factor. Lenders want to understand how much the owner will need to spend to attract and retain medical tenants, because high TI obligations can erode the property's effective return. Buildings that are already built out for medical use carry a premium because the next tenant can occupy the space with minimal additional investment, reducing the landlord's re-tenanting cost and the lender's risk.

One important nuance: while medical buildout increases the value of the space for healthcare tenants, it can reduce the building's value for alternative uses. If the medical tenants vacate, converting specialized medical space back to general office use requires additional investment to remove the medical infrastructure. This "dark value" consideration is something appraisers and lenders evaluate when determining property value, especially for single-tenant medical buildings where the entire building is configured for one occupant's specific needs.

Practice Acquisition Financing

Many medical office building loans are part of a larger transaction that includes the acquisition of an existing medical practice. When a physician buys a practice that comes with real estate, the financing must cover both the tangible asset (the building) and the intangible assets (the practice's goodwill, patient records, equipment, and workforce). This dual nature creates complexity that requires lenders experienced in healthcare transactions.

Practice goodwill, which represents the value of the practice's patient relationships, reputation, referral networks, and assembled workforce, can constitute 50% or more of the total purchase price. Lenders who finance practice acquisitions must have a methodology for evaluating goodwill, which typically involves analyzing the practice's trailing revenue and earnings, comparing the purchase price to industry valuation benchmarks (often expressed as a multiple of collections or EBITDA), and assessing the likelihood that patients and revenue will transfer successfully to the new owner.

SBA loans are the dominant financing vehicle for practice acquisitions that include real estate. The SBA 7(a) program specifically allows financing for goodwill and intangible assets, which most conventional commercial lenders will not do. A physician buying a dental practice with a building, for example, can use a single SBA 7(a) loan to finance the real estate, the practice goodwill, the equipment, and the working capital needed during the ownership transition. The SBA requires that the borrower inject at least 10% equity into the transaction and that the total acquisition price is supported by a business valuation from a qualified appraiser.

For transactions where the real estate value exceeds the SBA lending limits or where the buyer prefers to separate the real estate financing from the practice financing, a common approach is to use an SBA 504 loan for the building and a separate SBA 7(a) loan or conventional practice loan for the goodwill and working capital components. This two-loan structure can optimize rate and terms for each component of the transaction while staying within SBA program limits.

Demographic Tailwinds for Medical Office Demand

The long-term demand outlook for medical office space is supported by several powerful demographic and industry trends that lenders evaluate when underwriting MOB loans. These tailwinds contribute to the sector's favorable financing terms and strong investor interest.

An aging population is the single most significant driver of medical office demand. Approximately 10,000 Americans turn 65 every day, and the 65+ age group consumes healthcare services at roughly three times the rate of younger adults. As the baby boomer generation moves through its 70s and 80s over the next two decades, demand for outpatient medical services (primary care, specialty care, diagnostics, rehabilitation, and minor procedures) will increase substantially. This is not speculative; it is a demographic certainty that lenders can underwrite against with confidence.

The migration from inpatient to outpatient care is reshaping where healthcare is delivered. Advances in surgical techniques, anesthesia, and medical technology have made it possible to perform procedures in outpatient medical offices that once required hospital stays. Insurance companies and Medicare actively incentivize outpatient settings because they are less expensive than hospital-based care. This shift directly increases demand for medical office space while reducing the relative importance of hospital inpatient capacity. For medical office building investors and lenders, this trend means growing tenant demand and increasing competition among healthcare providers for well-located outpatient space.

Hospital system off-campus expansion is another powerful driver. Major hospital systems are aggressively expanding their outpatient footprints by leasing space in medical office buildings located in suburban and exurban markets where patient populations are growing. Rather than building new hospitals (which cost hundreds of millions of dollars and take years to complete), hospital systems are establishing satellite clinics, urgent care centers, and outpatient surgery centers in medical office buildings closer to where patients live. This strategy creates demand for MOB space from the most creditworthy tenants in healthcare, further strengthening the lending case for medical office properties.

Telehealth's impact has been more nuanced than initially feared. While the pandemic accelerated telehealth adoption, the long-term effect on medical office demand has been modest. Most medical services require in-person examination, testing, or treatment, and telehealth has largely supplemented rather than replaced office visits. Many healthcare providers now use a hybrid model where initial consultations or follow-up visits may occur via telehealth, but the core clinical work still happens in the medical office. Lenders have largely moved past early pandemic concerns about telehealth disruption and continue to view MOBs as a stable, growing property type.

Both SBA 7(a) and SBA 504 loans are available for medical office building purchases, and they are among the most popular financing tools for physicians, dentists, and other healthcare providers buying their own practice space. The SBA 7(a) program allows loans up to $5 million for real estate acquisition, equipment, working capital, and practice purchases. The SBA 504 program pairs a conventional bank loan (covering about 50% of the project cost) with a CDC loan (covering up to 40%) and a borrower down payment of just 10%, making it one of the lowest-down-payment options available for owner-occupied commercial real estate.

To qualify, the borrower must occupy at least 51% of the building for the SBA 7(a) program or 51% for existing buildings (60% for new construction) under the 504 program. Medical professionals frequently qualify because they are using the building for their own practice. The SBA also considers the overall financial health of the practice, the borrower's credit history, and the ability to service the debt from practice revenues. Most SBA lenders want to see a minimum credit score of 680 or higher, and they will review two to three years of personal and business tax returns.

One important advantage of SBA financing for medical office purchases is the ability to include multiple cost components in a single loan. A physician buying a building can finance the real estate purchase, necessary renovations, equipment, and even working capital in a single SBA 7(a) transaction. This simplifies the closing process and reduces the need for multiple loan applications with different lenders. Many SBA-preferred lenders have healthcare lending specialists who understand the revenue models and cash flow patterns specific to medical practices, which streamlines the underwriting process.

Owner-occupied medical office financing offers several distinct advantages over investment property loans. First, lenders view owner-occupants as lower risk because the borrower has a direct operational stake in the property, which reduces the likelihood of default. This translates into higher LTV ratios (up to 90% with SBA programs versus 65% to 75% for investment properties), lower interest rates, and more flexible qualification criteria. Second, owner-occupants can access SBA loan programs that are not available to pure investors, including the SBA 7(a) and 504 programs, which offer long terms, low down payments, and fixed rates.

Third, owning the building allows the medical practice to build equity over time instead of paying rent to a landlord, and the practice gains stability by controlling its own occupancy costs. The monthly mortgage payment often compares favorably to market rent, especially after factoring in equity buildup and tax benefits such as depreciation, mortgage interest deductions, and potential Section 179 deductions for building improvements. Many physicians find that their monthly cost of ownership is comparable to or even lower than what they were paying in rent, with the added benefit of asset appreciation.

There is also a strategic advantage to ownership. Medical practices that own their real estate are insulated from rent increases, lease non-renewals, and landlord-driven relocations. For a practice that has invested heavily in specialized buildout, the risk of being forced to relocate at lease expiration is a real concern. Ownership eliminates that risk entirely. Additionally, when the physician eventually retires or sells the practice, the real estate becomes a separate asset that can be sold independently, leased to the new practice owner, or retained as an investment property generating passive rental income.

Lenders evaluate the tenant mix in a medical office building by examining the creditworthiness of each tenant, the structure and duration of their leases, and the diversity of the tenant base. The strongest tenant profiles are large hospital systems, health networks, and publicly traded healthcare companies, because these organizations have investment-grade credit ratings and are unlikely to default on their leases. Solo practitioners and small group practices are considered higher risk, since the loss of a single physician (through retirement, relocation, or practice failure) can eliminate an entire income stream.

Lenders typically analyze the weighted average lease term (WALT) across all tenants, with longer WALTs indicating more stable cash flow. They also assess rollover risk by examining when leases expire and whether multiple tenants have lease expirations in the same year. A building where 40% of leases expire within 12 months presents significantly more risk than one where no more than 15% of leases roll in any given year. The presence of a creditworthy anchor tenant occupying 30% or more of the building typically strengthens the loan application considerably, because the anchor tenant provides a baseline of stable income that supports debt service even if smaller tenants turn over.

Beyond individual tenant credit, lenders also look at the complementary nature of the tenant mix. A building with a mix of primary care, specialty care, diagnostics, and ancillary services (pharmacy, physical therapy, lab) creates a referral ecosystem that benefits all tenants and increases their incentive to remain in the building. This synergy is a qualitative factor that experienced healthcare real estate lenders understand and value. Buildings located adjacent to or on the campus of a hospital enjoy an additional advantage, because hospital proximity drives patient traffic and makes the location more desirable for medical tenants.

Medical office buildout and renovation can be financed through several channels depending on whether the borrower is the building owner or a tenant. For building owners, construction-to-permanent loans allow the borrower to finance the buildout and then convert to a permanent mortgage once the work is complete. SBA 504 loans can include renovation costs in the total project budget, and many banks will structure a single loan covering both the acquisition and the buildout. The SBA 7(a) program is also flexible enough to include renovation funding in the loan amount, provided the total stays within the $5 million program limit.

For tenants leasing space in a medical office building, the landlord typically provides a tenant improvement (TI) allowance that covers a portion of the buildout cost. Medical office TI allowances tend to be higher than standard office TI allowances because of the specialized infrastructure required: additional plumbing for exam rooms, reinforced flooring for imaging equipment, lead-lined walls for radiology suites, and upgraded HVAC systems for infection control. TI allowances for medical space commonly range from $50 to $100+ per square foot, though the exact amount depends on market conditions, lease term, and the tenant's creditworthiness. Landlords typically recover TI costs through higher base rent over the lease term.

Equipment financing is a separate category that can cover medical equipment installed during the buildout, keeping the real estate loan focused on the building itself. Equipment loans and leases are available from specialized healthcare equipment lenders, and they are secured by the equipment rather than the real estate. Some lenders offer bundled practice financing packages that coordinate the real estate loan, equipment financing, and working capital into a single closing process, which reduces complexity for the borrower and ensures that all financing components are in place before construction begins.

Lenders determine the value of a medical office building primarily through the income approach, which capitalizes the property's Net Operating Income (NOI) using a market-derived capitalization rate. The appraiser will also consider the sales comparison approach (analyzing recent sales of comparable medical office buildings in the area) and the cost approach (estimating the replacement cost of the building minus depreciation). For medical office buildings, the income approach typically carries the most weight because investors and lenders focus on cash flow as the primary indicator of value.

Cap rates for medical office buildings vary by market, tenant quality, building age, and location, but they generally range from 5.5% to 8.5%. Buildings with investment-grade anchor tenants and long lease terms trade at lower cap rates (higher values), while buildings with short-term leases, small tenants, or deferred maintenance trade at higher cap rates (lower values). The quality of the lease structure, including whether leases are NNN, gross, or modified gross, directly affects the NOI calculation and, therefore, the property's appraised value.

Lenders may also apply a "dark value" analysis, which estimates what the building would be worth if the current medical tenants vacated and the space had to be re-leased or converted to general office use. This is especially relevant for single-tenant medical buildings where the entire income stream depends on one occupant. If the dark value is significantly lower than the leased value, the lender may underwrite to a blended figure or require additional reserves to mitigate the concentration risk. Multi-tenant buildings with diversified tenant rosters and staggered lease expirations are less susceptible to dark value discounting because the risk of total vacancy is much lower.
