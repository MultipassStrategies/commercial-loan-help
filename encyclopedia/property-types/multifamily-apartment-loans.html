Multifamily and Apartment Loans: Every Financing Option Explained

Commercial Loan Help Editorial Team March 17, 2026
Key Facts
  • Definition: 5+ residential units
  • Top options: Agency, CMBS, HUD, bank, bridge
  • LTV range: 65% to 80%
  • Minimum units: 5 (commercial threshold)
  • Strongest programs: Fannie DUS, Freddie Mac, HUD 223(f)
  • DSCR requirement: 1.20x to 1.25x typical

What Counts as Multifamily Commercial?

Properties with five or more residential units cross the threshold from residential to commercial lending. This is not a gray area. One to four units means a residential mortgage, governed by residential lending rules and underwritten primarily on the borrower's personal income and credit. Five or more units means commercial, with entirely different underwriting standards, different lenders, and different loan structures. The dividing line applies even if the building looks like a house. A converted Victorian with five rental apartments is a commercial property in the eyes of every lender.

This distinction matters because commercial multifamily lending opens the door to loan programs that simply do not exist on the residential side. Non-recourse financing (where the lender's remedy in a default is limited to the property itself, not the borrower's personal assets) is standard in commercial multifamily but virtually nonexistent in residential lending. Loan terms can extend to 30 or even 35 years with full amortization. And underwriting focuses on the property's income rather than the borrower's W-2, which means experienced investors can scale their portfolios far beyond what personal income qualification would allow.

The flip side is that commercial multifamily loans require more documentation, more sophisticated analysis, and a deeper understanding of the capital markets. Borrowers need to produce detailed operating statements, rent rolls, and property condition reports. Lenders will scrutinize vacancy rates, expense ratios, and rent comparables in ways that residential lenders never do. The reward for navigating this complexity is access to better leverage, better rates, and loan structures specifically designed for income-producing real estate.

Agency Lending: Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac

Agency lending is the backbone of multifamily finance in the United States. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the two government-sponsored enterprises (GSEs), purchase and securitize multifamily loans through their respective programs, providing liquidity that keeps rates low and terms favorable for borrowers. For stabilized multifamily properties, agency loans are often the best available option.

The Fannie Mae DUS (Delegated Underwriting and Servicing) program is the flagship agency product. DUS lenders are approved to underwrite, close, and service loans on Fannie Mae's behalf without prior approval on each deal, which speeds up the process considerably. Key features include non-recourse lending, fixed and floating rate options, terms from 5 to 30 years, supplemental loan availability, and interest-only periods on qualifying deals. Loan amounts start at roughly $1 million with no hard cap, though most DUS production falls in the $3 million to $100 million range. Fannie Mae requires stabilized properties with a minimum occupancy of 90% for at least 90 days prior to closing.

Freddie Mac's Optigo platform offers a similar suite of products. The standard Freddie Mac multifamily loan provides non-recourse, fixed-rate financing for stabilized properties with competitive pricing that often rivals Fannie Mae. Freddie Mac also operates the Small Balance Loan (SBL) program, specifically designed for loans between $1 million and $7.5 million. The SBL program features streamlined underwriting, faster closings, and rate pricing that is competitive with larger deals, making it one of the best options for borrowers in the small multifamily space.

Both agencies benefit from implicit government backing, which allows them to borrow at rates close to U.S. Treasury yields. This funding advantage flows directly to borrowers in the form of lower interest rates. Agency multifamily rates are typically 50 to 150 basis points below comparable CMBS or bank rates, depending on market conditions. The combination of low rates, non-recourse terms, long amortization, and standardized processes makes agency lending the first stop for most multifamily borrowers with stabilized assets.

HUD/FHA Multifamily Programs

HUD multifamily programs offer the lowest rates and highest leverage in the permanent multifamily lending market. The trade-off is time and complexity, but for borrowers with the patience to navigate the process, the long-term economics are unmatched.

The HUD 223(f) program is designed for the acquisition or refinance of existing multifamily properties. It provides fully amortizing, non-recourse loans with terms up to 35 years and LTV ratios up to 85% for market-rate properties (87% for affordable housing). Interest rates are the lowest available in multifamily finance, typically running 50 to 100+ basis points below agency rates. The loan is fully amortizing over its full term, meaning there is no balloon payment and no refinancing risk. For a borrower who plans to hold a property for 10, 20, or 30+ years, the total interest savings over the life of a HUD loan can be enormous.

The HUD 221(d)(4) program serves new construction and substantial rehabilitation projects. It offers even longer terms (up to 40 years plus the construction period) and similar non-recourse, fully amortizing structures. The 221(d)(4) program is the gold standard for ground-up multifamily development, providing construction and permanent financing in a single loan that eliminates the need for a separate construction-to-permanent refinance.

The drawbacks of HUD financing are real. Closing timelines run 6 to 12+ months, and in some cases longer. The documentation requirements are extensive, including HUD-specific appraisals, environmental reviews, architectural reviews for construction deals, and market studies. Construction projects under HUD are subject to Davis-Bacon prevailing wage requirements, which increase labor costs. After closing, borrowers face ongoing compliance obligations including annual financial audits, reserve deposits, and restrictions on distributions. These requirements make HUD loans best suited for experienced operators with long-term hold strategies who can absorb the upfront time and cost in exchange for decades of below-market debt service.

CMBS Multifamily Loans

CMBS (Commercial Mortgage-Backed Securities) loans are another non-recourse option for stabilized multifamily properties. In a CMBS transaction, the lender originates the loan and then pools it with other commercial mortgages into a bond that is sold to investors in the capital markets. This securitization model means the lender is not constrained by its own balance sheet, which allows for standardized terms and broad availability.

Typical CMBS multifamily loans feature fixed rates, 5 or 10-year terms, 25 to 30-year amortization schedules, and LTV ratios of 65% to 75%. Non-recourse is standard, with the usual carve-outs for fraud and misrepresentation (sometimes called "bad boy" guarantees). CMBS lenders will finance properties that may not fit agency guidelines, including those with slightly lower occupancy or mixed-use components, making CMBS a useful alternative when agency lending is not available.

The primary disadvantages of CMBS relate to inflexibility and prepayment. Once a CMBS loan is securitized, changes to loan terms require approval from a special servicer, and even minor modifications can be difficult and slow. Prepayment is governed by defeasance or yield maintenance provisions that can be extremely expensive, especially in the early years of the loan. Borrowers who anticipate needing to sell, refinance, or modify their loan before maturity should carefully evaluate the prepayment costs before committing to CMBS. For stable, long-term holds where the borrower does not expect to need flexibility, CMBS provides competitive rates and reliable non-recourse terms.

Bank and Credit Union Loans

Local and regional banks remain a significant source of multifamily financing, particularly for smaller properties and borrowers with established banking relationships. Bank loans are portfolio products, meaning the bank holds them on its own balance sheet rather than selling them into the capital markets. This gives banks flexibility to structure loans in ways that agency and CMBS lenders cannot.

The advantages of bank lending include relationship-driven underwriting (a strong banking history can compensate for a deal that is slightly outside standard guidelines), flexible loan structuring (banks can offer interest-only periods, stepped rates, or customized covenants), and the ability to close quickly. Banks can also lend on properties that do not meet the stabilization requirements of agency or CMBS programs, such as recently acquired properties with vacancy or properties in lease-up.

The disadvantages are equally clear. Bank multifamily loans are typically recourse, meaning the borrower personally guarantees the debt. Terms are shorter, usually 5 to 7 years with a balloon payment, creating refinancing risk at maturity. Rates may be higher than agency alternatives, and leverage is often limited to 65% to 75% LTV. Banks also face regulatory constraints on their commercial real estate concentrations, which can limit their appetite for multifamily lending during certain market conditions.

Bank loans work best for smaller deals (under $3 million) where the transaction size does not justify the cost of agency or CMBS execution, for borrowers who have strong existing relationships with a bank, or for properties that need a flexible lender willing to work outside standard guidelines. Credit unions operate similarly to banks in this space and can be competitive on rate and terms for qualifying deals.

Bridge Loans and Debt Funds

Bridge loans fill the gap for multifamily properties that are not yet ready for permanent financing. These are short-term loans (typically 1 to 3 years, with extension options) designed for transitional situations: value-add acquisitions, properties in lease-up, buildings undergoing renovation, or assets being repositioned to a higher use or tenant profile.

Bridge lenders, which include debt funds, private lenders, and specialized bridge lending platforms, underwrite primarily to the property's projected stabilized value rather than its current condition. This allows borrowers to access capital based on what the property will be worth after improvements, not just what it is worth today. Many bridge loans include future funding provisions that release renovation dollars as work is completed, functioning similarly to a construction draw schedule.

Interest rates on bridge loans are significantly higher than permanent financing, typically ranging from 7% to 12% or more depending on the risk profile, leverage level, and market conditions. Most bridge loans are interest-only during their term, which reduces the monthly carrying cost during the renovation period. Prepayment terms are generally more flexible than permanent loans, with many bridge lenders offering open prepayment or minimal exit fees after a short lockout period.

The typical bridge loan strategy is straightforward: acquire or refinance a transitional property, execute the business plan (renovations, lease-up, management improvements), stabilize the asset, and then refinance into a permanent agency or CMBS loan at a lower rate. This strategy is sometimes called the BRRRR approach (Buy, Rehab, Rent, Refinance, Repeat) adapted for commercial multifamily. Bridge lenders evaluate the borrower's track record, the reasonableness of the business plan, and the achievability of the projected stabilized value when making lending decisions.

How Lenders Underwrite Multifamily

Multifamily underwriting centers on the property's ability to generate sufficient income to service the debt. Lenders evaluate three core metrics, and the loan amount is ultimately constrained by whichever metric produces the lowest result.

Net Operating Income (NOI) is the foundation of every multifamily underwriting analysis. NOI equals gross potential rent (all units at market rent, fully occupied) minus vacancy loss minus operating expenses. Lenders typically use trailing 12-month (T-12) actual financials as the starting point, then apply their own adjustments. Common adjustments include normalizing management fees to market rate (usually 4% to 6% of effective gross income), applying a minimum vacancy factor (even if the property is 100% occupied, lenders will underwrite to at least 5% vacancy), and reserving for capital expenditures (replacements reserves, typically $250 to $350 per unit per year).

Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) is the ratio of NOI to annual debt service (principal plus interest). Most multifamily lenders require a minimum DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x, meaning the property must generate 20% to 25% more income than is needed to cover the loan payments. A higher DSCR provides a cushion against income fluctuations and gives the lender confidence that the property can weather periods of higher vacancy or lower rents without defaulting on the loan.

Loan-to-Value (LTV) measures the loan amount as a percentage of the appraised value. Maximum LTV varies by program (see the comparison table above), but even when the program allows higher leverage, the loan will be sized down if the DSCR constraint binds first. In practice, many multifamily loans are DSCR-constrained rather than LTV-constrained, particularly in lower-cap-rate markets where property values are high relative to income.

Beyond these core metrics, lenders also examine rent comparables (to verify that the property's rents are achievable and sustainable), expense ratios (to flag properties with unusually high or low operating costs), the borrower's track record and net worth, and the local market's supply and demand dynamics. Agency lenders use standardized underwriting models that create consistency and predictability, while bank and bridge lenders may apply more subjective judgment based on their experience with the market and property type.

Property Classes: A, B, C, and D

Multifamily properties are informally categorized into classes that reflect their age, condition, location, amenities, and tenant profile. These classifications are not standardized by any regulatory body, but they are universally used by lenders, brokers, and investors to quickly communicate a property's position in the market. Property class directly affects which financing options are available and at what terms.

Class A properties are the newest, highest-quality buildings in the best locations. They feature modern construction, premium finishes, full amenity packages (pools, fitness centers, concierge services), and command the highest rents in their market. Class A assets attract the most favorable financing terms from every lender type. Agency, CMBS, and bank lenders compete aggressively for Class A deals, resulting in the lowest rates and highest leverage available.

Class B properties are older but well-maintained buildings in good locations. They may lack the newest finishes or full amenity packages, but they are functional, clean, and competitive in their submarket. Class B is the sweet spot for many multifamily investors because these properties offer a balance of stable cash flow, manageable capital expenditure needs, and potential for value-add improvements. Financing is readily available from agency, CMBS, and bank sources, though rates and terms may be slightly less aggressive than Class A.

Class C properties are older buildings with functional but dated interiors, often serving workforce housing tenants. They typically have minimal amenities and may have some deferred maintenance. Class C assets can be excellent investments, but financing becomes more selective. Some agency and CMBS lenders will finance well-maintained Class C properties, while others may require the borrower to pursue bank or bridge financing, especially if the property needs capital improvements. Many value-add strategies target Class C properties for renovation and repositioning into Class B.

Class D properties are distressed assets with significant deferred maintenance, code violations, high vacancy, or other serious operational issues. Conventional and agency financing is generally not available for Class D properties. Borrowers pursuing Class D acquisitions typically rely on hard money, specialized bridge lenders, or debt funds that are comfortable with the elevated risk. The business plan for Class D almost always involves substantial renovation and repositioning before any permanent financing becomes possible.

Value-Add vs. Stabilized Multifamily Financing

The distinction between stabilized and value-add multifamily is the single most important factor in determining which loan products are available. Lenders draw a clear line between properties that are performing at or near their potential and properties that need work to reach that potential.

A stabilized property is one that is at least 90% occupied (some lenders require this occupancy level for 90+ consecutive days), generating rents at or near market rates, in good physical condition with no major deferred maintenance, and operating with a consistent financial track record (typically demonstrated through 12 months of trailing financials). Stabilized properties qualify for the full range of permanent financing options: Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, HUD, CMBS, and bank loans.

A value-add property falls short of stabilization in one or more areas. Common value-add characteristics include below-market rents (often due to poor management or lack of capital investment), elevated vacancy (either physical vacancy or economic vacancy from concessions), deferred maintenance or outdated unit interiors, and operational inefficiencies. These properties do not qualify for permanent agency or CMBS financing in their current state.

The financing strategy for value-add multifamily follows a two-step process. First, the borrower secures a bridge loan to acquire the property and fund renovations. The bridge loan is sized based on the property's projected stabilized value and the total cost of the business plan. During the bridge loan term (typically 2 to 3 years), the borrower executes the renovation, raises rents to market levels, reduces vacancy, and improves operations. Once the property meets stabilization benchmarks, the borrower refinances out of the bridge loan and into permanent agency or CMBS financing at a significantly lower interest rate. The spread between bridge rates (7% to 12%+) and permanent rates (5% to 6.5%) is the economic engine that drives this strategy. Successful execution requires accurate budgeting, realistic rent projections, and disciplined timeline management.

Affordable Housing and LIHTC Considerations

Affordable and workforce housing multifamily properties occupy a specialized corner of the financing market with unique programs, incentives, and compliance requirements. The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC) program, administered by state housing finance agencies under Internal Revenue Code Section 42, is the primary federal incentive for affordable housing development and preservation. LIHTC properties receive tax credit allocations in exchange for committing to income and rent restrictions for a compliance period (typically 15 to 30 years).

Financing for LIHTC properties involves a combination of tax credit equity (sold to investors who use the credits to offset their tax liability), soft debt from state and local housing agencies, and senior debt from lenders experienced in affordable housing. The senior debt component can come from banks, CDFIs (Community Development Financial Institutions), state housing finance agencies, or the agencies themselves. Both Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac have dedicated affordable housing programs with favorable terms, including lower DSCR requirements, higher LTV allowances, and reduced pricing for properties that serve low- and moderate-income tenants.

HUD programs are a natural fit for affordable multifamily. The 223(f) and 221(d)(4) programs frequently finance affordable housing projects, and HUD's regulatory framework aligns well with the compliance requirements of LIHTC and other affordable housing programs. Borrowers pursuing affordable multifamily financing should work with lenders and syndicators who specialize in this space, as the intersection of tax credit compliance, regulatory agreements, and conventional underwriting creates complexity that generalist lenders are often not equipped to handle.

Small Multifamily: 5 to 20 Units

Small multifamily properties (roughly 5 to 20 units) present unique financing challenges. Many institutional lenders have minimum loan amounts that effectively exclude smaller properties, and the fixed costs of agency or CMBS execution (appraisals, legal fees, environmental reports, third-party inspections) consume a larger percentage of the loan amount on smaller deals, making the economics less attractive for both the lender and the borrower.

The best financing options for small multifamily include the Freddie Mac SBL (Small Balance Loan) program, which was specifically created for this market segment. SBL offers loans from $1 million to $7.5 million with non-recourse terms, competitive rates, and streamlined underwriting. For borrowers who qualify, Freddie Mac SBL is often the best available option for small multifamily because it provides agency-level pricing and terms that would otherwise be reserved for larger deals.

Local and regional banks are another strong option for small multifamily. Banks can offer flexibility on loan structure and may be willing to lend at amounts below what agency programs will consider. The relationship component of bank lending is especially valuable in the small multifamily space, where the borrower's track record and banking history can help overcome the property's smaller scale. Credit unions may also be competitive for small multifamily loans in their service areas.

SBA loans may work for small multifamily if the borrower occupies one of the units. The SBA 7(a) program, for example, can finance a property where the owner lives in one unit and rents out the others, as long as the business (property management and rental) meets SBA eligibility criteria. This is a niche application, but it can provide favorable terms for owner-occupant borrowers who are just entering the multifamily space.

Getting a fair appraisal can be challenging for small multifamily. Comparable sales may be limited in some markets, and appraisers must determine whether the income approach, sales comparison approach, or a blend of both best reflects the property's value. Borrowers should work with appraisers experienced in small multifamily and be prepared to provide detailed income and expense documentation to support the valuation.

Once a property has five or more units, it crosses into commercial territory, which means different lenders, different underwriting criteria, and different loan structures. This applies regardless of the building's physical appearance. A converted single-family home with five separate rental units is considered commercial, while a large four-unit building is still residential.

The five-unit threshold matters because commercial multifamily loans offer benefits not available in residential lending, including non-recourse options, longer amortization periods, and underwriting based primarily on property income rather than the borrower's personal income. On the residential side, lenders evaluate the borrower's debt-to-income ratio, credit score, and personal financial history. On the commercial side, lenders focus on the property's Net Operating Income, Debt Service Coverage Ratio, and market fundamentals.

For borrowers who currently own four-unit properties and are looking to scale, crossing the five-unit threshold opens up an entirely different lending ecosystem with greater flexibility, higher leverage potential, and loan structures designed for professional real estate investment.

The HUD 223(f) program (for acquisition and refinance of existing properties) and the HUD 221(d)(4) program (for new construction and substantial rehabilitation) both provide rates that are typically 50 to 100+ basis points below comparable agency or CMBS loans. This rate advantage translates into significant savings over the life of the loan, particularly given the long terms (35 years for 223(f), 40 years for 221(d)(4)).

The trade-off is processing time and complexity. HUD loans take 6 to 12+ months to close (compared to 30 to 60 days for agency loans), require extensive documentation including a full HUD-specific appraisal and environmental review, and impose ongoing regulatory requirements including annual financial reporting and reserve deposits. Construction projects under HUD 221(d)(4) are also subject to Davis-Bacon prevailing wage requirements, which increase labor costs.

For borrowers who plan to hold a property for the long term and can tolerate the longer timeline, HUD financing produces the lowest monthly payments and best long-term economics in the multifamily market. The key is matching your investment strategy to the loan product. If you need speed, agency is the better choice. If you are optimizing for the lowest possible cost of capital over a multi-decade hold, HUD is the clear winner.

Maximum LTV varies by loan program. Agency loans (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac) typically offer up to 75% to 80% LTV for stabilized multifamily, with some programs allowing up to 80% for certain property types or borrower profiles. HUD 223(f) allows up to 85% LTV for market-rate properties and up to 87% LTV for affordable housing projects, which is the highest leverage available in the permanent multifamily market. CMBS loans typically cap at 65% to 75% LTV. Bank loans range from 65% to 75% depending on the lender's risk appetite.

Bridge loans for transitional properties are typically sized based on loan-to-cost (LTC) rather than LTV, with most bridge lenders offering 70% to 80% LTC. This means the lender will finance up to 70% to 80% of the total project cost (purchase price plus renovation budget), which can translate to a much higher percentage of the as-is value.

In all cases, the actual leverage you receive will also be constrained by DSCR requirements. Even if a program allows 80% LTV, the loan will be sized down if the property's income does not support a 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR at that leverage level. In lower-cap-rate markets, DSCR constraints often bind before LTV limits, resulting in effective leverage that is lower than the program maximum. The best approach is to work with a lender or broker who can model both constraints and tell you exactly how much leverage your specific property will support.

Multifamily underwriting is generally considered more straightforward and favorable than other commercial real estate property types because of the diversified income stream. With dozens or hundreds of individual tenants, the loss of any single tenant has a minimal impact on overall income, which reduces risk compared to office or retail properties that may depend on a few large tenants. This diversification is a key reason why multifamily consistently receives the most favorable lending terms of any commercial property type.

Lenders focus on several key metrics: Net Operating Income (NOI) based on trailing 12-month actual financials, the Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR, typically requiring 1.20x to 1.25x), vacancy rates compared to the local market, rent levels compared to comparable properties, and operating expense ratios. Lenders also examine the physical condition of the property (through appraisals and property condition assessments), the local rental market supply and demand dynamics, and the borrower's experience managing multifamily properties.

Agency lenders in particular use standardized underwriting criteria that create predictability for borrowers who are familiar with the process. Once you understand how Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac underwrites a deal, you can reliably predict the loan amount, rate, and terms before you even submit an application. This predictability is a major advantage over other property types, where underwriting can be more subjective and lender-specific. For multifamily, the numbers tell the story, and experienced borrowers use this transparency to structure acquisitions with confidence.
