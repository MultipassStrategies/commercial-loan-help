Why Standard Refinancing May Not Work the Way It Used To

Many of the commercial loans maturing in 2026 were originated between 2019 and 2022 — a period defined by near-zero interest rates, peak property valuations, and aggressive lending standards. Sponsors capitalized at the top, and many saw their invested capital double or even triple in just a few years. The problem: the market those loans were built on no longer exists.

Here's the mathematical dilemma many borrowers face today: property values have compressed, meaning your LTV is higher than when you originated. Interest rates have roughly doubled, meaning your debt service is larger. And in many cases, lenders are requiring borrowers to bring cash to closing just to meet current underwriting standards — a "cash-in refinancing" scenario that simply wasn't on the table when these deals were structured.

The good news: this is not uncharted territory. Bridge loans, mezzanine debt, preferred equity, and private credit are specifically designed for moments like this — when traditional bank refinancing falls short. The key is understanding which structure fits your deal, and getting matched to the right capital source before your maturity date arrives.

Your 5 Refinancing Options in 2026 — Explained in Plain English

Each definition below is a standalone explanation of a commercial real estate refinancing option available in 2026.

1

Traditional Refinance

A traditional refinance replaces your existing loan with a new one — typically from a bank, credit union, or agency lender (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac). This is the most straightforward option if your property's value has held up, your DSCR meets current lender requirements (typically 1.20x–1.50x), and your LTV is within acceptable limits. However, in 2026, many borrowers are finding that the math no longer works for a traditional refi due to higher rates and compressed values. If that's your situation, the options below are designed for exactly this moment.

2

Bridge Loan

A bridge loan is short-term financing (typically 12–36 months) that "bridges" the gap between your current situation and a future permanent financing event. Bridge loans are designed for borrowers who need time — time to stabilize a property, complete renovations, lease up vacant space, or simply wait for better market conditions to refinance into permanent debt. They are typically interest-only, faster to close than traditional loans, and available from private lenders, debt funds, and specialty bridge lenders. If your property doesn't qualify for traditional refinancing today but will in 12–24 months, a bridge loan may be the right move.

3

Mezzanine Debt

Mezzanine debt is a layer of financing that sits between your senior loan (first mortgage) and your equity. It's used to fill the gap when your senior lender won't provide enough proceeds to cover your refinancing needs. For example, if your property is worth $10 million and your senior lender will only go to 65% LTV ($6.5M), but you need $8M total, a mezzanine lender can provide the additional $1.5M. Mezzanine debt is secured by a pledge of your ownership interest in the property (not the property itself), which means it can be layered on top of most senior loans. The trade-off: it carries a higher interest rate than senior debt (typically 10–15%), but it avoids the need to bring in new equity partners or inject personal capital.

4

Preferred Equity

Preferred equity is capital that sits above your common equity but below your debt in the capital stack. Unlike mezzanine debt, preferred equity is technically an equity investment — not a loan — which means it doesn't add to your debt service obligations and doesn't trigger intercreditor issues with your senior lender. Preferred equity investors receive a priority return (typically 12–18%) before common equity holders receive any distributions. This structure is particularly useful in 2026 for borrowers who are over-leveraged but don't want to sell, recapitalize with new partners, or inject personal funds. The downside: preferred equity is the most expensive layer of capital, and it dilutes your ownership returns until it's paid off.

5

Private Credit

Private credit refers to non-bank lending from institutional capital sources — including private debt funds, insurance companies, family offices, and alternative asset managers. With over $1.5 trillion+ in private credit AUM globally, this sector has become one of the largest and fastest-growing sources of commercial real estate capital. Private credit lenders can often move faster, offer more flexible terms, and underwrite deals that traditional banks won't touch — including transitional assets, lower DSCRs, and higher leverage. In the current environment, private credit is filling the gap left by banks that have pulled back from commercial real estate lending. If your deal doesn't fit neatly into a bank's box, private credit may be your best path forward.

Most conventional commercial lenders require a minimum DSCR of 1.20x to 1.50x, depending on the property type and loan program. In 2026, many borrowers are finding that compressed property values and higher interest rates have pushed their DSCR below these thresholds — sometimes well below 1.0x.

If your DSCR falls short of traditional lender requirements, you still have options: bridge loans, mezzanine debt, and preferred equity can be structured to fill the gap. Some private credit providers will also consider deals with lower DSCRs if the borrower can demonstrate a clear path to stabilization. The key is to start the process early so you have time to explore all available structures before your maturity date.

Negative leverage occurs when the cost of debt (your interest rate) exceeds the property's return (its cap rate or cash-on-cash yield). In plain terms: you're paying more to borrow money than the property is generating in return.

For example, if your property has a 5.5% cap rate but your loan carries a 7% interest rate, every dollar you borrow is actually reducing your overall return — that's negative leverage. This dynamic has become widespread in 2026 due to the combination of cap rate expansion (property values declining) and interest rates that have roughly doubled since many loans were originated.

Negative leverage doesn't necessarily mean a deal is bad — it may still make sense to refinance if you believe the property will stabilize or appreciate — but it does change the math significantly. Understanding whether you're in a negative leverage position is critical to choosing the right refinancing structure.
